For eight hundred and fifty years, these arches carried water to Rome. Now they carry the sunset.

Stand in the open meadow and look along the great line of brick and stone arches marching across the grass towards the city. This is the par ko del yee ak weh dot tee, two hundred and forty hectares of the old Roman countryside, the kam pan ya, that somehow survived inside the modern city.

Several aqueducts cross the park, Roman and papal. The tallest arches you see belong to the ak wa klow dee ah, begun under ka lig yoo la in thirty eight ay dee and finished by klaw dee us in fifty two, with a second channel, the an ee oh no vus, running along the top. Lower down, there's the ak wa feh lee cheh, built by Pope six tus the Fifth between fifteen eighty five and fifteen ninety, and still used to water the fields today. To build it, his engineers cut straight through part of the older ak wa mar sha.

From the first of these aqueducts, the water flowed into Rome for some eight and a half centuries, until the Goths cut them in the year five thirty seven.

Look out, too, for the ruins of the villa del leh veen yah cheh, a Roman villa with its own great cistern, and for the ka sah leh dee ro ma vek ya, a farmhouse from the twelve hundreds that gave this whole area its old name, Old Rome.

What's remarkable is that any of it is here at all. In the nineteen seventies the area was full of shacks and building plots. In nineteen eighty six a group of local people formed a committee to save it, and by nineteen eighty eight they'd got it included in the ap pya an tee ka regional park.

If you can, stay until the evening. Walk along the klow dee ah as the sun goes down behind the arches, with the joggers and the dog walkers, and you'll see why the people who live round here fought so hard to keep it.
