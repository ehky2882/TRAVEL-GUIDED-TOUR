Parco degli Acquedotti, Rome

For eight hundred and fifty years, these arches carried water to Rome. Now they carry the sunset.

Stand in the open meadow and look along the great line of brick and stone arches marching across the grass towards the city. This is the Parco degli Acquedotti, two hundred and forty hectares of the old Roman countryside, the Campagna, that somehow survived inside the modern city.

Several aqueducts cross the park, Roman and papal. The tallest arches you see belong to the Aqua Claudia, begun under Caligula in thirty-eight AD and finished by Claudius in fifty-two, with a second channel, the Anio Novus, running along the top. Lower down, there's the Aqua Felice, built by Pope Sixtus the Fifth between fifteen eighty-five and fifteen ninety, and still used to water the fields today. To build it, his engineers cut straight through part of the older Aqua Marcia.

From the first of these aqueducts, the water flowed into Rome for some eight and a half centuries, until the Goths cut them in the year five thirty-seven.

Look out, too, for the ruins of the Villa delle Vignacce, a Roman villa with its own great cistern, and for the Casale di Roma Vecchia, a farmhouse from the twelve hundreds that gave this whole area its old name, Old Rome.

What's remarkable is that any of it is here at all. In the nineteen seventies the area was full of shacks and building plots. In nineteen eighty-six a group of local people formed a committee to save it, and by nineteen eighty-eight they'd got it included in the Appia Antica regional park.

Film-makers love it. Fellini used it for La Dolce Vita, and Sorrentino came back for La Grande Bellezza.

If you can, stay until the evening. Walk along the Claudia as the sun goes down behind the arches, with the joggers and the dog walkers, and you'll see why the people who live round here fought so hard to keep it.
