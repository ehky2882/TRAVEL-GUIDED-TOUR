Bosco di San Francesco, Assisi

Step through a door in a wall beside one of the most visited churches in Europe, and within minutes you're alone in a wood.

On the square of the Upper Basilica of Saint Francis, look for the door set into the wall. That's the way in. The path drops away from the basilica, down through woodland and olive terraces towards the valley below Assisi.

These sixty-four hectares were once neglected and abandoned. In two thousand and eight they were bought, with a gift from the bank Intesa Sanpaolo, and the work of bringing them back began. The walk down is about a kilometre and a half, on natural paths, with steps in places.

Halfway down you'll come to the old Benedictine complex of Santa Croce, founded by nuns in the twelve or thirteen hundreds: a small Romanesque church, a fourteenth-century tower, the remains of a hospital for pilgrims and the sick, and the old mill, which is now a little osteria. Next to it is the Ponte dei Galli, an old bridge that has survived as well.

[beat]

And then, at the bottom, in a clearing, comes the surprise. The artist Michelangelo Pistoletto has planted a hundred and twenty-one olive trees in double rows to make three huge, touching circles. He calls it the Third Paradise: the first paradise was nature, the second is the world humans have built, and the third is the one where the two might finally get along.

It's a rather fitting thing to find below Saint Francis, the saint who preached to the birds and wrote the Canticle of the Creatures. The basilica up the hill is about his life. This wood is about his idea.

Before you start the walk back up, stop by the circles and look up at Assisi on its hill above you. It's a view of the town most visitors never see, because most visitors never come down this far.
