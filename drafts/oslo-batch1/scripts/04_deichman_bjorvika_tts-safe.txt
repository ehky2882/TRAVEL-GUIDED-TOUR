Dykeman Byurveeka

Somewhere above your head, in a quiet room near the top of this building, sit manuscripts by writers including Margaret Atwood, Hahn Kahng and Karl Ooveh Knowsgord, and nobody will be allowed to read them until twenty one fourteen.

The open square between Oslo Central Station and the water gives you the best view, with the library's pale, glassy bulk ahead of you and the white slope of the Opera House just beyond, so look up at the upper corner, where a whole floor reaches out almost twenty metres over the square with nothing at all beneath it.

That overhang is the building's party trick, held up from above rather than propped from below, which is why it looks so improbable. Dykeman Byurveeka opened in June twenty twenty, designed by Atelyay Oslo and Loond hahgem, and a year later it was named Public Library of the Year by the international federation of library associations.

Walk round the outside and you'll notice there isn't one grand front door but three equal entrances facing different ways, each opening into a diagonal shaft of light that cuts up through the floors towards big skylights in the roof.

If you ask me, that's the whole philosophy in concrete and glass, because a library that has been open to all citizens since Carl Dykeman's bequest of books founded it in seventeen eighty five has built itself a home with no back door and no front one either, where everyone comes in on equal terms.

Head up to the top floor and look for the Silent Room, where those Future Library manuscripts are kept, and remember that a forest of a thousand spruce trees, planted in Noormarka in twenty fourteen, is quietly growing to become the paper they'll finally be printed on.
