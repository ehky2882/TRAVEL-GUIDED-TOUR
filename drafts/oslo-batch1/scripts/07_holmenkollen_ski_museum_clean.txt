Holmenkollen Ski Museum

Four thousand years of skiing, from Stone Age rock carvings to the kit that went with Nansen and Amundsen to the polar ice, are tucked into the hillside right in front of you.

Tip your head back and follow the steel ramp of the Holmenkollen jump all the way up into the sky, then bring your eyes back down to the foot of its tower, where the Ski Museum is built into the hill, with the landing slope dropping steeply away towards the arena far below.

Opened in 1923, it's the oldest ski museum in the world, built on the private collection of the architect Hjalmar Welhaven and first housed further up the hill at Frognerseteren, before moving here in time for the 1952 Winter Olympics and later burrowing deeper into the rock, with a new foyer by Snøhetta added for its centenary.

Inside, look out for the equipment from the polar expeditions, much of it given to the museum by Nansen and Amundsen themselves, because it's one thing to read about those journeys and quite another to stand in front of the gear that made them.

The jump has an even longer story, since the first competition here was held in January 1892, and the hill has been rebuilt something like nineteen times since, most recently in 2010 as a steel structure designed by Julien De Smedt, with its own built-in wind screen.

It's worth pausing on that pairing, since the museum holds skiing as Norway once lived it, a practical way of getting about in deep snow, while the jump above it turns the same pair of planks into pure spectacle, and both, in their way, are portraits of the country.

Make time to go up to the top of the jump tower, which is part of the museum visit, because from up there Oslo and the fjord lie spread out far below you, and if your nerve holds, there's a ski-jump simulator waiting inside as well.
