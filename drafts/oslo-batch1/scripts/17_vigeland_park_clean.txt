The Vigeland Park

A hundred and twenty-one human figures climb towards the sky on the column in front of you, all of them carved from a single block of granite, and it took four stone carvers some thirteen years to free them.

The granite column before you, on the highest point of the park, is the Monolith, seventeen metres tall and raised on a broad stepped platform where groups of stone figures gather around its base, so stand back far enough to see the very top, where the smallest figures, the children, are clustered.

The stone came from Iddefjord, in the far south-east of Norway, and arrived here in the late 1920s, and although Gustav Vigeland designed it, the carving itself was done by those four craftsmen, working directly into the granite, while Vigeland once described the column as his religion.

Everything around you is his, too, because in 1921 Vigeland reached an agreement with the city of Oslo that shaped the rest of his working life, and the result is more than two hundred sculptures in bronze and granite, laid out along a single line that runs from the main gates, over a hundred-metre bridge lined with fifty-eight bronze figures, past the fountain and up the steps to here.

I always think the park makes most sense read as one long story about being human, from childhood to old age, through love, quarrels and grief, and the Monolith is where that story stops being ordinary life and becomes something closer to longing, with people pressing upwards and nobody quite sure what waits at the top.

From here, walk back down the axis towards the bridge and look for Sinnataggen, the furious little boy stamping his foot, whose hand has been rubbed shiny by visitors hoping for a bit of luck, and since the park is free and open all year, it rewards a second visit on a quieter morning.
