Oslo City Hall

Every year on the tenth of December, the Nobel Peace Prize is presented inside this building, in a great hall that, for the rest of the year, belongs to civic receptions and to anyone who wanders in.

In the courtyard on the city side of the Rådhus, facing Fridtjof Nansens plass, the two square brick towers rise ahead of you, and along the walls around you runs a series of carved wooden reliefs, painted and touched with gold.

Those reliefs are the Yggdrasil frieze by Dagfin Werenskiold, telling stories from Norse myth around the great world tree, including the Norns watering it from their well, and they're cut from pine, soaked in linseed oil, then painted and gilded so they can stand up to the Oslo weather.

The towers look like twins but aren't, since the eastern one is sixty-six metres tall and the western one sixty-three, and the eastern tower also holds a carillon of forty-nine bells, while on this north side you can look out, too, for the building's astronomical clock.

Arnstein Arneberg and Magnus Poulsson won the design competition in 1918, the foundation stone went down in 1931, and after the war interrupted everything, the building finally opened on the fifteenth of May 1950, for the city's nine-hundredth anniversary, on ground where the crowded old neighbourhood of Pipervika had been cleared away.

Here's a thought to carry inside: because it took more than thirty years to finish, this building ended up telling the story of a city before and after occupation, and the murals in the main hall, by Henrik Sørensen and Alf Rolfsen, trace that story in paint, from everyday working life to the war years.
