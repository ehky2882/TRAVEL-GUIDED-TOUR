MUNCH

Look at the top of the building before you and you'll see it tip forward, as though the upper floors were leaning in for a better look at the Opera House and the old city across the water.

From the open quayside at the museum's foot, step back far enough to take in all thirteen storeys, wrapped in panels of recycled, perforated aluminium that shift from silvery to almost see-through depending on the light and the angle you catch them from.

This is MUNCH, designed by the Spanish studio of Juan Herreros, which won the competition in 2009, and opened by King Harald in October 2021, standing just over fifty-seven metres tall, and opinion in Oslo has been divided about it, with some admiring its boldness and others finding it severe.

What it protects is the gift of a lifetime, because in 1940 Edvard Munch left his own works to the city, close to twelve hundred paintings, around seven thousand drawings and more than eighteen thousand prints, the largest collection of his work anywhere.

The way The Scream is shown is rather touching, since three versions take turns on the fourth floor, so that one is always on view while the other two rest in darkness, protecting their fragile pigments, paper and card from the light.

The thing to hold onto is this: a museum whose most famous treasures must be kept out of the light has wrapped itself in a skin built for filtering light, which seems to me a fitting tribute to a painter who spent his life chasing moods of light and shadow.

[beat]

Once inside, head for the fourth floor and see which Scream is having its turn when you arrive, then carry on up to the higher floors, where the big windows look straight back over Bjørvika and the fjord.
