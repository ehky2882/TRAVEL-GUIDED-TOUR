## Script (clean)

Karura Forest

The city's real infrastructure, according to the architects who sent you here, isn't a road or a railway. It's this.

Karura Forest lies on Nairobi's northern edge, a few kilometres from the city centre. You're most likely at a gate off Kiambu Road or Limuru Road, where the tarmac gives way to red-earth trails and the noise of traffic drops away behind a wall of trees. If you can hear birds over the cars, you're in.

It's big — just over a thousand hectares, with more than fifty kilometres of trails — and it's one of the largest forests inside any city in the world. It was gazetted as a forest reserve in nineteen thirty-two. Inside, there's a waterfall on the Karura River, a bamboo thicket, marshland, and something you won't find in most city parks: the Mau Mau caves.

Have a look for those. The cave_bureau guide makes the point beautifully. Nairobi's forests — Karura to the north, Ngong and Oloolua to the south-west — were, in their words, "infrastructures of freedom". In the nineteen fifties, Mau Mau fighters used caves like these to hide, regroup and move unseen, right under the noses of the colonial city. They'd argue you can't read Nairobi at all without its forests and its caves.

Now the second fight this forest has seen. In the late nineteen nineties, huge parts of Karura were quietly handed to private developers for housing. Wangari Maathai and the Green Belt Movement led the resistance. There were protests in the forest itself, and Maathai was attacked and beaten by guards in nineteen ninety-nine. The forest was saved. She went on to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

[beat]

So twice in one century, these trees have stood in the way of somebody taking Nairobi from Nairobians. That's the infrastructure cave_bureau mean.

If you've a couple of hours, take the trail down to the Karura River — it drops quite steeply — and follow it to the waterfall and the caves. It's an easy walk, but wear proper shoes. In the rainy season, the water runs orange.
