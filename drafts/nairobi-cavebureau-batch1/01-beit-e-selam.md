## Script (clean)

Beit é Selam

Before we get to the food, the tree.

Maua Close, Westlands — a quiet turning off Parklands Road, a few hundred metres from The Alchemist. Look for a white wall and an arched, ornate metal gate, the sort of entrance you'd expect on a courtyard house in Lamu rather than a side street lined with office blocks. Through it, a terracotta-tiled patio runs the length of the plot under a long wooden pergola. And over all of it, throwing the shade, a very large fig.

That's a mugumo. In Kikuyu tradition it's a sacred tree — a shrine, a place to pray for rain, said to be where Ngai, God, rests when he comes down from heaven. Cutting one down is taboo; when a road or a church really must take one, elders are called in first to perform a rite. The architects of cave_bureau, whose Nairobi guide brought you here, count this as one of the finest specimens in the city. It's a strangler fig, by the way. It starts life as a seed lodged in another tree, sends its roots down to the ground, and slowly encloses its host. Rather a lot of patience for a tree.

Now the house. Beit é Selam means "house of peace" in Amharic, and it's a small converted home, filled with books, paintings and objects from right across the continent, with antique birdcages and chandeliers hanging above the bar. The owner is a musician, which explains the DJ booth by the patio.

The menu is split into Ndogo, Kati and Kubwa — Swahili for small, middle and big — and it wanders the whole of Africa: corn ribs dusted with mitmita, an Ethiopian chilli blend; kitfo tartare; and mandazi with chai for pudding. Menus change, so treat that as a flavour rather than a promise. And one reviewer in the Business Daily called the gents here "the Oscars of washrooms". You have been told.

[beat]

Here's the thing, though. In a neighbourhood that rebuilds itself every few years, the oldest thing on this plot is the guest everyone's been seated round.

If the mugumo has got under your skin, there's a more famous one not far from here, on Waiyaki Way. In twenty twenty it stood in the path of the new Nairobi Expressway — and after a public campaign the president issued a decree to protect it, and the road went round. It's a short ride away, and well worth the detour.
