Nairobi National Park

There aren't many places on earth where you can watch a rhino graze with a skyline behind it.

You're inside Nairobi National Park, on the southern side of the city — open grassland, scattered acacia, the odd stand of forest. Wherever your vehicle has stopped, turn and look north. On a clear day, the towers of the city rise straight out of the horizon. The city centre is only about seven kilometres away. Giraffe in the foreground, office blocks behind. That's the picture.

The cave_bureau guide puts it neatly: here you can "observe pre-nineteen hundreds Nairobi" — the rolling grasslands, forest and wildlife that were here before the railway and the city, kept in a hundred and seventeen square kilometres. It was the first national park in Kenya, opened in nineteen forty-six, after the conservationist Mervyn Cowie campaigned for it. Before that, this was a game reserve, where cattle grazed, rubbish was dumped, and the Royal Air Force even practised bombing. And its creation came at a cost: Maasai herders were moved off the land.

Now have a look at what's living here. Lion, leopard, buffalo, cheetah, hippo, zebra and wildebeest. And above all, black rhino. This is one of Kenya's most successful rhino sanctuaries, and one of the few places where you can be fairly sure of seeing one in the wild.

Here's something else worth knowing. The park is fenced on three sides — the north, east and west, the city sides. The south, along the Mbagathi River, is left open, so the animals can still wander out onto the plains and back again with the seasons.

[beat]

So the city has a wall on one side and an open door on the other. Nairobi hasn't swallowed this place, not yet. It has simply agreed to live next door.

Before you leave, head towards the main gate and find the Ivory Burning Site Monument. In nineteen eighty-nine, President Moi set fire to twelve tonnes of ivory here, to tell the world Kenya would no longer trade in it. It's a quiet spot now — and one of the most important in the whole park.
