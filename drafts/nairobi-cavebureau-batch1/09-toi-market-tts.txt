Toy Market

Somewhere in this market, there is almost certainly a coat that started life in another country.

Toy Market sits off Ngong Road, on the edge of Kibbehra — one of the largest informal settlements in Africa. It's an open air market of narrow lanes, thousands of stalls, and heaps of clothes, shoes and bags spread out on tarpaulins and hung from timber frames overhead. If you're surrounded by second hand jeans and somebody is calling out a price, you're in the right place.

The word you'll hear everywhere is meetoomba. It's Swahili for "bales", because that's how the clothes arrive: compressed into tight bundles, shipped from abroad, and opened here. Second hand clothing first came to Kenya as charity in the seventies and eighties; when trade was liberalised, it became a business. Today the largest share of Kenya's meetoomba comes from China, followed by the United States and Pakistan. Toy's traders buy much of their stock through Geekomba, the giant market in Eastlands, and some have deals to get first look when a fresh bale is cut open. That moment — when the bale opens — is where the bargains are.

The cave bureau guide is unusually frank about it: "For better and for worse, get a taste of Nairobi's ballooning second hand clothes market." Both halves of that are true. For better: thousands of livelihoods, and good clothes at prices people can afford. For worse: a trade often blamed for squeezing Kenya's own textile makers, and a market that has burned again and again. Toy has suffered serious fires in most years since twenty fourteen; one in twenty twenty three affected more than two thousand five hundred traders. Each time, they rebuild.

And that's what to notice here. Nothing at Toy was planned by an architect. The lanes, the frames, the shade — it's all improvised, rebuilt by hand after every fire. It's exactly the kind of city cave bureau keep pointing at: the one Nairobi makes for itself.

Before you leave, find a stall where they're opening a fresh bale and watch for a minute. It's the quickest education in the global clothing trade you'll ever get — and you might walk away with something rather good.
