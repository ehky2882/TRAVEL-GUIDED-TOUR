You have crossed into a neighborhood that was studied before it was named. In 1896 a young scholar named W. E. B. Du Bois took rooms a few blocks west of here and spent a year walking these streets with a notebook, and the book he made of it, published in 1899, took its title from the ward: it was the Seventh Ward, running from Spruce Street to South and from Seventh Street to the Schuylkill, and it held, by most counts, the largest free Black community in the North for much of the century before he arrived. The church in front of you was its anchor before the ward had a number.

Mother Bethel's standalone stop tells how the congregation came to this corner in 1794 and what Richard Allen and Absalom Jones did in the fever year. This walk needs only the frame. Allen bought this lot himself, in his own name, and the church has stood on it through four buildings, the present one finished in 1890 in brownstone and granite with the tower you are looking at. Around it, through the nineteenth century, grew the schools, the benevolent societies, the newspapers, the lodges, and the businesses that Du Bois counted house by house.

*[beat]*

The ward got a map before it lost its lines: Du Bois colored every house in it by the condition of the people inside, and within a few decades the ward itself was abolished as a political unit and taken off the city's maps. The map is what survives of it in libraries. The church is what survives of it on the ground: Lombard Street around it has been rebuilt twice, the blocks to the east were cleared in the nineteen sixties, and the congregation is not a remnant of the ward so much as the reason the ward gathered here. If a service is in progress, stay outside. Then turn east on Lombard.
