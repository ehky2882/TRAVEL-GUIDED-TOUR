Here the city stops. Front Street was the first street, the one the others were counted from, and for a century and a half the river was a few steps beyond it, down a bank to the wharves. In the nineteen sixties the bank was dug out for Interstate Ninety-Five, and the river has been on the far side of a six-lane trench ever since, reached by a handful of crossings. Walnut Street's footbridge is gone, taken down for the park that will eventually be built over the highway on a deck due at the end of the decade; until then Dock Street is the way across from here. Take it. It is noisy and it is not pretty, and until the deck is built it is exactly how the city gets to its first river.

On the other side, bear left along the water to the museum basin, where a white cruiser with two tall masts lies against the pier. That is the Olympia, a cruiser launched in 1892 and still afloat, and this walk uses her only as a landmark: stand near her bow and look at the river.

*[beat]*

It is wide, brown, and busy, with the bridge upstream and the working shore of Camden across, and nothing about it is composed. That is the thing to take from a walk that started in a garden square. This city has two rivers, and it has treated them differently from the beginning: the city kept one river for work and one for looking at, and this is the working one. The ships, the piers, the highway, the trench you just walked over, and the deck that will someday cover it are all the same decision carried forward, which is that the Delaware was for getting things done. The walk ends here, on the pier, with Front Street and the grid behind you and the water in front, and the one street that still gets you from one to the other.
