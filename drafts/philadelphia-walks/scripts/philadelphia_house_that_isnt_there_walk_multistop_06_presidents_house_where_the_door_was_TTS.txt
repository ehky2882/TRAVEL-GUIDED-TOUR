philadelphia — walk 5: the house that isn't there — segment 03: president's house — where the door was
Walk 5 · segment 03 · register AMERICAN
Vantage: the open-air site at Sixth and Market, at the north end of the Liberty Bell Center block; open daily, no ticket
---
There was a door here. It opened onto Market Street a little east of the corner, and for ten years, from seventeen ninety to eighteen hundred, it was the front door of the house the president of the United States lived in. Look at the low brick walls and the open steel frames in front of you. They are not a building. They are the outline of one, set out at full size on the place it stood: the front wall, the doorways, the room with the bow window at the back, the kitchen, the quarters behind the kitchen. Where a frame stands empty with nothing above it, that is where a door was.

The merchant whose house it was lent it to the government when the capital moved here; the walk has said elsewhere what that loan amounted to. This stop is about the ground, because the ground is all that is left, and because the ground has turned out to know more than the house did.

Nine people were held in slavery in this house by George Washington while he was president: oh na Judge, Hercules poh zee, Moll, Austin, Richmond, Giles, Paris, Christopher Sheels, and Joe Richardson. Pennsylvania had passed a law in seventeen eighty that freed any enslaved person brought into the state after six months of continuous residence, and the household moved them across the state line and back before each six months ran out, so that the law never reached them. One of the nine, oh na Judge, walked out of this house in seventeen ninety six while the family was at dinner and was never brought back.

The house came down in eighteen thirty two. Shops went up, then came down, and for most of the twentieth century this corner was paved. When the bell's building behind you was planned, its front door landed within a few steps of where the nine had slept; nobody had yet worked out where the quarters were. The dig in two thousand seven found the foundations. It found the kitchen floor, the passage to the main house, and the curve of the bow window, under a sidewalk people had crossed for thirty years to visit a bell named for liberty without knowing what they stood on.

That is why there is no building here and should not be. A rebuilt house would have a front, and a front is a way of deciding what faces the street. The frames decide nothing: the dining room and the quarters stand in the same steel at the same height, and you can walk through any of the doorways you like, which the nine were not free to do when the doors were real. What is written on this ground has been before a federal court since early two thousand twenty six and still is; the walk takes no side, and describes nothing here a judge might change.

The walk is over. It began at a hall a trade built for itself and still owns, and it ends at a house a merchant built, a government rented, a city tore down, and the ground kept. Everything else on the route is standing. This stop is the one that says what the standing ones leave out, and it says it with an outline, at the corner of Sixth and Market, exactly where the door was.
