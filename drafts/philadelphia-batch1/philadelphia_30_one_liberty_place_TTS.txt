Turn around. Before you look at the tower you came for, look the other way, east down Market Street, to the dark stone mass of City Hall two blocks off and the small bronze figure on top of it. Fix the height of his hat in your mind, because everything in front of you was measured against it, and then turn back west.

You're at Sixteenth and Market. The tower a block ahead on the right, blue glass stepping up to a spire, is One Liberty Place; its shorter twin behind it is Two Liberty Place, and the taller glass slabs farther north are the ones that came after. From this corner you can keep the hat and the spire in one slow turn of your head, and that turn is the whole stop.

Right. The agreement was never a law. For the better part of ninety years no one in Center City built above William Penn, by custom and by cost, and the skyline you'd have seen from this corner in nineteen eighty three was a flat shelf of office buildings with one figure standing over all of them. Then a developer named Willard rowss proposed two towers on this block, and the fight that followed was about identity as much as height: a city that had kept its founder on top for ninety years was being asked whether it still wanted to. It decided it didn't. Helmut yahn designed the tower, taking its stepped crown from New York's Chrysler Building and recasting it in blue glass; the steel passed the top of the hat in nineteen eighty six and the building opened by nineteen eighty seven, at nine hundred forty five feet, almost four hundred feet above the hat.

There is a legend attached, and it is a legend: for the twenty years after the hat was passed, the city's teams won nothing, and when a small figure of Penn was fixed to the top of the kom kast tower in two thousand eight, the baseball team won that fall. People here tell it with a straight face and do not believe it, which is the correct way to hold a story like that.

What the tower changed was not the skyline. Cities change skylines constantly, and most of them never notice. What changed was the thing the skyline had been for: a hundred years of restraint that nobody had voted on, kept because breaking it would have felt rude, and broken the moment one person decided it wouldn't. The glass in front of you is the building that stopped deferring.

Turn one more time, back to the hat. He is still up there, at exactly the height he always was, and everything behind him is a statement about how long a city can agree to something it never wrote down.
