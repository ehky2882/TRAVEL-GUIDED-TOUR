ATLAS · PHILADELPHIA · 02 · The Liberty Bell
---
It's a broken bell.

You should be on the Chestnut Street sidewalk at the south end of the long glass building on the west side of the Mall, between Fifth and Sixth. Look through the glass. The Bell hangs at the far end of the hall, with the red brick of Independence Hall framed in the window behind it — the building it was cast for, across the street. You can see it clearly from here without going in, and the line to go in can be long.

Begin with what it was made to be. The Pennsylvania Assembly ordered a bell for the new State House tower in 1751 from the Whitechapel foundry in London. It arrived, was hung, was tested, and cracked on the first ring. Two local founders named Pass and Stow melted it down and recast it, twice, in 1753, and that is the metal you're looking at — their names are on it. It rang for the Assembly, for proclamations, for funerals, for the reading of the Declaration in the yard on the eighth of July 1776. A working bell in a working tower.

Now the crack. The story most people know says it split while tolling for the funeral of Chief Justice John Marshall in 1835 — and that is a story, told afterward, with nothing written at the time to anchor it. What is documented is this: on Washington's Birthday in 1846 the bell was rung, the existing fracture widened past repair, and it has not been rung since. The thin line you see was actually drilled wider, deliberately, in an attempt to stop the crack from running. It didn't work.

Here is the part that matters more than the crack. For more than eighty years it had no name. In 1835 an anti-slavery publication printed it as the Liberty Bell, taking the word from the verse cast around its crown — proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof — and pointing out that the country hadn't. The abolitionists gave the bell its name. It was theirs before it was everyone's.

Then it traveled. Between 1885 and 1915 it went by rail to expositions across the country, seven trips, and crowds met it at every station. It came back worn and never left again. It moved out of the tower to a glass pavilion on the first day of 1976, and across to this building in 2003.

*[beat]*

So what you're looking at is an object that has been re-meant three times: a tower bell, then an argument, then a relic. The metal never changed. Everything said about it did. Stand with that for a moment, with the building it was made for showing through the window behind it.
