Every map of this city starts at this river. Thomas Holme drew the grid in 1682 from the Delaware westward, and the widest street on his plan runs inland from the shore a few hundred yards to your left; the city was laid out as a thing you arrived at by water and walked into.

Face the river. The gray ship tied up in the basin in front of you is the cruiser Olympia, launched in 1892, and the long dark hull beside her is the submarine Becuna; the museum behind you keeps them both. Across the water is Camden, and upriver to your left the blue towers of the bridge. Behind you and to your left, between Chestnut and Walnut, a deck is being built over the highway, and Dock Street is the way back into the city while the work goes on.

About the ground you're on: none of it existed in Holme's time. The shore ran roughly where the highway runs, and the wharves stepped out from there; between 1967 and 1976 the city filled the river edge to make this landing, named it for Penn, and ran a boulevard along it. The highway arrived in the same years, in a trench between the new land and the old streets, and the city has spent the decades since trying to get back across it to the water it was built to face. The latest attempt is a park the size of a city block, laid over the road between Chestnut and Walnut.

This was the river that worked: shipyards along both banks, sugar refineries, coal barged down from upstate, immigrants stepping off the pier at Washington Avenue a mile south. The Olympia came here to retire because this was where ships came.

*[beat]*

Stand here long enough and the proportions come right. The grid was drawn with a front, and this was it. For two centuries the front was a working waterfront, then for a generation it was a highway with a parking lot beside it, and the latest answer is a park with a ship beside it. A roof over the trench does not undo the trench, but it does put a floor over it, and a floor is what a front door needs.
