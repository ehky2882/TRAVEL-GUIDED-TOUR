PHILADELPHIA — 07 — PHILADELPHIA MUSEUM OF ART STEPS & EAKINS OVAL

Spines: S4 (one clause, the view down the boulevard, "the hat" once) · G3 Centennial (its one mention) · docket 8
---
Most of the people who come here never go inside. They climb seventy-two steps to a pair of bronze doors, turn around, and go back down, and the building at the top has spent the better part of a century being visited from the outside.

The hill it stands on had a job before it had a museum. This was the reservoir for the waterworks below you on the river, a flat tank of city water on the rise that gives the neighborhood its name, and when the reservoir went out of use the city had a hilltop at the end of its new boulevard and nothing on it. What went up between 1919 and 1928 was a Greek temple the size of a city block, designed by Horace Trumbauer's office with Clarence Zantzinger and Charles Borie: three wings around a court, warm Minnesota stone, and a row of painted terracotta figures in the north pediment that visitors rarely notice. The museum itself is older than its building. It was founded in 1876 for the Centennial exhibition held across the river in West Fairmount Park, and it lived in that fair's Memorial Hall for half a century before it moved up here.

The steps are the other history. In 1976 a boxing film ended its training sequence at the top of them, and the flight of stairs became a destination in its own right, climbed by people with no intention of seeing a painting. For a sequel in 1982 the film's star commissioned a bronze of the character from the sculptor Thomas Schomberg, eight and a half feet of boxer with gloves overhead, and left it at the top when the cameras were done. The museum did not want it there. The bronze went to the sports complex in South Philadelphia, came back for later films, left again, and in 2006 settled at the foot of the steps, where it stood for twenty years. In 2026 the city moved it inside the museum for an exhibition and then, in September of that year, set it permanently at the top of the steps, on the spot where the camera had first put it. The space it left is not empty. A statue of Joe Frazier, the heavyweight champion who was from this city and was not fictional, was moved from the stadium district to the foot of the steps in June 2026.

*[beat]*

If you climb them, the view at the top is the one the boulevard was built to deliver: a mile of trees and flags running straight to the tower on City Hall, the hat the whole skyline kept beneath until 1987, and off to the right the glass that finally stood taller. The museum's front door is behind you. It is open, but this stop was never about the door. The building was put on this hill to be the end of a view, and that is how the city has used it: as a facade to arrive at, a flight of stairs with a bronze at the top, and a front door that most people reach and never open.
