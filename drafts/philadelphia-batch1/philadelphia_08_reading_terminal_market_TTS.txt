Trains used to run over your head. For ninety years every commuter into this corner of the city came in on tracks laid a full story above the market floor, and the market was built underneath on purpose, so that a lang kas ter County farmer and a clerk off the morning train would meet in the same aisle. The last train left in nineteen eighty four, and the aisle is still there.

You're at Twelfth and Arch, at the corner of a long brick wall with the market's doors in it. Look south down Twelfth toward Market Street and you'll see the ornate terra cotta front of the old station headhouse, eight stories, with the arched iron shed running back from it toward you. Go in, find the open seating at the center, and stop there.

Okay. Philadelphia named its main street for what ran down the middle of it: for a hundred and fifty years, sheds of market stalls stood in the center of High Street, with the carts going around them, and the city sold its food in the road. In eighteen fifty nine the city pulled the sheds out. The markets moved indoors, to this block, and in eighteen ninety two, when the Reading Railroad wanted the same ground for its new terminal, the railroad and the merchants cut a deal: the trains would go upstairs, on an elevated shed by the engineers Wilson Brothers, and the market would keep the floor. The headhouse on Market Street came a year later, dressed by Francis kim bul. Not one market day was lost in the move.

Look up at the ceiling while you're sitting. That is the underside of the train shed, the floor the locomotives sat on, and when the trains stopped the shed was nearly lost with them: the railroad was bankrupt, the terminal was a shell, and the convention center planned for the site could have taken the market as easily as it took the platforms, and it didn't. The shed was restored and became the convention center's great hall; the market stayed where it had always been, under the hall, and has been run by a nonprofit since nineteen ninety four.

This is the beginning of an argument the city has been having with itself since the start, about where the middle is. Penn drew the middle a mile west of here and nobody went; the middle stayed by the river, then crept along this street a block at a time, markets first and everything else following. Taking the stalls out of the roadway in eighteen fifty nine did not end that; it moved the market indoors and let the street keep the name.

Stay as long as you like, and buy something; a market is the one kind of landmark that only works if you do.
