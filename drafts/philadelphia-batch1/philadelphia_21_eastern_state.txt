This building faces away from you. The front on Fairmount Avenue, towers, arrow slits, a gatehouse with a portcullis, is a medieval castle built in 1829 by an architect who knew perfectly well that nobody would ever attack it. John Haviland put the castle on the street to frighten the street. The building he cared about is behind the wall, and it faces inward.

Inside the thirty-foot granite there is a hub, and from the hub seven long cellblocks run outward like the spokes of a wheel, so that a guard standing in the middle could look down every corridor at once. Each cell held one person. Each had a skylight, a water tap, a toilet, heat, and a small walled yard of its own, which in 1829 was more plumbing than most houses in the city had. What the cells did not have was anyone else. The Pennsylvania system, as the world came to call it, was solitary confinement as a complete theory: a prisoner alone with work, a Bible, and his own thoughts would arrive at penitence, and penitence was the purpose, which is why the building is called a penitentiary and not a prison. Prisoners were moved under hoods so they would not see one another. Visitors crossed the Atlantic to study it; prisons on several continents were built on Haviland's plan.

*[beat]*

The theory did not survive the building. Solitude on that scale broke people more often than it reformed them, the state ran out of room and began putting two to a cell within a generation, and new blocks were squeezed between the spokes until the wheel was hard to read. The system was abandoned in name in 1913 and the prison ran on, overcrowded and aging, until the last people held here left in 1971. Then the gates closed on eleven acres and nothing happened for twenty years. Trees grew through the cellblock roofs.

What stands behind the wall is kept deliberately in that condition. The site calls its approach a stabilized ruin: decay arrested, nothing restored to look new, the trees cut back only where they threatened the structure. This stop does not go in, and that is not a shortcoming. The building was designed so that from here you would see exactly this much of it, a wall meant to be looked at, with the real architecture turned the other way.
