The original is in Paris, and Philadelphia paid for it.

The figure in the gateway in front of you, elbow on knee, is a cast; so is nearly everything behind him, and the distinction between a cast and an original matters less here than in most museums, because Auguste Rodin worked in clay and plaster, and bronze was something made from his models in editions, in his life and after. A cast is not a replica of a Rodin; it is a Rodin.

Jules Mastbaum understood this. He was a Philadelphian who had made a fortune in movie theaters, a business of showing the same reels in many rooms at once, and in 1924, on a visit to the Rodin museum in Paris, he bought his first bronze. Within months he was buying for a city rather than a house. He asked the architects of the boulevard itself to design the building, one of them the same Gréber who had laid out the circle at Logan Square: a small limestone pavilion set behind a garden and a reflecting pool. For its door he commissioned the piece Rodin had never finished casting, the Gates of Hell, a twenty-foot bronze doorway crowded with figures out of Dante, which the sculptor had worked at for thirty-seven years and which existed, when he died in 1917, only in plaster. The Philadelphian ordered two casts, and the first came here and was built into the entrance; the second he sent to the museum in Paris, where visitors photograph it as the original. By the strict count it is the younger of the two.

*[beat]*

Its founder did not live to see it. He died in 1926, and his widow, Etta, finished the building and the collection and handed both to the city, which opened them on the twenty-ninth of November 1929, five weeks after the stock market fell. Elsewhere in this city a founded thing outlived the building it started in; here the founding arrived as a building, the collection and its house in a single deed, with nothing left over to outlive.

The figure at the top of the Gates, small, brooding, is the one enlarged to sit in the gateway behind you. The door itself was retired from service as a door, and you go in beside it, past a doorway that was cast to be opened and was set into the wall instead.
