PHILADELPHIA — 05 — CHRIST CHURCH
Tier 1 · Single stop · Batch B2 · Register: American
Vantage: Second Street, at the churchyard gate, steeple overhead
Spine: pays S2 (a founded thing — the Episcopal Church organized here) · pays S6 (Absalom Jones, one clause) · docket 11 (active worship)
---
It leans. Look up at the steeple from the gate on Second Street and you can see it, or convince yourself you can: the spire sits about twenty two inches off true at the top, and the people who spent most of twenty nineteen up there in scaffolding decided not to straighten it. Steady it, yes; pull it plumb, no. It had been leaning for most of its life.

You're on Second Street between Market and Arch, the long brick body of the church beside you and the tower rising at this end. The building went up through the seventeen twenties and seventeen forties, tall round headed windows and a balustrade along the roofline, grand by colonial standards, done in the manner of the London churches the vestry had plainly been studying. The steeple came later and cost money the parish didn't have, so in the early seventeen fifties one of its members organized a lottery to pay for it, Benjamin Franklin, by deed, which is how most things got done in this town. Robert Smith, a Scottish born carpenter and one of the colonies' early architects, finished it in seventeen fifty four, and for the next half century, by the church's own account, nothing in North America stood taller.

Two things were founded in this room, and neither was a building. In seventeen eighty nine, a church that had been the Church of England in America met here and organized itself as something that answered to no crown, the Episcopal Church, with Christ Church as its birthplace. And in eighteen oh four, ab sa lum Jones, who had been enslaved to a member of this parish's own vestry and had bought his freedom and his wife's, was ordained here as the first Black priest in that church.

Many of the men who signed the Declaration a few blocks from here kept pews in this church, which tells you something about Philadelphia's revolution: it was conducted by churchgoers. The building didn't change in seventeen seventy six. What changed was who it answered to, and then, within a generation, who was allowed to stand at the front of it. A founded thing is like that, the shell stays put while the meaning inside it moves, and all of it happened inside walls that were already old when it did.

It's an active parish. If a service is in progress, stay out here; the steeple is the point anyway, and it doesn't mind being admired a little crooked.
