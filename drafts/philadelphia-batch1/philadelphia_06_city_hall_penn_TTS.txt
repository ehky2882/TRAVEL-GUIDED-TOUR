The man on top is thirty seven feet tall. That is not a statistic; it is the reason the building looks the way it does, because the whole stone pile under him was built to hold one figure at a height from which he could be seen from the rivers, and for most of a century nothing in this city was permitted to stand above his hat.

You're in the open courtyard at the center of the building, with a portal in each of the four walls. The tower rises off the north wall, look up and to that side and you'll see the clock faces, and above them the bronze figure, his back to you and his face turned northeast toward the river he arrived on. The round marker set into the paving under your feet marks the middle of the square that William Penn's surveyor drew in sixteen eighty two for the city's public buildings. The city took one hundred eighty nine years to get around to using it for that.

Now. Of the five squares on the plan, this was the one meant for government, and it was the last to be built on; for two centuries it held a waterworks, then nothing much, while the city's actual business happened a mile east by the Delaware. When the city finally came here it came with everything it had: construction began in eighteen seventy one to John mik ar thur's design and did not finish until nineteen oh one, in a French Second Empire style so heavy with carving, hundreds of figures, by Alexander Milne kawl der's workshop, that the building was out of fashion before the roof went on. Nobody tore it down because nobody could afford to.

The tower was the argument. At five hundred forty eight feet to the top of the hat it stood over everything else in the city, which was the whole idea, and the agreement that followed was never written down: no building in Center City would rise above Penn. It held by courtesy and by the cost of breaking it, which is how most agreements between gentlemen hold, and it held until nineteen eighty seven. You'll see what broke it from Sixteenth and Market. Here, what matters is that for ninety odd years a city that had become one of the largest in the country chose to keep its skyline at the height of one man's brim, and meant it as a compliment.

Walk out through the west portal into dill wurth Park and turn around. From the fountains you get the whole tower in one piece, the statue a dark shape against the sky, and you can see what the agreement protected: not a building, but a proportion. There is an elevator to a deck just under his feet; whether it is running is a question for the visitor center inside the east portal, and this stop assumes it is not.

A plan drawn before anyone lived here set aside its middle for the public, and the public eventually filled it with the heaviest thing it owned. The surprise is not that the tower was built; it's that a growing city spent ninety years treating one man's hat as a ceiling and called it good manners.
