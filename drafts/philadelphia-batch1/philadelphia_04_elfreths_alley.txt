PHILADELPHIA — 04 — ELFRETH'S ALLEY
Tier 1 · Single stop · Batch B2 · Register: American
Vantage: the mouth of the alley at Second Street, looking east
Spine: plants S5 (Brick — the small house owned; ordinary, which is the point) · locked treatment 4
---
Nothing famous happened here, and that is the whole reason to stop.

You're at the mouth of the alley on Second Street, looking east toward the river — thirty-odd brick houses along a lane barely wide enough for a cart, which is what it was cut for. In 1703 two landowners punched a shortcut through their block so people could reach the Delaware without walking around it, and the path took the name of Jeremiah Elfreth, a blacksmith who owned the land at this end.

Walk in slowly. The houses are small — two and three stories, a room or two deep — and they went up one at a time between about 1720 and 1836 for people who worked with their hands: shipwrights, tailors, a pewterer, two dressmakers. Nobody of consequence lived here.

Look at the brick itself as you go. In some of the walls you'll catch bricks set end-on with a dark, glassy face — fired hotter, glazed in the kiln — alternating with the long ones in a pattern called Flemish bond. It cost more, and someone on a tradesman's wage decided it was worth it. About halfway down, on your left, a gap opens into Bladen's Court, a courtyard within the alley, laid out in the middle of the eighteenth century, with the pump that once served the houses around it still standing at the end. Step in if you like; people live behind every wall here, and the alley only works because they do.

*[beat]*

Philadelphia tells its own story in marble and public rooms a few blocks south of here. This is the other version — the city as most of its people actually had it, a brick house the width of a room, owned by someone who made things, on a street nobody intended to be looked at. By the nineteen thirties the alley was poor and hemmed in by factories, and in 1934 the residents and a few allies formed an association that kept it standing — not as a museum, mostly, but as a street, which is the harder thing to keep. The Association calls it one of the oldest continuously lived-on streets in the country; that claim is theirs to make. What isn't in dispute is that the place is ordinary, and that ordinary, here, turned out to be the thing worth saving.

Walk back out the way you came. The city gets grander from here; it doesn't get more honest.
