PHILADELPHIA — 17 — MOTHER BETHEL AME CHURCH
Tier 2 · single stop · B3 · American register
Vantage: the southwest corner of Sixth and Lombard, looking across at the church and the statue courtyard on the northeast corner
Spines: PLANTS S6 (free Black Philadelphia) · S8 FIREWALL · docket 3, 11 · F7 · spends the last "by the church's own account"
---
The first church on this corner arrived on wheels. In 1794 Richard Allen bought a blacksmith's shop, had it hauled by his own teams to the lot he already owned, and set it down here as a house of worship. Everything that followed — four buildings, a denomination, a movement — started with a shed that had been somewhere else the week before.

Stand on the corner opposite, so the whole church fits in view. It is gray stone, rough-faced, with a square tower at the corner nearest you and the entrance on the Sixth Street side, to your left as you face it. The low brick wall at the corner encloses a small courtyard, and the bronze figure in it is Allen, cast in 2016 by Fern Cunningham-Terry. Plaques along the same wall remember nine people killed at a sister church in Charleston in 2015. The sidewalk gives you all of this; nothing here requires a ticket.

Allen was born enslaved in 1760 and bought his freedom as a young preacher. In 1787 he and Absalom Jones organized the Free African Society, a mutual-aid society for the city's free Black residents — dues, sick relief, widows, burials — and around the same time the two of them led Black worshippers out of St. George's Methodist Church rather than keep praying from the seats assigned to them. Jones went on to found the city's first Black Episcopal congregation. Allen bought this lot in 1791, and when the shed came, it was dedicated as Bethel. The church says the ground under it is the oldest parcel in the country continuously owned by African Americans, and that is the church's own account; what nobody disputes is that the parcel has never left them since.

In 1793, when yellow fever emptied the city of anyone who could afford to leave, the Society's members stayed and nursed the sick and buried the dead, at the makeshift hospital at Bush Hill and door to door. Afterward the printer Mathew Carey published an account of the epidemic that accused Black Philadelphians of profiting from it. Allen and Jones answered in print, in January 1794, with a narrative of their own — the first pamphlet by Black authors, by most accounts, to be entered for copyright. They did not quote him back; they itemized.

*[beat]*

The building you're looking at is the fourth on the ground, from 1889 and 1890, by Edward Hazlehurst. Allen and his wife Sarah are interred on its lower level, below a sanctuary that still holds his pulpit. In 1816 the courts confirmed the congregation's title to its own church, and that spring Allen called the other Black Methodist churches to Philadelphia, organized them into the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and was made its first bishop of a church that has since spread to several continents.

If a service is in progress, stay outside. The small museum downstairs keeps short hours; this stop was written from the sidewalk and loses nothing there. What you are looking at is ownership: a congregation that held its own deed from the week the shed arrived, replaced its building three times on its own say-so, and has not had to ask anyone's permission to stand on this corner since.
