ATLAS · PHILADELPHIA · 16 · Second Bank of the United States
---
That's a Greek temple. It was a bank.

You should be on the south side of chess nut Street, between Fourth and Fifth, facing a marble building with eight heavy columns across the front and a plain triangular pediment above them. No windows on the facade, no sign, no ornament beyond the columns themselves. There's a matching portico on the far side you can't see from here. It looks lifted whole from Athens, and that was the intention.

Begin with the architect. William strick lund won the competition for this building in eighteen eighteen with a design modeled directly on the Parthenon, and it was finished in eighteen twenty four. He was a young man and this was the building that made him. The columns are dor ik, the simplest and most severe of the Greek orders. The stone is Pennsylvania marble, quarried up the skoo kull. Nothing about it is Philadelphian in style, and that was the point: a republic barely forty years old was choosing what it wanted to look like, and it chose the ancient democracy it most admired.

Now think about what went on inside. This was the Second Bank of the United States, chartered in eighteen sixteen, the country's central bank, and for about a decade a very powerful one. Then Andrew Jackson decided it was a monopoly run for the rich, vetoed its recharter in eighteen thirty two, and let it die. The federal charter lapsed in eighteen thirty six. The temple had outlived the god in a dozen years.

So the building found other work. It was a customs house for ninety years. Now it holds a gallery of portraits, faces of the people who were in this city in the seventeen seventies and seventeen nineties, many of them painted from life by Charles Willson peel and his family. A building meant to project permanence ended up holding the faces of the people who were trying to invent one.

Here's what I'd take from the columns. A new country doesn't have an architecture; it borrows one and sees if it fits. Philadelphia tried on Athens. The bank failed, the look stayed, and for the next thirty years half the courthouses and banks in America were built to resemble this one.

Look at the steps. They're worn in the middle where two centuries of feet have gone in, depositors, then customs clerks, then people coming to see the portraits. The marble kept count.
