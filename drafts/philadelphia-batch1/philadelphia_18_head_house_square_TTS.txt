The date that matters here is seventeen forty five. That was the year two merchants, Edward ship un and Joseph wor tun, paid for a row of market stalls on Second Street because the city's market on High Street was too far to walk with the week's food, and the neighborhood south of Dock Creek wanted its own.

You're at the north end of it, at Pine Street. The two story brick building with the arch and the cupola is the head house, and behind it the open shed runs one block south toward lom bard on brick piers. Stay on the sidewalk and look along the shed's length; the shed is the public part of this square.

Right. The stalls were called sham bulz, the old word for a butcher's row, and the shed is their shape: a roof, piers, and air, empty most days, which is what a market shed is supposed to be between markets. The head house came later, in eighteen oh four, and it was not built for selling at all. The ground floor was an engine house, two fire engines behind the arch, and the room upstairs was where the fire company met; the clock in the cupola was bought by neighbors who put up the money themselves. A twin head house stood at the South Street end, with a second block of shed between.

In the nineteen fifties the city cleared that southern half for parking, and then, having removed half of the thing, decided the other half was precious: the head house was restored by nineteen sixty one and the shed rebuilt to its old appearance by nineteen sixty three, under jee edwin brum baw, the same architect who had just drawn the tomb in Washington Square. The whole neighborhood was being remade the same way in those years, old brick kept and new brick added to match.

This is the small side of the city's idea of itself, and it is the side that holds up. Merchants and neighbors paid for a shed so they could buy fish without walking a mile, and a fire company took the upstairs so the shed would not burn; nobody governed anything here. The city's middle has been drifting west for three centuries, Market Street carries that argument, but a market this far from it meant the neighborhood had decided to be a middle of its own.

When the market does run, it runs under this roof, and the roof has never belonged to anyone but the street.
