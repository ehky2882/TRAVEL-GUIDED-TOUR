ATLAS · PHILADELPHIA · 01 · Independence Hall
---
It is smaller than you expect. Almost everyone says so.

You should be on the north side of chess nut Street, between Fifth and Sixth, with the red brick building directly across from you and the white tower rising from its center. Two low wings run out to either side, linked to the main block by short arcades. Behind you is the long green of the Mall. The security entrance is around the back, on the south side, and you don't need it for this.

Start with the brick. This was built as the Pennsylvania State House, begun in seventeen thirty two and finished about twenty years later, to the plans of a lawyer named Andrew Hamilton and a master builder named Edmund wool ee. It was a provincial assembly hall for a colony of farmers and merchants, and it looks like one: two stories, a long row of windows, a hipped roof, a modest front door. The Assembly met on the ground floor in the room on your left as you face it. The colony's supreme court sat on your right. Nobody designing it imagined what would be signed in that left hand room in July of seventeen seventy six, or debated there through the summer of seventeen eighty seven, and the building has been apologizing for its own scale ever since.

Now the tower. It is the part that looks grand, and it is the part that isn't original. The first steeple, the wooden one that held the big bell, rotted so badly it was taken down in seventeen eighty one and the brick tower sat capped and plain for almost fifty years. What you're looking at was designed by William strick lund in eighteen twenty eight, taller, more elaborate, with a clock and a cupola, because by then the city understood what this building had become and wanted it to look the part. The famous building, in other words, was dressed up afterward to match its fame.

One more thing to hold in mind while you look at the left hand windows. The men who met in that room wrote about liberty with a precision that still astonishes, and a good many of them owned other human beings while they did it. Both facts are true of the same room. The building doesn't resolve that for you, and neither will I.

So stand with the scale for a minute. A country that would stretch to the Pacific was argued into being in a space about the size of a large living room, in a building that a colonial legislature thought was quite enough. The tower was the city's later opinion. The room was the original one.
