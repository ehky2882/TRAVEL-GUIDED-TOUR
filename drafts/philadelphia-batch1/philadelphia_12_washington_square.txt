PHILADELPHIA — 12 — WASHINGTON SQUARE
Tier 1 · single stop · B3 · American register
Vantage: the plaza in front of the Tomb of the Unknown Revolutionary War Soldier, west side of the square, near Seventh and Walnut
Spines: PLANTS S1 (the drawing came first) · pays S6 (the burial ground) · S8 containment · docket 1, 3, 7 · F13
---
You're standing in a cemetery. It was a cemetery before it was a park, and for most of a century the dead outnumbered the living here by a very wide margin, which is worth knowing before the trees and the benches persuade you otherwise.

Face the low stone wall with the flame in front of it. Behind the flame, a bronze of the general the square is named for, cast from the figure Houdon made of him. To your right, through the trees, is Walnut Street; Independence Hall is a block beyond it, back over your right shoulder. The paths meet at the center of the square behind you.

So. In 1682, before a single house stood here, a surveyor named Thomas Holme drew this square on paper for William Penn: five open squares set into a grid between two rivers, one at the center and one in each quarter, and this was the southeast one. The drawing came first; the city grew into it. That holds everywhere in Philadelphia, and it is unusual — most cities are drawn after the fact, and this one was drawn before anybody arrived to disagree with the drawing.

The city did not treat the drawing gently. In 1706 this square was set aside as a potter's field, the burying ground for people who had no church to bury them: the poor, strangers who died here passing through, sailors, and Philadelphians who were Black, enslaved and free, laid in ground the city's congregations would not open to them. During the British occupation of 1777 and 1778, soldiers of both armies were buried here, many of them prisoners from the Walnut Street jail. In the fever summer of 1793 the square took the dead again, and the Free African Society's members did much of the carrying. The city closed the ground to burials in 1794, let it green over, and planted it.

*[beat]*

The tomb was finished in 1957, to a design by G. Edwin Brumbaugh, and the soldier under the stone was taken from this ground the year before. Nobody knows which army he belonged to. The people who chose him accepted that, and the inscription chose a side for him anyway; you can read it for yourself and decide how you feel about the choice.

One more thing, by the Sixth and Walnut gate on your way out: a young sycamore, grown from a cutting of a tree whose seed went around the moon on Apollo fourteen. The original was planted here in 1975 and died; this one replaced it. The square has always been a place where the city put what it meant to remember and then had to decide, later, what the remembering was for.
