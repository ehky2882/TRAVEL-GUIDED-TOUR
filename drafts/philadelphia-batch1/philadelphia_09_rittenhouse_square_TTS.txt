The usual question about this square is who designed it, and that is the wrong question. The right one is why it's full. All five of Penn's squares are handsome; this is the one that is used, at every hour, by people who did not come to see it.

You're in the middle, by the long pool, with diagonal paths running out to the four corners and a low stone balustrade around you. Beyond the trees on every side the buildings come right to the curb: apartment houses, a hotel or two, a few older mansions turned into something else, shops on the ground floors. That tight edge is the first thing to notice, because it is the answer.

So, the design, briefly, since you'll want it. In sixteen eighty two this was the southwest square on Thomas hohm's drawing, and for a long time it was pasture and not much else; unlike the squares to the east, nobody was buried here. The layout under your feet dates from nineteen thirteen, when the neighbors paid the architect Paul kret to give the square a plan: the diagonals, the central oval, the pool, the stone work at the entrances. Halfway along the southwest path there's a small bronze goat by Albert less lee, worn gold along his back by generations of children, which tells you more about the square than any plaque would.

But kret's plan is not why it works. Jane jay kubz used this square as an example in nineteen sixty one, and her argument was about the edge, not the center: a park is kept alive by the mixture of things around it, homes, offices, a church, a music school, shops, places to eat, because each of them sends people in at a different hour, and a park that is used at every hour is a park that is safe at every hour. A square surrounded by one kind of building empties when that building closes.

Look around once more with that in mind. The drawing gave the city this open square two and a half centuries before anyone could say what it was for, and the city, by accident more than design, built the right things against its edges. The other four squares each got a monument or a boulevard or a highway. This one got neighbors.

That is the whole secret, and it is not a design secret; it is a zoning accident that nobody has been foolish enough to fix.
