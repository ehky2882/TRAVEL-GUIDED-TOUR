PHILADELPHIA — 20 — RACE STREET PIER & THE BEN FRANKLIN BRIDGE
Tier 2 · Single stop · Batch B2 · Register: American
Vantage: the tip of the pier, bridge overhead to the north
Spine: pays S3 (two rivers, one flat city — the Delaware as the working front) · PRE-PLANT PAYOFF (S3 plants at 22) · docket 1 (Lenape statement lives in W1; present-tense vocabulary only)

You'll hear them before you see them: the trains. Every few minutes a high speed line to New Jersey crosses the river on the bridge above you, and the sound comes down through the steel like weather.

You're standing at the end of a pier that pushes out into the Delaware from the foot of Race Street, with the Ben Franklin Bridge rising immediately to your north, blue steel, two stone towers, seven lanes, two rail tracks and a walkway. It opened in nineteen twenty six; Ralph mod jess kee did the engineering and Paul kret, the Frenchman who drew the Parkway on the other side of town, gave it its face. Across the water is kam dun. Behind you is the city, and between you and it, the highway.

The pier itself is a city dock that was rebuilt in twenty eleven by James Corner Field Operations as the first finished piece of a plan for this whole stretch of riverfront. A lawn and seating below; above it, a long ramp that climbs about twelve feet along the bridge side, so that walking out you rise toward the span and the river opens in front of you. The railing on that side leans in toward you, deliberately, it tips the whole pier toward the water. At night the paving itself glows, from solar lights set into it.

Philadelphia has two rivers, and it treats them differently. The one in front of you is the working one, wide, brown, moving freight, and it is the reason the city is here at all: the first streets ran down to this water, and for a century and a half the port was the town. Then the city turned its back. The highway behind you went in through the sixties and seventies and put a wall of concrete and noise between Philadelphia's original streets and the river they were laid out to reach, so that for two generations the Delaware was something you crossed and otherwise ignored. The other river, the skoo kull, got the park and the boathouses and the picture. This one got the port and the interstate. The pier under your feet is the city turning back around, one dock at a time.

When you're ready, walk back toward the streets, under the highway, which at least is honest about what it is, and notice how little of the city faces its water, which this pier was built to start correcting.
