PHILADELPHIA — 14 — BETSY ROSS HOUSE
Tier 2 · Single stop · Batch B2 · Register: American
Vantage: the Arch Street sidewalk, between Second and Third, north side
Spine: pays S5 (the small house owned) · docket 14 (attribute-don't-sort — the house's claim stated as the house's)
---
This is the house's story, and the house tells it well, so hear it the way the house does.

You're on Arch Street between Second and Third, on the north side, in front of a narrow brick house two and a half stories tall with a garden court beside it and a flag out front. Stay on the sidewalk for now. The house dates from around 1740 — a shop below, living quarters above, scarcely wider than a single room. In 1776 a young upholsterer named Elizabeth Griscom Ross, newly widowed at twenty-four, was renting a house on this street and running her trade from it, and she did make flags — the colony's navy paid her for them the following year, which is on paper.

The rest came in 1870, when her grandson William Canby stood up before the Historical Society of Pennsylvania and told what the family had been saying among themselves for decades: that a committee had called on her in the summer of 1776, that she had suggested a five-pointed star in place of a six, and that the first flag of the new country had come off her table. That is the account as the family told it, nearly a century after the fact, and this house is where they said it happened — others have placed her next door, and the house, understandably, holds to its address.

By the nineteen thirties the building was near collapse. A radio manufacturer named Atwater Kent paid for its restoration, hired the architect Brognard Okie to do it, bought the neighboring lots to make the garden, and opened the whole thing to the public on Flag Day, 1937; four years later he gave it to the city.

*[beat]*

Set the flag aside for a moment and look at what is actually standing. A brick artisan's house of about 1740, a room wide, shop downstairs and bed upstairs, is precisely the kind of building most of this city lived and worked in — Arch Street was lined with them — and almost none survive on this block now except this one. The flag is the reason it was saved. The house is the reason it's worth seeing. Whatever happened upstairs in 1776, the thing in front of you is real; the only question is which real thing you're looking at, and the house has made its choice.
