There are seven rooms inside this building, and no two of them agree on what century it is. One is Egyptian, with hieroglyphs copied from inscriptions along the Nile and checked, since, by people who read them. One is Gothic; one is Norman, one Renaissance, one Ionic, one Corinthian, and the seventh the builders called Oriental, which was a nineteenth-century way of saying Moorish. Seven halls, seven architectures, stacked on two floors behind one granite front: that is what is in there, and the front is the one part of it the street is given.

The front earns the look on its own. The porch is Norman, deep and round-arched, and the whole facade is New England granite, cut, squared and numbered at the quarry and fitted here like a kit, which is how a society that calls its members builders would want it done.

*[beat]*

The Grand Lodge of Pennsylvania bought this corner in 1867, held a competition, and gave the work to James Windrim, who was twenty-seven and a member. The cornerstone went in during 1868; the building opened in 1873; and the decorating of the seven halls ran on through the 1880s under George Herzog, who was painting ceilings across the street at the same time.

The date is the thing to carry out of here. When the Temple opened, the square it faced was a construction site, because City Hall's foundations had been going in since 1871 and would be going in, one way and another, for twenty-eight more years. The Masons raised a two-hundred-and-fifty-foot tower to face a hole in the ground, and for two decades it had nothing to answer to across Broad Street. Then the hat arrived in 1894, more than twice as high, and the Temple became what it has been since: the building across from the building.

None of that is a loss. A tower built to be seen and then quietly overtaken is a specific kind of object, and it keeps its porch pointed the same way regardless. The halls behind it are ticketed and the tours run on the Lodge's own calendar, so this stop stays on the sidewalk and takes the seven rooms on report. What the sidewalk gets is the granite, the arch and the tower, and in 1873 those were finished while the thing across the street was still a wall going up.
