PHILADELPHIA — 28 — CHRIST CHURCH BURIAL GROUND & ARCH STREET MEETING HOUSE
Tier 2 · Single stop · Batch B2 · Register: American
Vantage: the iron rails at Fifth and Arch, then two blocks east along Arch to the meeting house portico
Spine: pays S2 (the founded man under a slab) · pays S5 (plain brick as doctrine) · docket 11 (active meeting) · docket 16 (Franklin by deed)
---
Boston started this.

In the eighteen fifties, Bostonians put up a statue of Benjamin Franklin — their native son, after all — and let it be known that his grave in Philadelphia sat behind a seven-foot brick wall in a corner where nobody went. The city took the hint. In 1858 a section of that wall came down and iron rails went in, so that anyone on the sidewalk at Fifth and Arch could see the stone without paying or asking. That is where you're standing. Through the rails, a few feet in, lies a low marble slab with two names on it, his and his wife Deborah's, and the year 1790.

There will be pennies on it. There usually are — the habit is old and local and comes from a saying attributed to him about pennies saved — and the church that owns the ground would rather you kept yours: the coins have pitted the marble, and conservators had to repair a crack in it in 2016. Look, and keep your change.

The burial ground was bought in 1719, when the churchyard three blocks east filled up, and it's two acres of signers, physicians and printers under old trees. If the gate on Arch Street is open, the walk to the grave is a short one; if it isn't, the rails were put here for exactly this.

Now walk two blocks east along Arch, toward the river. On your left, set back behind a brick wall of its own, is a long, low, plain brick building with a columned porch and nothing else — no steeple, no colored glass, no ornament anywhere. That is the Arch Street Meeting House, built in 1804 by a Quaker carpenter named Owen Biddle on ground William Penn gave the Friends to bury their dead. The building stands on the graves; many who died in the fever of 1793 are under it. It was built for the yearly meeting, the business session of the whole region's Quakers, and the room on the west end still fills on Sunday mornings — if a meeting is in progress, stay outside.

*[beat]*

Hold the two together. A man who founded half this city's institutions lies under a slab you could cover with a tablecloth, and the sect that founded the city itself built its principal house as a brick box with a porch. Philadelphia's founders, by instinct and then by doctrine, did not build to be looked at; they built to be used, and the marble came later, from other people. The same idea in houses is Elfreth's Alley, a few blocks from here. Plainness wasn't a shortage of money in this town. It was a position.

In that west room, Lucretia Mott argued the end of slavery from a plain bench. The room hasn't changed, which was always the point of the room.
