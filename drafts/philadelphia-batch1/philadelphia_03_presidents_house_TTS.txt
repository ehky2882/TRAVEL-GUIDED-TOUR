ATLAS · PHILADELPHIA · 03 · The President's House
---
It's November of seventeen ninety, and the President of the United States has just moved into a rented house.

You should be at the corner of Market and Sixth, inside the low brick walls that mark the outline of the house Washington lived in. The Liberty Bell Center is directly to your south. The open frames of brick and glass trace the old rooms. There's no roof, no door, nothing to enter; you're standing in a plan.

Start with the address. This was one hundred and ninety High Street, High is what Market Street was called, and the house belonged to Robert Morris, the financier who had helped pay for the war. When Congress voted in seventeen ninety to make Philadelphia the temporary capital for ten years while a permanent one was built on the Potomac, Morris moved out and offered his house. George Washington rented it. He lived here from seventeen ninety until seventeen ninety seven, and John Adams after him until eighteen hundred, and then the government packed up and left for Washington, as it had always said it would.

That is the thing to hold onto. Philadelphia was a capital on loan. It was the obvious choice, and it got the job with an expiration date already written in. The president lived in a merchant's house. Congress met in a county courthouse. The Supreme Court sat in the city's own hall. Nothing was built for the purpose, because the purpose was leaving. For ten years the city ran the republic out of borrowed rooms and then handed the keys back.

Look at the outline of the back rooms, toward the Bell Center. Washington's household here included nine people he held in slavery, among them oh na Judge, who escaped from this house in seventeen ninety six, and the cook Hercules poh zee, who left the following year, and he moved them in and out of Pennsylvania on a schedule to keep them from qualifying for freedom under the state's own law. That is documented, and it belongs in any honest account of the address.

Morris's house was pulled down in eighteen thirty two and the block went to shops, then to the Mall.

Ten years. Two presidents. A rented house that the city never got to keep and the country never meant to stay in. Philadelphia has been the former capital far longer than it was ever the capital, and this empty rectangle on a busy corner is the clearest place to feel the difference.
