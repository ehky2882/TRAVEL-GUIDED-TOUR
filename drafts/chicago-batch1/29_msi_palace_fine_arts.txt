CHICAGO — STOP 29
GRIFFIN MUSEUM OF SCIENCE AND INDUSTRY — THE 1893 SURVIVOR
Single-stop · T2 · Clean version

---

It's 1893, and everything around you is white, enormous, and temporary.

You should be on the north side of the building, out on the lawn, looking up at the long front — a rank of columns, a dome over the center, carved figures along the roofline, all of it pale stone. Behind you, Jackson Park: lagoons, a wooded island, the lake through the trees. Frederick Law Olmsted laid that ground out. This is the Griffin Museum of Science and Industry now, and it is the last building of the World's Columbian Exposition still standing where it was built.

Now.

Twenty-two years after the Great Chicago Fire, the city put on the fair here — roughly two hundred buildings, most of them shaped by Daniel Burnham's office, all of them painted white, which is where the nickname came from. The White City. The walls were a material called staff: plaster of Paris, hemp fiber and glue, troweled over wood and steel. Fast to raise, cheap to demolish, and not intended to see a second winter.

This building was the exception, for one reason. As the Palace of Fine Arts it was going to hold borrowed masterworks, and the museums lending them refused to send anything into a plaster shed. So Charles Atwood put a steel-and-brick structure underneath, then plastered over it to match the neighbors.

The fair was designed to be thrown away. The only piece of it left is the piece nobody was willing to gamble on — and it stands not because anyone judged it the finest building on the grounds, but because a lender in another city wanted a guarantee in writing.

It housed the Field Museum until 1920, then sat empty and came apart. Louis Sullivan, among others, argued it should be cleared. Julius Rosenwald, who ran Sears, disagreed; he took the park commissioners to Germany to show them what a science museum could be, put up several million dollars, and had the crumbling plaster recut in limestone. The museum opened in 1933. He insisted his name stay off it.

*[beat]*

Every column and every figure up there is a replacement, carved in the nineteen-thirties. But the bones behind them — the steel and brick that only exist because somebody demanded a guarantee — are the last of the White City, and they have been holding this front up for well over a century.
