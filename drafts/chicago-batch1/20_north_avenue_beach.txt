CHICAGO — STOP 20
NORTH AVENUE BEACH
Single-stop · T2 Popular · clean version

---

The ship in front of you has never been anywhere.

Come down onto the sand south of the beach house, so you are looking back at it with the lake on your left and downtown off to your right. Blue and white, portholes down the side, decks stacked up toward a bridge and a mast. It reads as a lake steamer tied off at the shore. It is a building, made of concrete, and it is not going anywhere.

Walk around to the lake side.

The original was a Works Progress Administration job, finished in 1940 and designed by a Park District architect named Emanuel Buchsbaum in the Art Moderne style, which made everything built that decade look like it was already in motion. The frame structure rotted out over the next sixty years and came down. What you are looking at is a slightly larger concrete copy from the end of the nineties, doing the same work the first one did — lifeguards, showers, changing rooms, chair rentals, somewhere to buy something fried.

The sand is an invention too. This beach came out of the same Depression-era program: something over eight hundred thousand square feet of new shoreline pushed into the lake, running north from here to Fullerton.

*[beat]*

Now look south along the path, past the volleyball nets, for a low concrete wing hanging over a row of stone tables. That is the Chess Pavilion, from 1957. Twenty boards set into concrete on three tiers, a five-foot limestone king and queen standing at either end, bishops and knights carved into the walls — sculpture by Boris Gilbertson, architecture by Maurice Webster, paid for by Laurens Hammond, the man behind the Hammond organ, who liked to play chess at this beach and thought there ought to be a proper place to do it around the clock.

Which is the thing about this stretch. Everywhere else, Chicago is making an argument — about height, about money, about permanence, about who got there first. Here it manufactured a coastline, parked a ship that cannot sail on top of it, and installed a limestone chess set, and the skyline's entire job is to be the view behind somebody's volleyball game.

Stay a while if you have it. The lake runs colder than it looks well into the summer, and the wind off it will tell you what kind of day you are actually having.
