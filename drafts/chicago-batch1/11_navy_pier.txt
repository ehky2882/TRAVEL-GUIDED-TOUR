CHICAGO — STOP 11
NAVY PIER — HEAD OF THE PIER
Single-stop · T1 Essentials · clean version

---

You have walked something like half a mile out into a lake.

Stand at the far east end of the pier, past the ballroom with the domed roof, out where the railing turns and there is nothing ahead but open water. If the Centennial Wheel is behind your right shoulder and the lake fills the view in front of you, you are in the right place.

Turn around.

That is the whole city from the outside — one of the few places you can stand off the coast of Chicago and read it as a single object rather than a street you are on. The ground you are doing it from is engineered: the pier runs about three thousand feet east into Lake Michigan, and when it opened in 1916 it sat on roughly twenty thousand wooden piles driven into the lakebed.

It was built as freight infrastructure. The city's 1909 lakefront plan called for two piers, the planners talked themselves up to five, and exactly one got built — a working cargo dock with a public promenade over the top of it, which nobody had really tried.

Then the wars took it.

*[beat]*

In 1942 the Navy came back, and what it did with the lake is hard to believe standing next to it. It needed to qualify carrier pilots and had no carriers to spare, so it bought two Great Lakes excursion steamers — side-wheel paddle boats with staterooms and a dance floor — cut everything off above the waterline and bolted flight decks on top. The Wolverine and the Sable, docked right here. Every morning they steamed a mile out and spent the day launching and recovering warplanes, pilots flying with their cockpits open in case they went into the water. Around eighteen thousand qualified out there, including a young naval aviator named George H. W. Bush. Drivers on Lake Shore Drive pulled over to watch.

The Navy chose Lake Michigan because it was big enough to behave like an ocean and quiet enough to be a classroom. That is what this pier is actually for. Everything built along it — the wheel, the shops, the theater — faces back toward the city. The water behind you is the reason any of it is here.

So give it a minute before you walk back. Weather comes off that lake and decides what kind of day Chicago is having, and the end of the pier is the closest you get to asking it directly.
