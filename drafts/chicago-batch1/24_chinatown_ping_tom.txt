CHICAGO — STOP 24
CHINATOWN & PING TOM MEMORIAL PARK
Single-stop · T2 · Clean version

---

Two Norwegian-American architects who had never set foot in China designed the building that has stood for Chinatown for almost a hundred years.

You should be on Wentworth Avenue a block or so south of Cermak, the tiled gate behind you. Ahead on your left, at twenty-two sixteen, a three-story building faced entirely in glazed terra cotta: two pagoda-topped towers, open balconies across the upper floors, green and gold dragons worked into the tile. That's the Pui Tak Center, built in 1928 as the On Leong Merchants Association Building, and known here for a century as Chinatown's city hall.

Christian Michaelsen and Sigurd Rognstad, sons of Norwegian immigrants, got the commission because no architect of Chinese descent was licensed to practice in Illinois — the exclusion laws had closed that door along with most others. So they took the job, tracked down a German scholar's book on Chinese architecture, and worked from the plates. The terra cotta was fired by an Illinois pottery better known for garden vases. A Chicago meeting hall in a skin copied from photographs, and the neighborhood took to it immediately.

The gate is younger — 1975, four characters hand-painted onto the tile that translate roughly as "the world is for all." When it went up it marked the actual edge of the neighborhood; it doesn't now. Chinatown kept expanding and left its own front door somewhere in the middle.

A few blocks northwest, where the streets run out at the river, there's a park with a red pagoda. Chinatown had two parks until 1962, when the Dan Ryan Expressway took both, and for nearly thirty years afterward it had almost none. Ping Tom, a businessman from here, assembled a private firm and bought a dead rail yard along the water. The park carries his name because he died four years before it opened.

*[beat]*

Two moments, forty years apart, the same shape to both: something this neighborhood wanted, wasn't handed, and went and arranged for itself — once with a library book, once with a land purchase. Probably part of why this is one of the few Chinatowns in the country still gaining people rather than losing them. Stay a minute and look at the tile. Copied from a book by two men from Norway, fired in an Illinois kiln, and it has been the face of this place longer than almost anyone on this street has been alive.
