MUSEUM CAMPUS — THE VANTAGE ACROSS MONROE HARBOR

Three of the buildings around you are about a century old. The place they are standing in is not yet thirty.

Work your way out to the point beside the ad ler Planetarium — the low round one at the far end of the drive — and stand where the walkway curves and the harbor opens up. Lake at your back, city in front of you, the boats of mun roh Harbor filling the water in between.

Pick out the three.

Furthest north, the long white one with the row of columns is the Field Museum, opened in nineteen twenty-one. It did not start here. For its first quarter century the collection lived down in Jackson Park inside a leftover building from the eighteen ninety-three world's fair, and this Greek temple is the permanent home it eventually built for itself.

South of that, the white marble box with the wave shapes running along its roofline is the shed Aquarium, nineteen thirty, by Graham, Anderson, prohbst and White — the same firm behind the Wrigley Building and the Merchandise Mart. To fill it, twenty insulated railroad tank cars ran eight round trips down to Key West and hauled back a million gallons of ocean water, because nobody had tried a serious saltwater aquarium a thousand miles inland before.

And the twelve sided one you are standing beside opened that same year: the first planetarium in the Western Hemisphere, paid for by a Sears executive named Max ad ler, who had seen one in Munich and decided Chicago should have the next. It opened on his birthday.

The odd part is what was never built. For seventy years those three buildings sat on the same piece of lakefront and were not one place, because eight lanes of Lake Shore Drive ran between them. In nineteen ninety-eight the city shifted the northbound lanes a few hundred feet west and laid park over the old roadbed, and three institutions became a campus without a single new structure going up.

Chicago's instinct, nearly everywhere else you have stood on this trip, is to add — another floor, another tower, another wing. This is the rare case of the city improving a place by taking something out of it.

Give the skyline a minute from here before you go in. This is the angle the postcards use, and the reason you can stand still and look at it is that somebody moved a highway.
