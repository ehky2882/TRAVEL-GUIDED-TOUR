ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 04: Willis Tower (exterior street vantage)
T1 Essentials | TTS-safe version

The name on the building is Willis. Almost nobody who lives here says it.

You should be standing on Wah-ker Drive, on the sidewalk between Adams and Jackson, with the black tower filling the sky across the street. Step back toward the river if you can — you'll want the distance. Then look up until the building stops.

Start with the shape.

It isn't one tower. It's nine, bundled together like a fistful of square straws, each of them about seventy-five feet across and all of them tied into one block at the ground. Two of the nine stop at the fiftieth floor. Two more stop at the sixty-sixth. Three more end around the ninetieth. The last two carry on alone to the top. That staircase silhouette isn't styling — those setbacks are tubes ending.

The idea belonged to fahz lur rah mahn kahn, a structural engineer born in dah kuh who spent his career at Skidmore, Owings and Merrill here in Chicago. Bundling the tubes let the building brace against the wind as one thick object rather than one tall thin one, which meant less steel, which meant more height for less money. The story goes that he demonstrated it by holding up a handful of cigarettes at uneven heights. Whether or not that happened, the sketch is the building.

Sears commissioned it, filled it, and then left it — the company moved out to the suburbs in the nineties. The name on the lease now belongs to a London insurance broker that bought the rights in two thousand nine. The city declined to update its vocabulary.

It stood as the tallest building in the world for roughly twenty-five years, and it isn't the tallest in the country anymore. Chicago is unusually relaxed about that. This is a place that invented the tall building, lost the record, invented a new way to build tall, lost it again, and mostly just kept going.

Fourteen hundred and fifty feet to the roof, and it still reads as nine plain boxes of different heights. Stay a second and find the seams.
