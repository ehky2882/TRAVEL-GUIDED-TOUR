ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 07: Buckingham Fountain — Grant Park
T1 Essentials | Clean version

---

The fountain is a portrait of Lake Michigan. Once you know that, you can't go back to seeing it as decoration.

You're in Grant Park where Columbus Drive meets Ida B. Wells Drive, at the rim of a pink marble basin roughly as wide as a city block, with three stacked tiers rising out of the center. Pick a side and find the bronze animals down in the water. There are four groups of them, one at each compass point.

Right.

Those are sea horses, and there are four sets because four states touch Lake Michigan: Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Wisconsin. The basin is the lake. The tiers are the water it holds. The whole object is a scale model of the thing sitting half a mile behind you — a strange, faintly grandiose idea for a fountain, and the reason this one doesn't resemble anything else. The bronze work is by Marcel Loyau, a French sculptor who won a prize in Paris for it the same year the fountain opened.

When it's running, the center jet goes up around a hundred and fifty feet and holds a major display for twenty minutes before dropping back. It doesn't run all year; this is a fountain in a city with real winters. If the basin is dry when you're standing here, that's the calendar, not a failure.

It arrived in 1927. Kate Buckingham paid for it, put her brother Clarence's name on it, and then left money for its upkeep so the city would never get to argue about whether to maintain it. The design came from Edward Bennett, who eighteen years earlier had co-authored the Plan of Chicago with Daniel Burnham — the document that argued this entire lakefront belonged to the public rather than to whoever could afford to buy it.

Bennett didn't pick this spot casually. In that plan, roughly this point is the center of Chicago, with the city's main axis running west from here into the neighborhoods. Most of the plan never got built. The fountain marking the middle of it did.

[beat]

The model was a fountain at Versailles. This one is about twice the size, which tells you most of what you need to know about the civic mood here in the nineteen twenties.

Find a spot on the rim if there's room. It was built to be sat on.
