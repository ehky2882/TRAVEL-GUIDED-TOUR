ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 03: Art Institute — lions and steps, Michigan Avenue face
T1 Essentials | Clean version

---

It's 1893, and this building isn't a museum yet.

You should be on Michigan Avenue, on the far sidewalk if you can get there, looking across at a wide limestone front with a broad staircase and a bronze lion on either side of it. Stand back far enough to take in the triangular pediment above the columns.

Take the lions first.

Bronze, more than two tons each, installed in May 1894 — which means the building stood here for a full year with nothing guarding the door. Edward Kemeys modeled them. He was self-taught, and he specialized in animals, which was a real profession at the time. He described the north lion as on the prowl and the south one as standing in defiance, and once you know that you can't unsee it: the north one's head is low and pushed forward, hunting. The south one's chin is up, daring you.

They have no names. Chicago never gave them any and dresses them instead — wreaths in December, helmets when a team makes a run.

You'll hear that they were cast from sculptures at the World's Fair. They weren't. It's a good story that has been checked and doesn't survive it.

Now the building. This went up for the World's Columbian Exposition, the only fair structure built outside Jackson Park, and it hosted the fair's lectures and congresses before the museum moved in at the end of the year. Boston architects, Corinthian columns, friezes copied off the Parthenon — the entire grammar of permanence, deployed by a city not yet sixty years old.

What you can't see from here is that the museum straddles a railroad. Commuter trains run in a trench directly behind this facade, and the building has been extended back over them more than once. The front you're looking at is the oldest part of it, and by now the smallest.

Look at the pediment. Carved into it are the names of great artists.

[beat]

When those names went up, the museum owned work by almost none of them.

Which isn't embarrassing. It's the most revealing thing on this facade: carve the ambition into the stone first, then go and earn it. They did earn it. The names never had to move.
