ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 14: Daley Plaza — The Picasso
T2 Popular | Clean version

---

Fifty feet tall, a hundred and sixty-two tons of steel, and nobody has ever been able to say what it is.

You're in Daley Plaza, the open block bounded by Washington, Dearborn, Randolph and Clark. The sculpture stands at the east end — rust-colored, wide as a bus, and open through the middle, so you can see the buildings behind it straight through. Directly behind it is the tower it was made for, in exactly the same material.

Look at the face first, or at where a face would be.

Picasso was asked for a sculpture in 1963 and sent a model a couple of years later. He was offered a hundred thousand dollars, turned the money down, and described the finished thing as a gift to the people of Chicago. He never titled it, never explained it, and never saw it — he never set foot in the United States at all. It was fabricated at the steel works in Gary, Indiana, trucked here in pieces, and unveiled in August 1967 in front of a very large and largely unimpressed crowd.

People hated it. There were protest signs. Guesses ran from a woman to a bird to an Egyptian god, and Picasso later suggested it had something to do with his Afghan hound. That is as settled as this gets.

Now look past it at the building. That's the Daley Center, mostly courtrooms. Jacques Brownson designed it after studying under Mies van der Rohe here in Chicago, and it's six hundred and forty-eight feet tall with only thirty-one floors, because courtrooms need the height. Mies is said to have told him he wished he'd done it himself.

Tower and sculpture are both Cor-Ten steel, engineered to rust — the corrosion forms a skin that seals what's underneath. The color you're looking at is the finish. It was never going to be shiny.

[beat]

Watch the base for a minute. The lower slope is worn smooth, because for almost sixty years Chicago kids have used the most argued-about artwork in the city as a slide.

Nobody won the argument. Everybody kept the sculpture.
