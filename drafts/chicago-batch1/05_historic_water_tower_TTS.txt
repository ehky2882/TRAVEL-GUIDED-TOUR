Stop 05: Historic Water Tower — the fire survivor
T1 Essentials | TTS-safe version

Almost everything you've heard about this building is slightly wrong.

You're at Michigan Avenue and Chicago Avenue, in the small plaza around a yellow limestone tower with battlements and turrets, looking like a castle that got lost. Before anything else, turn around and look across the avenue. That larger building in the same yellow stone, same architect, same year — that's the pumping station, and it is the more important of the two.

The tower was never a water tower in the way you're picturing. There's no tank up there. It was built in eighteen sixty-nine to house a standpipe — a hundred and thirty-eight feet of iron pipe, three feet across — whose only job was to absorb the surges coming off the pumps across the street so the water mains wouldn't hammer themselves apart. The pipe came out in nineteen eleven. The spiral staircase that wrapped around it is still in there.

Here's the wrong part. You will be told this was the only building to survive the Great Fire of eighteen seventy-one. It wasn't. Several structures came through it. What's true is narrower and better: this was the only public building left standing inside the burned district, which is why it became the thing people walked toward afterward to work out where their houses had been.

And the pumping station, the building that mattered, failed. Its roof caught fire during the night and collapsed onto the pumps, destroying them — and with them the water pressure that might have fought the fire. The waterworks went down at the exact hour the city needed it.

Roughly three hundred people died. The cow is a legend; the city formally exonerated Missus O'Leary in nineteen ninety-seven, more than a century late.

Here's what I'd want you to take from standing here. The fire did not make this city cautious. Within twenty years Chicago had invented the steel frame, reversed its own river, and started building the tallest structures on earth. This tower survived by being heavy, solid and old-fashioned — and the city that grew up around it went the exact opposite way, at speed, and never really stopped.

Oscar Wilde came through in eighteen eighty-two and called it a castellated monstrosity with pepper boxes stuck all over it. Chicago kept it anyway. The pumping station is still pumping.
