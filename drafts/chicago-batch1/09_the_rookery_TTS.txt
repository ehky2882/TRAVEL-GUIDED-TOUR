Those birds are an in-joke that outlived everyone who got it. After the fire of eighteen seventy-one, the city put up a temporary city hall on this corner, built around an old municipal water tank that had survived. Crows and pigeons moved into the walls. Chicagoans started calling the place the rookery — partly for the birds, partly for the politicians working underneath them, and the general sense that you'd be rooked doing business there. The owners wanted something dignified on the door. The name stuck anyway, and John Root carved the birds in so nobody could quietly forget why.

Root and Daniel burn um finished it in eighteen eighty-eight, and when it opened it was called the largest office building in the country. It sits on a floating foundation — iron rails laid in a crisscross grid and set in concrete, spreading the load across ground too soft to hold a normal footing. Chicago is built on mud. Every tall thing here is a negotiation with that fact.

The reason to step inside is the light court: a two-story room under a glass roof, dropped into the middle of the block so the offices around it could face windows inward. Frank Lloyd Wright was brought in to remodel it in nineteen oh five and wrapped Root's ironwork in white marble with fine gold geometry. It's the only interior he ever did in downtown Chicago.

The lobby is open to the public on weekdays and Saturday mornings, and the Adams Street door is the one to use. If it's shut, the arch and the birds are the point anyway.
