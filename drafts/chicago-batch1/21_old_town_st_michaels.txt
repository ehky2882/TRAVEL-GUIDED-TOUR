It is January of 1872, and the ground under this neighborhood is still warm.

Stand at the corner of Cleveland Avenue and Eugenie Street with the church on your side — red brick, a steeple running up past everything around it. This is St. Michael's. Back off far enough to take in the whole front at once.

Start with the walls.

It was finished in 1869 by a German parish that had been here since the 1850s, on land given by a brewer named Michael Diversey, whose name you have probably already seen on a street sign. When it went up, its tower made it the tallest building in Chicago. Two years later the fire came north. The priests and parishioners packed what they could into an oxcart and left, and it took the roof, the interior, every parish building. What stood the next morning was the walls — the tower gone, the bells melted together in the wreckage.

Rebuilding began inside the week; the new church went up inside the old walls and was rededicated in October of 1873, almost two years to the day.

Now look past the church at the houses. Small, wooden, close together.

*[beat]*

There is a reason for that, and it is the half of the story downtown does not tell. In January of 1872, with parts of Chicago still smoldering, thousands of German and Irish North Siders marched on City Hall to fight a proposed ban on building in wood. Both sides had a case: the city had just burned down, and brick cost more than these families had, so a ban meant selling the lot at a loss and leaving. They carried signs reading "Leave a home for the laborer." They won. The code that passed weeks later changed almost nothing, and wood kept going up until the summer of 1874, when a second bad fire pushed the ban through city-wide.

Which means the wooden houses around you sit inside a window about two and a half years wide.

Downtown gives you the version where Chicago answers the fire with steel and height and nerve. This is the same refusal from underneath — a neighborhood that had just watched everything it owned burn, marching for the right to rebuild it out of pine.

One last thing, if you have the legs. Five blocks north on Hudson Avenue a small wooden cottage came through the fire standing, saved by a policeman named Bellinger who tore up the sidewalk out front and soaked the place down as it went past. The cider he supposedly used is a legend — his widow said so herself, forty years on, and it stuck anyway. The cottage is credited to William Boyington, who also built the Water Tower: one architect, two survivors, one limestone and one boards.
