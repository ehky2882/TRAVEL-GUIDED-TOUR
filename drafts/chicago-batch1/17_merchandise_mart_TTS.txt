For thirteen years, this was the largest building in the world.

You're on the south bank of the Chicago River, somewhere between Wells Street and Franklin, looking north at a limestone Art Deco block that runs two full city blocks along the water and doesn't appear to end in either direction. Twenty-five stories, stepped back at the top, with a squared tower in the middle.

Alright.

Marshall Field's company put it up in nineteen thirty as a wholesale market — the idea being that instead of buyers traveling around to a hundred scattered showrooms, you build one roof and make the whole trade come to you. Roughly four million square feet of floor. It held the world record for size until the Pentagon opened in nineteen forty-three, and it was big enough to need its own postal code, which it kept until two thousand eight.

It's built over a railyard, with the city's freight tunnels running underneath, so goods arrived below the floor and went straight up. A building this size is really a piece of infrastructure wearing a facade.

The Kennedy family bought it in nineteen forty-five and held it for decades. Joseph Kennedy lined the riverfront with bronze busts of American merchants on tall pedestals — Marshall Field among them, and Aaron Montgomery Ward, who is a complicated figure to memorialize outside a building this large.

For most of its life the place has been closed to ordinary visitors: a trade building, badge required, buyers only. That has loosened. The lower floors are open on weekdays now, and the riverfront in front of you was rebuilt as public space.

Now look at the river face itself, that two-and-a-half-acre wall of stone. After dark, in season, it becomes a screen: dozens of projectors throw contemporary artwork across the whole thing, and it's billed as the largest permanent digital art projection anywhere. The viewing spot is the riverwalk jetty roughly between Wells and Franklin, and the program changes through the year.

Which is a strange second act. The biggest building on earth spent its first life as a machine for moving furniture, and spends its evenings now being famous for its surface.
