ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 12: The Michigan Avenue Streetwall — viewed from Grant Park
T1 Essentials | Clean version

---

Turn your back on the lake and you'll see what makes this street unlike any other main street in America. One side of it isn't there.

You're in Grant Park, west of Columbus Drive, on grass or a path, looking back at Michigan Avenue. To the west, a continuous cliff of buildings — different decades, different heights, wildly different intentions, all of them holding one straight line at the sidewalk. To the east, nothing but park, and then the lake. Every other great commercial street in this country has two walls. This one has one.

Follow the line south.

Notice that the buildings never step forward or back. That isn't taste. It's law. In 1836, canal commissioners selling off lots wrote a note on a map: the ground east of Michigan Avenue was to remain public, forever open, clear and free of any buildings or other obstructions whatever. It was a sales pitch — the lots fetched more with a guaranteed view — and it became one of the most consequential sentences ever written about an American city.

Worth knowing that the ground under your feet isn't original either. Michigan Avenue was the shoreline. Everything east of it is made land, filled in over decades with whatever the city had to hand, a great deal of it rubble. Chicago moved the lake in order to build the park that the law says can't be built on.

The promise held because somebody made it hold. Starting in 1890, Aaron Montgomery Ward, who ran a mail-order empire from a building on this street, sued the city again and again to keep structures out of the park. He won four times at the Illinois Supreme Court. He blocked a museum, a library, an armory, a circus. The city largely resented him for it — obstructionist was among the politer words — and he kept at it for twenty years.

[beat]

So the view in front of you isn't a design achievement. It's a legal one. Every open foot between you and that wall of stone exists because a retailer with very good lawyers refused to let Chicago sell its own front yard. The wall itself is protected now, too, as a landmark district — the line holds going forward as well as back.

His name is on almost none of it. The park you're standing in belongs to somebody else entirely.
