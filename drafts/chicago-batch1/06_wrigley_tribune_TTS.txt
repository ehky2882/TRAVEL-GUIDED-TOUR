Two buildings, facing each other across Michigan Avenue, having an argument.

You're at the north end of the doo sah bluh Bridge, on the plaza between them. West side: a white tower with a clock, bright to the point of glowing. East side: a gray Gothic slab with a crown of stone buttresses at the top, like a cathedral that took a wrong turn.

Wrigley first.

William Wrigley Junior made a fortune in chewing gum and built this between nineteen twenty and nineteen twenty-four, when there were no major office buildings north of the river at all. The bridge you just crossed wasn't finished when they broke ground. He bet the avenue would happen, and it did. The skin is terra kot uh in graded shades of white, palest at the top, and it's been floodlit at night since it opened — a building designed, quite deliberately, to be an advertisement.

Across the street, the Tribune ran an international competition for its headquarters in nineteen twenty-two, with a large cash prize. More than two hundred entries came in from around the world. Howells and Hood won with the Gothic tower you're looking at. Eliel Saarinen came second — and Saarinen's losing entry, a clean stepped shaft with no historical costume at all, went on to shape American skyscrapers far more than the building that beat it.

Now walk to the base of the Tribune and look at the wall. Embedded in the stone at eye level are around a hundred and fifty fragments, labeled: the Great Wall, the Parthenon, Westminster Abbey, the Taj Mahal, the Alamo, the Berlin Wall. Correspondents collected them and mailed them home. You can put your hand on them.

So: one company sells gum, dresses as a wedding cake, lights itself up, and is completely honest about wanting your attention. The other sells news, dresses as a cathedral, and physically bolts pieces of the world's monuments onto itself to borrow their seriousness.

The newspaper moved out in twenty eighteen. The tower is apartments now. The stones stayed exactly where they were.
