ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 02: Chicago Riverwalk at DuSable Bridge — the river reversal vantage
T1 Essentials | Clean version

---

In 1900, Chicago decided its river was running the wrong way, and changed it.

You're on the DuSable Bridge, where Michigan Avenue crosses the Chicago River, or on the Riverwalk just below it. Two road decks, four stone towers at the corners, green water underneath moving west, inland, away from the lake.

Face the water.

That direction is the engineering. Chicago drank from Lake Michigan and emptied its sewage into this river, and this river ran into the lake. The result was typhoid, repeatedly. Rather than move the city or the lake, engineers dug a canal southwest and dropped the river's level until it gave up and flowed backward — away from Lake Michigan, down toward the Illinois River and eventually the Mississippi. St. Louis sued. The earth-moving methods worked out on that canal were later carried to Panama.

Now the bridge. It opened in 1920, the first of its kind: two decks, two leaves, each leaf weighing about three thousand four hundred tons and balanced so precisely that two modest motors can raise it. In the twenties it opened thousands of times a year for shipping. Now it opens a few dozen.

The four corner towers carry stone reliefs of scenes from the city's founding. One of them, on the northeast, celebrates the recovery from the fire. The one on the southwest depicts fighting at Fort Dearborn, which stood at this bridge's south end — carved in the nineteen twenties by sculptors who treated the removal of the Potawatomi from this land as an adventure story. It wasn't. The Treaty of Chicago of 1833 moved them west, and the ground you're standing on is the result.

The north end sits near the homestead of Jean Baptiste Point DuSable, a Black trader who was this place's first permanent settler from outside the Native nations. The bridge took his name in 2010, ninety years after it opened.

[beat]

That southwest tower is a small museum in the warm months — five floors up from the gear room, if it's open when you pass.

A city that answers a public health crisis by reversing a river is a city that has decided geography is negotiable. Everything Chicago has done since follows from that.
