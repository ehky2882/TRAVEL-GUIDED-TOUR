ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 15: Monadnock Building — the last of the load-bearing giants
T2 Popular | Clean version

---

Put your hand flat on the brick. That wall is about six feet thick where you're touching it.

You're on Jackson Boulevard, at the base of a long, dark, purple-brown building that runs the full block south toward Van Buren. No columns, no carving, no cornice worth the name — just brick, flaring slightly outward at the bottom and again at the top, with bay windows rippling down the whole length of it. Walk a few steps south along the wall while you listen.

Begin at the bottom.

Six feet of solid masonry at the base, tapering to roughly eighteen inches by the sixteenth floor. There is no frame inside the north half of this building. The brick is the structure — the wall is doing all the work, the way walls had done it for six thousand years. It's generally called the tallest load-bearing masonry office building ever built, and that's less a boast than a ceiling. Any taller and the ground floor would have been all wall and no room.

Burnham and Root designed that half, finished in 1891. The Boston investors paying for it insisted on no ornament at all, which frustrated Root until it didn't; what he came back with is closer to an Egyptian pylon than a Victorian office block. Louis Sullivan later described it as a cliff of brickwork, sheer and stark. Root died that January, forty-one years old, with the building still going up.

Now keep walking south and watch the brick. Two years later the other half arrived — Holabird and Roche, same color, same profile, and behind that face, a steel skeleton carrying everything. The wall on this end is a coat, not a structure.

[beat]

So on one block, in two years, the wall stopped holding buildings up. Every tall building anywhere since sits on the south side of that line.

One last thing. The doorways here step down below the sidewalk. It's been settling into the mud since the day it opened, and the city has simply built up around it.
