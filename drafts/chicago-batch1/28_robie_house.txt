CHICAGO — STOP 28
ROBIE HOUSE — HYDE PARK
Single-stop · T2 · Clean version

---

There is no front door on the front of this house.

You should be at the corner of Woodlawn Avenue and Fifty-Eighth Street, the long side of the house running away from you down Woodlawn. Brick in narrow courses, limestone sills carrying the whole length, and a roof that pushes out past the wall about twenty feet with nothing underneath holding it up. Bands of art glass instead of separate windows. What you won't find, from anywhere on this sidewalk, is a way in.

Start with the roofline.

Frank Lloyd Wright drew this in 1908 and it was finished in 1910, and the eaves are the entire argument. Everything is pulled sideways, nothing rises, and the roof reaches twenty feet past its own support — which in 1910 read less as elegant than as impossible, on a street of tall narrow houses that all did the opposite.

Frederick Robie was twenty-eight, an engineer who made bicycle and motorcycle parts. His requirements were specific: fireproof, no boxed-off rooms, a garage, a playroom for the children, and — his words — no conglomeration of doo-dads. He also wanted to be able to look out at the neighborhood without the neighborhood looking back in.

The last requirement is why the house is shaped the way it is. The living floor sits raised a level, the glass runs high, the eaves cut every sightline from the street, and the entrance is folded around the side behind a wall. The horizontal line that architects copied for the next fifty years began as one man's privacy problem, solved exactly.

Robie lived here about a year. His father died, the business went with him, and he sold. By 1926 a theological seminary owned it and was using it as a dormitory, and twice — in 1941, then again in 1957 — announced plans to pull it down. Wright turned up to fight for it both times. It is the only one of his buildings he ever did that for.

*[beat]*

The interior has since been taken back to its 1910 condition, and guided tours run most days in small groups, booked ahead. The sidewalk, though, is where the argument actually happens; this thing was built to be seen from exactly where you're standing. Walk the length of it down Woodlawn. You'll get everything Wright wanted you to have and nothing Robie didn't.
