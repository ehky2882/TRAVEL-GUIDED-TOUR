CHICAGO — STOP 25
PILSEN — EIGHTEENTH STREET & THE MURALS
Single-stop · T2 · Clean version

---

Eighteenth Street runs west about two miles, and for most of it the buildings are a century older than the paint on them.

You should be on Eighteenth somewhere between Halsted and Ashland, storefronts on both sides. Above the ground floor: brick, carved limestone over the windows, cornices with scrolls and steps and small pediments. Lower down — side walls, gaps between buildings, under the viaducts — paint. Almost everything up high is Czech. Almost everything painted is Mexican.

So.

The name came from a restaurant. Czechs arrived in numbers after the fire of 1871, when the factories were hiring, and one of them opened a place called At the City of Plzeň, after the Bohemian city he had left. Those cornices are what Chicago calls Bohemian Baroque — Old World ornament executed in Illinois brick, by people building themselves a version of somewhere else.

At Eighteenth and Allport, heavy stone, an arched entrance, a carved face peering out of the foliage above the door. Thalia Hall, 1892, put up by a saloonkeeper named John Dusek who wanted his neighbors to have theater in their own language; the hall inside was modeled on the opera house in Prague. Closed in the 1960s, untouched for fifty years, a working music venue again now.

Mexican families began arriving in the fifties and sixties, many pushed out of the Near West Side when the University of Illinois built its campus there. The murals started in the late sixties. At the Eighteenth Street station, a schoolteacher named Francisco Mendoza spent years painting the platforms and stairwells with his students. In 2019 a crew removing graffiti painted over part of the work; after the neighborhood objected, the transit authority brought in a Pilsen muralist, Roberto Valadez, approved by Mendoza's family, and he put it back.

*[beat]*

Repainting is the normal life cycle of a mural here, not the exception. In 2018 the city proposed protecting the district by law — the buildings, and, in a first for Chicago, the murals too. Preservationists called it the only real defense against demolition; residents said it would cost them more than they could carry. In 2020 the council committee voted it down, unanimously. So the paint survives the way it always has: somebody redoes it. Whatever you're standing in front of is the current version; the wall will outlast it. Give it a minute anyway. The people who painted it knew that too.
