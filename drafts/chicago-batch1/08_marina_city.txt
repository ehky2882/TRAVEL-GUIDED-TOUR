ATLAS — CHICAGO
Stop 08: Marina City — corncob towers, river vantage
T1 Essentials | Clean version

---

The bottom nineteen floors are a parking garage, and nobody tried to hide it.

You're on the river, somewhere along Wacker Drive or the Riverwalk with a clear view north across the water at two round concrete towers. Look at the lower third of either one. That open spiral with cars visible on it is exactly what it appears to be: a ramp, winding up nineteen stories, with no wall in front of it.

Count the floors.

Above the cars, the apartments start. Each one is a wedge, radiating out from a central concrete core like a slice of pie, opening onto a curved balcony — which is what gives the towers their scalloped edge and their nickname. Almost nothing inside is a right angle. Sixty-five floors, just under six hundred feet, and when they were finished they were both the tallest apartment buildings and the tallest reinforced concrete structures anywhere.

Here's the part worth carrying away. This was paid for, in large part, by a union of building janitors and elevator operators. They came to the architect, Bertrand Goldberg, wanting a new headquarters. He talked their president out of it — argued that if downtown Chicago emptied out at five o'clock and everyone moved to the suburbs, the high-rises their members worked in would go dark and the jobs would go with them. Build apartments instead, he said. Keep people downtown.

So a janitors' union financed the tallest residential building in the world, on the theory that a living city is a working city.

[beat]

When the towers went up, the concrete was poured continuously into forms that climbed as they filled — the whole core rising a few feet a day without stopping. People came down to the river to watch it happen.

Goldberg had trained under Mies van der Rohe, and then spent his career arguing with him — curves instead of boxes, concrete instead of steel and glass, a building shaped like something grown rather than something assembled.

The theater at the base is a music club now. The ice rink is a restaurant. But look up at the balconies: chairs, plants, bicycles, someone's laundry. People still live there. That was the whole idea.
