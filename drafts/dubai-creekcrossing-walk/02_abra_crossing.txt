DUBAI W1 — THE CREEK CROSSING — 02 THE ABRA (walk version — creek payoff)

Step down onto the floating dock and take the next boat with space on the bench. There's no ticket, no timetable, and no ceremony — an abra leaves when it's full, which at this station is usually about a minute after it arrives.

You'll be sitting on a wooden bench that runs around a diesel engine, knee to knee with roughly twenty strangers. The fare is a dirham — sometimes two, depending on the boat — passed hand to hand down the bench to the driver once you're moving. The word abra comes from the Arabic for "to cross." Not to sail, not to voyage. To cross. The boat is named for the errand.

Now, while the engine settles into its note, look down the water. Both banks at once — the wharves and minarets of Deira on one side, Bur Dubai behind you, dhows moored in the distance. For most of this city's existence, what you can see from this bench was the entire city: two banks, and the water working between them. The pearl fleets left from here. The trade boats came home to here. Every fortune this coast made or lost for a century passed across this water in a hull.

[beat]

Here is the thing to understand mid-crossing, because you'll never understand it better than now. Everything Dubai has done since — the ports, the airline, the towers full of traders, the man-made islands — is a larger version of what you are doing at this exact moment: crossing water to reach the trade on the other side. The ports are this boat, scaled up. The airline is this boat with wings. The city never outgrew the creek. It just kept rebuilding it, bigger, in steel and concrete and flight paths, all over the world.

The boat is slowing now. That's Deira Old Souk Station ahead — step off carefully, the dock moves. The Spice Souk starts almost at the water's edge, and that's where we're headed next.
