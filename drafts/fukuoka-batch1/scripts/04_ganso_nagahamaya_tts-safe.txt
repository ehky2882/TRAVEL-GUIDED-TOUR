Gahnsoh Nahgah hahmah yah

Locals don't really read the menu here. They just tell you how firm they want their noodles.

The shop is on the ground floor of a building in Nahgah hahmah, in a district that grew up around the city's fish market. Look for the queue, the ticket machine just inside the door, and the long counter with the steam coming off the pots.

This is one of the founding shops of Nahgah hahmah ramen. It started in nineteen fifty two as a street stall and moved to this neighbourhood around nineteen fifty five, when the fish market did. The idea was to feed the people who worked there, quickly, at all hours, which is why the doors still open at six in the morning and don't close until gone one at night.

Start with the bowl. A thin, pale pork bone soup, lighter than you might expect, with very thin straight noodles that cook in seconds. Speed was the point.

Now, the ordering. You buy a ticket from the machine, and then you say how you want your noodles. Nahmah means barely boiled, still with real bite. Betah nahmah is nahmah with extra fat. And when you finish your noodles, you can ask for a second serving to drop into the soup you have left. That's kah eh dahmah, and this shop is widely held to be where it started. If you want the second lot firm as well, it's nahmah tahmah.

Here's the curious thing. You'll see shops nearby with very similar names. In two thousand and nine some long serving staff left and opened their own place across the road from the old shop, and the names have caused confusion ever since. This is the original. They share the same roots, but they're different shops.

It's cash only, so have some yen ready. Afterwards, if it's early enough, wander down towards the fish market. And come back after dark sometime: the Nahgah hahmah street stalls, where all of this began, were revived in twenty twenty three.
