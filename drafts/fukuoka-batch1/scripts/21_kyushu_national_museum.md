## Clean script

**Kyushu National Museum**

When this opened in 2005, it was the first new national museum in Japan for more than a century.

Come up out of the long, colour-lit tunnel and the moving walkways from Dazaifu Tenmangu, and stand facing the building. A huge, low curve of glass, with a blue roof that rolls across the top like a wave, and wooded hills behind it.

The architect was Kiyonori Kikutake, working with Kume Sekkei. Kikutake was one of the Metabolists, the group that reimagined Japanese cities in the sixties, and here he went for something softer: a flowing roof clad in blue titanium over glass walls with a double skin. It's built on seismic isolation, which won it an engineering award, so the treasures inside can ride out an earthquake.

Have a look at its scale. When it opened it had the largest floor area of any single building in Japan's national museum system. Before it, there were only three national museums, in Tokyo, Kyoto and Nara, and the newest of those dated from 1897.

The idea is what sets it apart. This museum sets out to explain how Japanese culture formed by looking at Asia, at the trade and contact that came across the sea to Kyushu first. Dazaifu, once the government outpost for this whole region, was where much of that arrived.

The hill it stands on belongs to Dazaifu Tenmangu, and the shrine's head priest was among the people calling for a museum here as long ago as 1893. It took more than a century.

[beat]

On the walk back down to the shrine, stop on the approach road to look at the coffee shop by Kengo Kuma, built from thousands of interlocking strips of timber. And if there's time, go and see the plum trees at Tenmangu itself.
