Kyooshoo National Museum

When this opened in two thousand and five, it was the first new national museum in Japan for more than a century.

Come up out of the long, colour lit tunnel and the moving walkways from Dahzye foo Tenmahn goo, and stand facing the building. A huge, low curve of glass, with a blue roof that rolls across the top like a wave, and wooded hills behind it.

The architect was Keeyohnohree Keekootahkay, working with Koomay Sekkay. Keekootahkay was one of the Metabolists, the group that reimagined Japanese cities in the sixties, and here he went for something softer: a flowing roof clad in blue titanium over glass walls with a double skin. It's built on seismic isolation, which won it an engineering award, so the treasures inside can ride out an earthquake.

Have a look at its scale. When it opened it had the largest floor area of any single building in Japan's national museum system. Before it, there were only three national museums, in Tokyo, Kyoto and Nara, and the newest of those dated from eighteen ninety seven.

The idea is what sets it apart. This museum sets out to explain how Japanese culture formed by looking at Asia, at the trade and contact that came across the sea to Kyooshoo first. Dahzye foo, once the government outpost for this whole region, was where much of that arrived.

The hill it stands on belongs to Dahzye foo Tenmahn goo, and the shrine's head priest was among the people calling for a museum here as long ago as eighteen ninety three. It took more than a century.

On the walk back down to the shrine, stop on the approach road to look at the coffee shop by Kengoh Koomah, built from thousands of interlocking strips of timber. And if there's time, go and see the plum trees at Tenmahn goo itself.
