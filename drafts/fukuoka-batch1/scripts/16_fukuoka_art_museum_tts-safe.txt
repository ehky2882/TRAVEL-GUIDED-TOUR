Those tiled walls weren't stuck on afterwards. The tiles were set into the concrete as it was poured.

Stand on the broad approach that leads up from the path round Oh hoh ree Park, with the lake behind you and the museum ahead: long, low, tiled walls and broad terraces, with a café looking out over the water near the entrance.

The building is by Kooneeoh My kahwah, one of the founders of modern architecture in Japan, who had worked for Le Corbusier in Paris. It opened in November nineteen seventy nine. Have a look at the walls close up. My kahwah used a technique of tiles cast into the concrete, and around them, concrete that has been chipped back by hand to a rough surface. It's a building made to weather well, and after forty odd years it has.

The museum closed in twenty sixteen for a long renovation and reopened in March twenty nineteen. The architecture was left alone. What changed was the way in: the new approach you're standing on opens the museum straight onto the park, so it no longer turns its back on the people walking the lake.

Inside are about sixteen thousand works. Highlights include Dahlee's Madonna of Port Lyeegaht, painted in nineteen fifty, works by Meeroh, Shagahl and Warhol, a Baskeeah, and a pumpkin by Yah yoy Koosahmah from nineteen ninety four. There's also a fine collection of older Japanese art, including Buddhist sculpture and tea ceremony treasures.

And here's the thing about the renovation. The easy option would have been a glass extension and a new entrance. Instead, the city kept My kahwah's building and simply turned it to face the park.

When you go in, look for the small My kahwah memorial space, with models of the building and the original chairs used since nineteen seventy nine. Afterwards, walk the two kilometre path round Oh hoh ree Park, or cross the moat to the ruins of Fookoo oka Castle.
