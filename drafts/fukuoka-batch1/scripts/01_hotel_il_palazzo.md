## Clean script

**Hotel Il Palazzo**

It's 1989, Japan is at the height of its bubble years, and a hotel company in Fukuoka hands its new building to an Italian who has never built anything in this country.

Stand in the open square in front of the hotel, on the Haruyoshi side of the Naka River, with the street behind you and the building filling your view. A long wall of deep red stone, cut into bays by tall columns, with bands of pale green metal running across it. No big glass lobby, no name in lights.

Start with that red. It's travertine, a marbled stone from Iran, and the green bands are copper lintels that have been left to weather to that soft verdigris. The architect was Aldo Rossi, who won the Pritzker Prize the year after this opened, and he borrowed from Venetian palaces, from the boulevards of Paris and from Nishi Hongan-ji, a temple in Kyoto, to make a front that simply stands still and lets the city come to it.

The square is part of the idea. Rossi wanted to show architecture as a small city, and he said the Palazzo could not be built upon nothing, that its beauty depended on the everyday life around it. The owners now talk about holding markets out here, which would have pleased him.

The man who brought him to Fukuoka was the designer Shigeru Uchida, who did the interiors, and the bars were handed to Ettore Sottsass, Gaetano Pesce and Shiro Kuramata. It was billed as Japan's first design hotel.

And here's the thing. Buildings from those years usually get gutted or pulled down. This one was given a two-year renovation by Uchida's own studio, years after his death, and reopened in October 2023 with its original 1989 logo brought back.

[beat]

If you fancy a look inside, the lounge is downstairs in what was once the disco, built round Uchida's water sculpture, Dancing Water, and after nine in the evening it opens as a bar to anyone. On your way in, look for a tall red clock called Dear Morris. It's named after Morris Adjmi, Rossi's assistant, who saw the building through on site.
