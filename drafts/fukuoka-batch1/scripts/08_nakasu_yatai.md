## Clean script

**Nakasu Yatai**

By morning, there'll be nothing here at all. That's rather the point of a yatai.

You should be on the riverside walkway on the Nakasu side of the Naka River, with the water to one side and a row of small wooden stalls along the pavement, each with a curtain across the front, a counter, a handful of stools, and steam rising from behind the cloth.

These are yatai, Fukuoka's street food stalls. The tradition goes back to around 1945, when temporary stalls appeared in the chaos after the war. By the 1960s, stalls taking over the pavements had become a public argument, and in 2013 the city passed an ordinance to settle it. A yatai, in the law's words, is a stall set up for a fixed time in a fixed place, and it must leave at least two metres of pavement clear for everyone else.

So every evening the stalls are wheeled in and assembled, and every night they're taken away again.

Have a look at what's cooking. Raw food isn't allowed, so it's all hot: ramen, oden simmering in its broth, grilled skewers, rolled omelette with mentaiko. People drink shochu or beer. And you will be sitting elbow to elbow with strangers, which is the whole idea.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how unusual this is. Plenty of cities have cleared their street stalls away. Fukuoka chose instead to write rules that let them carry on, because the city decided the stalls were part of what it is.

[beat]

Don't settle at the first stall you see. The local way is to have a dish and a drink at one, then move on to the next. And if you walk south along the river towards Haruyoshi Bridge, there's a stall there known for its tiny gyoza.
