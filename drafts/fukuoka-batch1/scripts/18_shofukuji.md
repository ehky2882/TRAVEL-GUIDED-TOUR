## Clean script

**Shofukuji**

Japan's Zen Buddhism has many homes, but this was the first.

Stand at the outer gate and look straight ahead. A long, straight path runs through the precinct, with the inner gate, the Buddha hall and the abbot's quarters lined up one behind the other.

The temple was founded in 1195 by the monk Eisai, who had just come back from studying in Song dynasty China. The shogun, Minamoto no Yoritomo, gave him the land, and the temple was finished in 1204. The temple carries an inscription naming it the first Zen temple in Japan, and tradition says the Emperor Go-Toba wrote it himself.

Have a look at that straight line. It's the classic Zen layout, every hall on one axis, so walking in is a kind of gradual approach. Most of the halls you see were rebuilt in the Edo period, but the plan is Eisai's.

Eisai is also remembered for bringing tea seeds back from China, and there's a tea bush in the grounds said to be the origin of Japanese tea. Much later, the painter-monk Sengai was abbot here, and his playful brush drawings made the temple famous.

Here's what I love about this place. It's in the middle of a city of more than a million and a half people, a few minutes from Hakata Station, and it's usually quiet. The whole precinct is a national historic site, and nobody has tried to turn it into an attraction.

[beat]

Walk out through the side lanes into the temple quarter around it. Within ten minutes you'll pass several more old temples, including Tochoji with its great wooden Buddha, and the narrow streets of Gokusho-machi, where old Hakata survives best.
