Hahkahtah Eessoh

Fookoo oka people have a name for the soup here. They call it the pork bone cappuccino.

You're on a side street a few minutes' walk east of Hahkahtah Station, among the office blocks. The shop is the one with the queue outside. Inside, a ten seat counter and a handful of tables, twenty one seats in all.

It opened in November twenty twelve, which is young by local standards, and it has been in the national ramen guides every year since twenty twenty. The reason is the soup. The kitchen keeps three big pots going, mixing different cuts of pork bone and simmering them until the bones break apart. Have a look at the surface of your bowl when it arrives: a fine layer of foam. Nobody whips it. It comes from the fat and the air churning together during that long, hard boil, and the shop treats it as a sign that the work was done by hand.

The rest is simple, the way Hahkahtah ramen is meant to be. Very fine straight noodles. Soy sauce from Fookoo oka, spring onions from Eetoh sheemah, just along the coast. If you're hungry, order the special ramen, with thick slices of pork, a soft boiled egg and wood ear mushroom.

What's worth noticing is how much a dozen years of foam has changed the conversation. For most of its history, Hahkahtah ramen was fast, light and cheap, a bowl for fish market workers and late night drinkers. This is richer and more deliberate. It's still the same family of soup. It just takes itself a little more seriously.

Bring cash, and expect to wait at lunchtime. Afterwards, walk back west to Hahkahtah Station and through it to the old town on the other side. Kooshee dah Shrine and the temple quarter are about twenty minutes on foot, if you fancy walking it off.
