It opened in two thousand and five, and it's run by two cousins, Antonio and Toh mahs Kah sah blahn kah, both from families long in the city's hospitality trade. The cooking is what they call traditional Andalusian: fried fish, octopus, scrambled eggs with wild mushrooms, the things this city has always eaten at a bar.

And then there's the tor tee yah al whisky. Spanish potato omelette is the most ordinary dish in the country, made in every home. Here it's served in a sauce made with whisky, and it has become the house signature, copied by other kitchens since. Order it, and something fried to go with it. The poon tee yee tahs, tiny fried squid with garlic and baby broad beans, are a good choice.

What makes a place like this work is the counter. Have a look at it: the regulars standing, the plates going over the top, the orders called out across the room. It's quick, sociable and loud. You don't come for the room; you come for the room's company.

That's the interesting bit. Nobody would put this bar in an architecture guide for its building. But an architect will tell you that a good bar counter is one of the best designed public spaces in Spain. It gets strangers talking.
