Plaza del Salvador

Seville's second church stands on the site of its first great mosque. Part of the minaret is still there.

You're in the Plaza del Salvador, in the middle of the old city, between the Plaza de la Encarnación and the cathedral. On one side of the square is the huge church front of the Iglesia Colegial del Salvador, with its giant columns and its dome. In front of it, the square is full of people standing about with drinks.

The story starts in eight twenty-nine. That's when the mosque of Ibn Adabbás was built here, and for more than three hundred and fifty years it was the main mosque of the city. Only in eleven eighty-two did the Almohads open a bigger one, where the cathedral now stands.

After the Christian conquest the mosque became a church. By the sixteen seventies it was falling apart. It was pulled down, and in sixteen seventy-nine work began on the church you see now. José Granados de la Barrera designed the façades, and Leonardo de Figueroa finished the job in seventeen twelve, closing the vaults and raising the dome.

Look for the bell tower, round the side of the church. At its base, the stonework is different. That's the bottom of the old minaret. And the courtyard behind the church, the Patio de los Naranjos, the Courtyard of the Orange Trees, was the mosque's courtyard, where the faithful once washed before prayer.

Inside, the main altarpiece by Cayetano de Acosta is an explosion of gilded carving from the seventeen seventies. And in the side chapels are two of the most important images of Seville's Holy Week: the Cristo del Amor, carved by Juan de Mesa in sixteen twenty, and Jesús de la Pasión, attributed to Juan Martínez Montañés.

And here's the thing. The square in front works the way a square in Seville should. At two o'clock on a weekday, the steps are crowded with people having a beer before lunch, as though the great church were simply the largest bar front in town.

Before you go, walk round to Calle Córdoba and find the entrance to the Patio de los Naranjos. Stand among the orange trees, look up at the base of the tower, and you're in the courtyard of the ninth-century mosque.
