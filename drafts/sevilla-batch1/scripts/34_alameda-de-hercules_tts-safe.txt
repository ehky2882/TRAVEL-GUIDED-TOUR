Ah lah meh dah deh Air koo less

This is said to be the oldest public garden in Europe. It started life as a swamp.

You're on the Ah lah meh dah deh Air koo less, a long, wide, tree lined promenade in the north of the old city. At the southern end you should see two tall stone columns with statues on top. At the northern end, two more, with lions. In between are caffays, children, dogs and benches.

Long ago, a branch of the Gwah dahl kee veer ran through here. In the thirteen eighties it was cut off, and the old riverbed became a marshy lagoon. In fifteen seventy four the city's governor, the Count of Bah rah hahs, drained it, laid out water channels and fountains, and planted rows of white poplars, ah lah mohs, which is where the word ah lah meh dah comes from. It was the first public garden of its kind in Spain, and very possibly in Europe.

Now have a look at the southern columns. They're Roman, from a temple that stood on Kah yeh Mar moh less, a few streets away. On top stand Hercules, the legendary founder of Seville, and Julius Caesar, who rebuilt it. There was meant to be a third column, but it broke during the move. The northern pair are copies, added later, and their lions in the eighteenth century.

The Ah lah meh dah has had a rough ride. After the Civil War this became one of the poorest and roughest parts of Seville. Between two thousand and six and two thousand and eight it was rebuilt and largely closed to traffic, and the neighbourhood around it has been transformed.

And here's the thing. Nobody designed the Ah lah meh dah to be fashionable. It was a drained swamp with some trees. Four and a half centuries later, it's where young Seville comes in the evening, and it's still, above all, a place for doing nothing very much.

Walk south east for about fifteen minutes to Kah yeh Mar moh less. Three more of those Roman columns are still standing there, in a sunken space at the side of the street, just where the temple stood. They are the sisters of the two at the end of the Ah lah meh dah.
