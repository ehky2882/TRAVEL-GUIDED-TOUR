## Script (clean)

From the outside, this church looks rather grand. Inside, it looks as though it might take off.

You're on Calle San Luis, a long, straight street in the north of the old city. In front of you is a tall Baroque church front of brick and stone, with twisting columns framing a central window, and two towers flanking a dome. That's San Luis de los Franceses.

It was built between sixteen ninety-nine and seventeen thirty-one as the church of a Jesuit novitiate, a house for training young priests, and it's attributed to Leonardo de Figueroa, the architect who shaped so much of Baroque Seville. The Jesuits were expelled from Spain in seventeen sixty-seven, and the building has had a long, chequered life since. It belongs today to the provincial government, which has restored it.

Go inside and stand in the middle. The plan is a Greek cross, four equal arms around a round central space, and everything is pulling your eye upwards to the dome. It's about thirteen and a half metres across, and it's covered in frescoes attributed to Lucas Valdés, finished around seventeen nineteen.

Now look at the altarpieces. There are seven of them, attributed to Pedro Duque Cornejo, and they glitter, because the main one is set with mirrors and blue glass. Twisting columns climb all around the room. In candlelight, it must have looked like being inside a jewel box.

Underneath, there's a crypt, opened to visitors after excavations between twenty ten and twenty thirteen uncovered around three hundred burials.

And here's the thing. This church was built to train young men to go out and win the world for the faith. It's a building designed to give you a vision. You don't have to share the faith to feel the force of it.

When you come out, walk north up Calle San Luis. In about ten minutes you'll reach the old city wall and the Arco de la Macarena, and beside it the basilica of the Macarena, home to the most beloved image of the Virgin in Seville.
