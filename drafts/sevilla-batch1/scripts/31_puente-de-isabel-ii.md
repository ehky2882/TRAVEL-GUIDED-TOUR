## Script (clean)

For seven hundred years, the way across the river here was a line of boats chained together.

You're on the Puente de Isabel II, which everyone in Seville calls the Puente de Triana. It's the iron bridge with a row of iron rings set into its arches, between the city centre and Triana. Stand in the middle, on the downstream side, facing the Torre del Oro.

Before this bridge, the crossing was a pontoon bridge, a string of boats roped and chained side by side with a walkway laid across them. The Almohads built it in the eleven seventies, and with endless repairs it lasted until the eighteen fifties. Imagine the river in flood, and that bridge of boats straining at its chains.

This was the first permanent bridge in Seville. Work began in eighteen forty-five, and it opened in eighteen fifty-two, under Queen Isabel the Second, hence the name. It was designed by two French engineers, Gustave Steinacher and Ferdinand Bernadet, and they took as their model the Pont du Carrousel in Paris, which crosses the Seine by the Louvre. The Guadalquivir is wider than the Seine here, so the Seville version is longer.

Have a look over the side at the arches and those iron rings. They look like the structure, and once they were. Since a rebuilding in the nineteen seventies, the deck has been carried on hidden steel girders, and the rings and arches are there out of affection, as decoration.

And here's the thing. Sevillians love this bridge in a way they don't love any of the newer, bigger crossings. It's not about engineering. It's because it joins the city to Triana, two places that have always insisted they're different.

*[beat]*

At the Triana end of the bridge is a small brick and tile chapel with a little tower, the Capillita del Carmen. Aníbal González, the architect of the Plaza de España, finished it in nineteen twenty-eight. People in Triana call it the mechero, the cigarette lighter. Have a look, and you'll see why.
