# 34 · Alameda de Hércules

## Script (clean)

This is said to be the oldest public garden in Europe. It started life as a swamp.

You're on the Alameda de Hércules, a long, wide, tree-lined promenade in the north of the old city. At the southern end you should see two tall stone columns with statues on top. At the northern end, two more, with lions. In between are cafés, children, dogs and benches.

Long ago, a branch of the Guadalquivir ran through here. In the thirteen eighties it was cut off, and the old riverbed became a marshy lagoon. In fifteen seventy-four the city's governor, the Count of Barajas, drained it, laid out water channels and fountains, and planted rows of white poplars, álamos, which is where the word alameda comes from. It was the first public garden of its kind in Spain, and very possibly in Europe.

Now have a look at the southern columns. They're Roman, from a temple that stood on Calle Mármoles, a few streets away. On top stand Hercules, the legendary founder of Seville, and Julius Caesar, who rebuilt it. There was meant to be a third column, but it broke during the move. The northern pair are copies, added later, and their lions in the eighteenth century.

The Alameda has had a rough ride. After the Civil War this became one of the poorest and roughest parts of Seville. Between two thousand and six and two thousand and eight it was rebuilt and largely closed to traffic, and the neighbourhood around it has been transformed.

And here's the thing. Nobody designed the Alameda to be fashionable. It was a drained swamp with some trees. Four and a half centuries later, it's where young Seville comes in the evening, and it's still, above all, a place for doing nothing very much.

*[beat]*

Walk south-east for about fifteen minutes to Calle Mármoles. Three more of those Roman columns are still standing there, in a sunken space at the side of the street, just where the temple stood. They are the sisters of the two at the end of the Alameda.
