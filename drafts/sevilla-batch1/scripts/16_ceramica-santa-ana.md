## Script (clean)

The front of this shop is its own advertisement, and it's made of the thing it sells.

You're on Calle San Jorge in Triana, just over the Puente de Triana, near the corner with the Plaza del Altozano. Cerámica Santa Ana is the shop whose whole front is covered in painted tiles, lettered with its own name. You can't really miss it.

Santa Ana has been selling the hand-painted work of Triana's workshops since around eighteen seventy. For most of its life it was not only a shop but a factory, one of dozens in this neighbourhood, with kilns and tall chimneys. Seville's tile tradition runs back to Roman times, and the clay came from the riverbanks nearby. Old photographs of Triana from the fifties and sixties show a skyline of factory chimneys.

Have a look at the façade properly. This is how Seville has always advertised: not with paint or neon, but with ceramic. Walk round the city and you'll find tiled shop signs, street names and old advertisements for cars and watches, some of them a hundred years old and still bright. Glazed tile does not fade in the sun.

Inside, the shop is packed with plates, tiles and jugs, much of it in patterns that have been made in Triana for generations.

And here's the thing. The factory itself stopped working, but it didn't disappear. In twenty fourteen the old Santa Ana works became a museum, the Centro Cerámica Triana, which keeps the kilns where they stood. The shop carried on next door. So in one corner of Triana you can buy the tiles and see where they were fired.

*[beat]*

Walk round the corner to the museum's entrance on Calle Antillano Campos. Downstairs are the old kilns. Upstairs, the collection runs from Moorish times to the nineteen fifties, and it's the best introduction to the tiles you'll see all over this city.
