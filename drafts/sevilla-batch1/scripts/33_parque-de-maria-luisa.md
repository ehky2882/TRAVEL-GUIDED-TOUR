## Script (clean)

This park was a gift from a princess, and it was redesigned by a Frenchman who looked to the Moorish gardens of Andalusia.

You're inside the Parque de María Luisa, south of the old city, between the river and the Plaza de España. You should be on one of the shady avenues, with tall trees overhead, tiled benches, low fountains and long, straight channels of water running between the paths.

In eighteen ninety-three, the Infanta María Luisa Fernanda, Duchess of Montpensier, gave the gardens of her palace of San Telmo to the city for public use. The park is named after her. From nineteen eleven, the French landscape designer Jean-Claude Nicolas Forestier reshaped it, and a few years later Aníbal González began building the pavilions of the nineteen twenty-nine exposition among its trees.

Have a look at the water. Forestier took his ideas from the gardens of the Alhambra and the Alcázar: water running in narrow tiled channels, little pools, fountains that bubble rather than spout. In this climate, water and shade are luxuries, and he arranged them like jewellery.

Then find the Glorieta de Bécquer, in the north of the park, a memorial to the Romantic poet, made in nineteen eleven by Lorenzo Coullaut-Valera. Under a great old tree, a bust of the poet sits above three marble women, often read as the stages of love: love hoped for, love enjoyed and love lost. Bécquer wrote about all three.

And here's the thing. This is not a park in the English sense, a lawn with trees in it. It's a series of outdoor rooms, each with its own walls of hedges, its own floor of tiles and its own fountain. It's a garden built for a hot city.

*[beat]*

Walk south through the park to the Plaza de América, at its far end. Its pavilions were built for the fair of nineteen twenty-nine, and its square is full of white doves. Bring something for them, or prepare to be thoroughly inspected.
