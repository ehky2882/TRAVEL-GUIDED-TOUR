## Script (clean)

The name comes from a poem. Ask almost anyone in Seville to finish the first line, and they probably can.

Calle Antillano Campos, in Triana, on the far side of the river, a few steps from the Triana market. Look for a small bar with its walls covered in tiles. Inside, the counter runs along one side; upstairs there are a few more tables.

Las Golondrinas means "the swallows". It opened in nineteen sixty-two and it's named after a rhyme by Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer, the Sevillian Romantic poet, which begins with the dark swallows coming back to hang their nests on your balcony. It's one of the best-known poems in the Spanish language. It's a poem about love that won't return, which is a melancholy thing to name a bar after, and I find that rather lovely.

Look at the walls, because they're the reason this bar belongs in a guide like this. Tiles, painted plates, lines of verse: this is Triana, the potters' quarter, and for centuries the ceramics for half of Seville came out of the workshops round these streets. The bar is wearing the neighbourhood's own craft.

Upstairs, the small wooden tables and rush-seated chairs are the kind you'd find in the casetas, the family tents of the April Fair.

Now the food. You come for two things. The punta de solomillo, little pieces of pork tenderloin seared on the plancha, and the champiñones, grilled mushrooms with garlic, parsley and a garlicky aioli, served in a little dish shaped like a boat. If you hear a hammering in the kitchen, that's your pork being prepared.

*[beat]*

When you've eaten, walk a few doors down the same street to the Centro Cerámica Triana, in an old tile factory. Its kilns are still there, and it's the best place to understand those walls you've just been sitting beside.
