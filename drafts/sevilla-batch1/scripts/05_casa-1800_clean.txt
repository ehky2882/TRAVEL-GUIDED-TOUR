Casa 1800

The name says eighteen hundred. The house was actually finished in eighteen sixty-four. Nobody here seems to mind.

You're on Calle Rodrigo Caro, a short, shady street in Santa Cruz, a couple of minutes from the cathedral. Look for a three-storey townhouse with iron balconies and a heavy wooden door. That's Casa 1800.

The street is named after Rodrigo Caro, a seventeenth-century Sevillian poet and antiquarian, best remembered for a melancholy poem about the ruins of the Roman city of Itálica, just outside Seville. It's a fitting address for a house that has been carefully put back together.

The building was a palace-house over three floors, and for a time it was the home of a mayor of Seville, José María Piñar y Miura. When it was restored, over two years, the builders kept what they could: the original balconies, the carpentry, the ceilings and the walls.

Go in and look up. The house is built round a central patio that drops light down through all three floors, and that's the oldest air-conditioning in Seville. In summer the shaded patio holds the cool air of the night; the rooms round it draw on it all day.

At the top there's the roof terrace, with a small pool, and the view is the point. From up there you can see the Giralda, the bell towers of Santa Cruz, the gardens of the Alcázar and, on a clear day, the towers of the Plaza de España.

What strikes me here is how ordinary this house once was. Not a ducal palace, just a good, solid nineteenth-century family home of the kind that lines every street of this quarter. That's what makes it worth noticing.

Before you go, follow the street to its end, into the Plaza de Doña Elvira. It's a small square of orange trees, tiled benches and a fountain, and it's one of the loveliest places in the city to sit down for five minutes.
