Corral del Ray

The street is so narrow here that the hotel lives on both sides of it.

You're on Kah yeh Corral del Ray, a cobbled lane in the Alfalfa quarter, a few minutes north east of the cathedral. Look for the plain whitewashed fronts with heavy wooden doors and iron grilles at the windows. The hotel is in two old houses that face each other across the lane.

The main house is a seventeenth century kah sah pah lah see oh, which is the Sevillian name for a town palace. From the street you'd never guess. That's the point of the type. Seville's grand houses keep their faces shut and their hearts open, and everything happens round a patio inside.

Here the patio runs the full height of the house, three floors open to the sky, with old arches at the bottom and carved timber ceilings in the galleries. Look for the doors: some are medieval, Moo deh har work, the joinery made by craftsmen trained in the Islamic tradition. The whole thing feels closer to a Moroccan riad than a hotel lobby, and that's not an accident of decoration. It's the same idea of a house, a few hundred miles apart.

Then there's the roof. Sevillian houses have a flat roof terrace called an ah soh teh ah, once used for drying laundry and sleeping on summer nights. This one has been turned into a mee rah dor, a lookout, and from it you see a field of rooftops and church towers with the Hee rahl dah rising behind them.

What I like about it is the restraint. Nobody has tried to make this house grander than it was. The old ceilings, the worn stone and the antiques are allowed to be what they are.

From here it's two minutes to the Plaza deh lah Alfalfa. Walk down there in the early evening, when the neighbourhood comes out, and count how many doorways along the way open onto a patio just like this one.
