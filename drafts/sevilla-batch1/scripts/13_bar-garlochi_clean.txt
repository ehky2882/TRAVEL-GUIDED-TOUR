Bar Garlochí

When Queen Elizabeth the Second died, this bar built her an altar.

You're on Calle Boteros, a narrow street between the Plaza de la Alfalfa and the Plaza del Pan. Garlochí doesn't open until late in the evening, around nine. From the street there's not much to see, just a door and a name. Inside is quite another matter.

It opened in nineteen seventy-eight, and it is devoted, top to bottom, to Holy Week, Semana Santa, Seville's great week of processions. Candelabras. Religious images. Velvet. Statues of the Virgin, gazing down from the shelves. Enough flowers for a funeral. One visitor put it well: in here, it's always Holy Week.

The man behind it is Miguel Fragoso, a devout Christian, and the extraordinary thing is that the bar is entirely sincere. It isn't a joke about religion. It's a celebration of the theatre of Seville's processions, the music, the gold and the scent of wax, by people who love it.

The house drink is the Sangre de Cristo, the Blood of Christ, a cocktail. Have one. It's a rite of passage.

And from time to time the bar builds an altar to someone. One went up for the late Duchess of Alba. In September twenty twenty-two, when Queen Elizabeth died, the staff took the flowers away from a statue of Saint John the Baptist, put up a portrait of the queen and draped it with a Union Flag. The manager said it was simply a tribute to an illustrious figure.

[beat]

Holy Week is in spring, but you can see the real thing any time. Walk five minutes west to the Plaza del Salvador, where the great processions pass in Holy Week, and step into the church to see the carved figures that are carried through these streets.
