Ah bahn tahl

The name is an old Castilian word for an apron. It's a modest name for a kitchen that first won a Michelin star in two thousand and nine.

You're on Kah yeh Al kahl deh Hoh seh deh lah Bahn deh rah, a quiet residential street in Nair vee on, just east of the old city walls. Ah bahn tahl sits behind a discreet front on the ground floor. If you're not eating tonight, there's not much to see from the pavement, and that's rather the point.

Ah bahn tahl opened in the early two thousands, and its chef, Hoo lee oh Fair nahn dess Keen teh roh, was trained in Seville. The star arrived in two thousand and nine, and for years Ah bahn tahl was one of the very few restaurants in the city to hold one.

The cooking is Andalusian, but contemporary. The house philosophy is a phrase about knowing where you come from in order to understand where you're going. In practice that means the ingredients of this region, olive oil, the fish of the Andalusian coast, sherry, cooked in a modern way and served as a tasting menu.

The dining room is designed to step out of the way. It's open, bright and warm, without any of the gilded drama you might expect. In a city where so much of the beauty is old and ornate, there's something bracing about a room that is simply clean and calm.

And here's the thing. Seville's food culture is built on the bar, the standing, the sharing and the noise. A tasting menu is the opposite of all that: you sit, you wait, you are told a story. Ah bahn tahl's achievement has been to make that slower meal feel Sevillian too.

From here, walk a minute north to Kah yeh Loo ees Mon toh toh. Along it stand the surviving arches of the Kahn yos deh Kar moh nah, the old aqueduct that for centuries brought water into the city. Follow them towards the old town.
