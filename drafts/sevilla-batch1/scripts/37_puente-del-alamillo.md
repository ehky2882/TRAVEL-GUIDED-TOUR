## Script (clean)

This bridge has no cables at the back. The tower holds the whole thing up by leaning.

You're in the Parque del Alamillo, at the northern end of the Isla de la Cartuja, looking towards the river. You should see a great white tower, leaning backwards at a steep angle, with a fan of cables running down from its front edge to a road deck crossing the water. It's not quite like any other bridge you'll have seen.

It was designed by the Spanish engineer and architect Santiago Calatrava, and built between nineteen eighty-nine and nineteen ninety-two, for the Expo. The tower is about a hundred and forty metres tall, and it leans back thirty-two degrees from the vertical. The span across the river is two hundred metres, hung from thirteen pairs of cables.

Have a look at the back of the tower. On an ordinary cable-stayed bridge, there'd be another set of cables there, anchoring the tower to the ground and stopping it toppling forward. Here there are none. The tower's own enormous weight, leaning backwards, balances the weight of the road. It's like a man leaning back on a rope to hold up a load.

Calatrava's original idea was for two bridges, one on either side of the island, as a matching pair. Only one was built. Some engineers grumble that it's an expensive way of doing a simple job. Most people just look at it and grin.

And here's the thing. The Expo of nineteen ninety-two was about announcing that Seville, after a long, quiet century, was back in the modern world. This bridge is that announcement in steel and concrete. It's meant to be seen from miles away, and it is.

Stay in the park until dusk if you can. The Parque del Alamillo is one of the largest parks in Seville, and from its paths along the river the bridge turns gold and then white against the evening sky.
