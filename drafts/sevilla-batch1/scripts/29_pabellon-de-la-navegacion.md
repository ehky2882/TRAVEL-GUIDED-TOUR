# 29 · Pabellón de la Navegación

- **Length:** 334 words, about 130 seconds at a natural pace

## Script (clean)

This building was designed to look like the upturned hull of a ship. You'll see why from the river.

You're on the Camino de los Descubrimientos, on the Isla de la Cartuja, facing the river. The pavilion is the long, low building with a great curving roof that swells up and sinks down again, like the keel of a boat turned over. Beside it, on the waterfront, stands a slender observation tower.

It was built for the World Expo of nineteen ninety-two, on the theme of navigation, and it was designed by Guillermo Vázquez Consuegra, one of Seville's leading architects. The Expo marked five hundred years since Columbus sailed from Spain, and this building was about the voyages that made Seville rich. For two centuries, the official trade with the Americas had to pass through this city.

Have a look at the roof. Steel, glass and timber, curved like the ribs of a hull. Inside, curving timber beams hold up a lofty hall, so that walking through it is a bit like being inside a ship.

After the Expo, the building stood empty for years. It was refitted by the same architect, and in twenty twelve it reopened as a museum of navigation. In June twenty twenty-six that museum closed, at least for now, while the regional government decides what to do with it next. So if the doors are shut, this is a building to read from the outside.

And here's the thing. The Expo left Seville with dozens of pavilions, and many of them have struggled to find a second life. This one is among the best of them, and it's protected now as heritage. Its story is a reminder that a great building isn't quite enough. It also needs a purpose.

*[beat]*

Walk along the riverfront to the tower. In nineteen ninety-two it was the Torre Schindler, a lift-served lookout. From its foot, look across the water to the old city, where those voyages began and ended.
