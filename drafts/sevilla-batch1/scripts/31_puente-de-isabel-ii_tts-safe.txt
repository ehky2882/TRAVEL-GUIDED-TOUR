You're on the Pwen teh deh Ee sah bel seh goon dah, which everyone in Seville calls the Pwen teh deh Tree ah nah. It's the iron bridge with a row of iron rings set into its arches, between the city centre and Tree ah nah. Stand in the middle, on the downstream side, facing the Toh reh del Oh roh.

Before this bridge, the crossing was a pontoon bridge, a string of boats roped and chained side by side with a walkway laid across them. The Al moh hads built it in the eleven seventies, and with endless repairs it lasted until the eighteen fifties. Imagine the river in flood, and that bridge of boats straining at its chains.

This was the first permanent bridge in Seville. Work began in eighteen forty five, and it opened in eighteen fifty two, under Queen Ee sah bel the Second, hence the name. It was designed by two French engineers, Goos tahv Shtine ah ker and Ferdinand Bair nah deh, and they took as their model the Pon dew Kah roo sel in Paris, which crosses the Sen by the Loov rah. Have a look over the side at the arches and those iron rings. They look like the structure, and once they were. Since a rebuilding in the nineteen seventies, the deck has been carried on hidden steel girders, and the rings and arches are there out of affection, as decoration.

And here's the thing. Sevillians love this bridge in a way they don't love any of the newer, bigger crossings. It's not about engineering. It's because it joins the city to Tree ah nah, two places that have always insisted they're different.
