## Script (clean)

This house is called Pilate's House because a nobleman came home from Jerusalem and started measuring.

You're in the Plaza de Pilatos, in the San Bartolomé quarter. In front of you is a white wall with a white marble gateway set into it, a triumphal arch in miniature, crowned with a balustrade. That's the entrance to the Casa de Pilatos.

Building began in fourteen eighty-three, for Pedro Enríquez and his wife Catalina de Ribera, and it was finished by their son, Fadrique Enríquez de Ribera, the first Marquis of Tarifa. In fifteen nineteen Fadrique went on a pilgrimage to the Holy Land. When he came back, he started a procession. In Lent of fifteen twenty-one, the Way of the Cross set out from this house to a cross outside the city walls, the Cruz del Campo, over the distance that was believed to separate Pilate's palace in Jerusalem from Calvary. That's why the house came to be called Pilate's.

The cross outside the walls is still there, by the way. And if Cruz del Campo sounds familiar, it's because Seville's most famous beer, Cruzcampo, was named after it.

Have a look at the gate before you go in. It was designed in fifteen twenty-nine by a Genoese, Antonio Maria Aprile, and it's an early taste of the Italian Renaissance in Seville.

Inside, in the main courtyard, three worlds meet. Mudéjar plasterwork. Gothic balustrades. And about a hundred and fifty different tile patterns, made in the fifteen thirties by the brothers Diego and Juan Pulido. Around the walls stand the busts of Roman emperors, many of them from the ruins of Itálica.

And here's the thing. This isn't a style in transition. It's a family who could afford the best of everything, Islamic, Gothic and classical, and saw no reason to choose.

*[beat]*

If you fancy a walk, follow the old pilgrimage route. The Humilladero de la Cruz del Campo, the little shrine at the end of the Way of the Cross, still stands on Calle Luis Montoto, about twenty-five minutes east of here.
