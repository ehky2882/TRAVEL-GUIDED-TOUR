For nearly three hundred years this was somebody's home. It only became a hotel in two thousand and nine.

Kah yeh San tee ah go, in the quiet parish of Santa Kah tah lee nah, north east of the centre. The palace is the long front on the street with a monumental doorway framed by marble columns. Through the entrance, if the doors are open, you should glimpse the courtyard beyond.

It was built around seventeen twenty nine for Mahn well Loh pess Peen tah doh, a naval commander, merchant and shipowner who made his fortune in the trade with the Americas. He did very well out of it, became the Marquis of Toh reh Blahn kah, and spent his money here. His architect was Diego Antonio Dee ahs, master of works to the archbishop and one of the most inventive builders of his day.

The plan is pure Seville. A long front on the street, carriage houses beside the entrance, the rooms arranged round a central patio, and a garden behind. The courtyard is the organising heart of it all, and it shows the Baroque at its most composed: no frenzy, just good proportions and a lot of light.

The house passed down through families of the nobility, and its last owners, the Marquises of Vee yah pah ness, lived in it until the early two thousands. That long private life is why so much survives.

And here's the thing. A hotel needs a lot of things a palace doesn't: lifts, bathrooms, a kitchen, fifty rooms. The conversion fitted them in around the old bones, with furniture by the designer Patricia Oor kee oh lah, and put a pool up on the roof. Down in the patio, you'd hardly know.

From here, walk a few minutes north west to Kah yeh Heh roh nah. On the corner opposite the church of Santa Kah tah lee nah is El Reen kon see yo, which has been pouring drinks since sixteen seventy. That's older still than the admiral's house.
