Par keh deh Mah ree ah Loo ee sah

This park was a gift from a princess, and it was redesigned by a Frenchman who looked to the Moorish gardens of Andalusia.

You're inside the Par keh deh Mah ree ah Loo ee sah, south of the old city, between the river and the Plaza deh Ess pahn yah. You should be on one of the shady avenues, with tall trees overhead, tiled benches, low fountains and long, straight channels of water running between the paths.

In eighteen ninety three, the Een fahn tah Mah ree ah Loo ee sah Fair nahn dah, Duchess of Mon pon see ay, gave the gardens of her palace of San Tel moh to the city for public use. The park is named after her. From nineteen eleven, the French landscape designer Jean Claude Nicolas Foh rest ee ay reshaped it, and a few years later Ah nee bahl Gon sah less began building the pavilions of the nineteen twenty nine exposition among its trees.

Have a look at the water. Forestier took his ideas from the gardens of the Al ahm brah and the Al kah sar: water running in narrow tiled channels, little pools, fountains that bubble rather than spout. In this climate, water and shade are luxuries, and he arranged them like jewellery.

Then find the Gloh ree eh tah deh Beh ker, in the north of the park, a memorial to the Romantic poet, made in nineteen eleven by Loh ren soh Koo yow Vah leh rah. Under a great old tree, a bust of the poet sits above three marble women, often read as the stages of love: love hoped for, love enjoyed and love lost. Beh ker wrote about all three.

And here's the thing. This is not a park in the English sense, a lawn with trees in it. It's a series of outdoor rooms, each with its own walls of hedges, its own floor of tiles and its own fountain. It's a garden built for a hot city.

Walk south through the park to the Plaza deh Ah meh ree kah, at its far end. Its pavilions were built for the fair of nineteen twenty nine, and its square is full of white doves. Bring something for them, or prepare to be thoroughly inspected.
