### 25 | pont-de-sant-agusti | Pont de Sant Agustí
Forty years ago these houses were grey. Somebody decided they shouldn't be.

You are on the Pont de Sant Agustí, the slim pedestrian bridge that crosses the Onyar from the Plaça de la Independència. Stop halfway across and face upstream. On your right, the old town climbs the hill. Along the water, a row of tall, narrow houses in ochre, rust, cream and pink, with their windows hanging out over the river, and the cathedral and the bell tower of Sant Feliu above them.

These are the Cases de l'Onyar, the river houses, and they are the picture on every postcard of Girona. Many of them grew up against the old line of the city wall, which is why they rise straight out of the water like a cliff. For a long time their river fronts were simply grey and neglected.

In the mid nineteen eighties, the city restored them. The architects Josep Maria Fuses and Joan Maria Viader worked on eighty three properties, and two local artists, Enric Ansesa and Jaume Faixó, drew up a palette of thirty nine colours for the fronts. They gave the colours names like Fog Grey, Escudella Earth, after the Catalan stew, and Church Ash. Choosing to paint them at all was, by their account, a very difficult decision at the time.

And here's the thing. What looks like the oldest, most natural view in the city is in fact a piece of late twentieth century design. The houses are old, but the colours are a decision made forty years ago by a handful of people with taste and a great deal of nerve. It worked so well that nobody can now picture Girona without them.

[beat]

Look downstream next, to the red iron bridge a little way along. Then walk across to the old town side and find Casa Masó, the white house among the colours. It's the only one of these river houses that you can go inside.

### 26 | pont-de-les-peixateries-velles | Pont de les Peixateries Velles
This red iron bridge was built by Gustave Eiffel's company, about a dozen years before his tower in Paris.

You are on the Pont de les Peixateries Velles, the red bridge across the Onyar, between the old town and the west bank. Stand in the middle and look down at the ironwork around you. It's a lattice of red painted iron, criss-crossing like a trellis.

According to the city's own archive, the bridge was built in eighteen seventy seven. It was made by the French firm Eiffel and Company, and if you look carefully you may find the name G. Eiffel and Compagnie pressed into the iron. Locals call it the Iron Bridge or simply the Red Bridge, and visitors tend to call it the Eiffel Bridge. Its proper name means the bridge of the old fish markets, after the old fish market that once stood beside it.

Have a look at how light it is. The old stone bridges of the city are heavy and solid; this one is mostly air. In eighteen seventy seven that was the newest technology in the world, prefabricated iron, made in a factory and assembled on site. It must have looked startlingly modern against the medieval houses.

What I like is that Girona doesn't seem to mind the clash. The river houses are centuries old, the colours are from the nineteen eighties, and this bridge is a piece of nineteenth century engineering. Somehow it all sits together, and the red of the bridge has become part of the palette of the river.

[beat]

Before you move on, look upstream from the middle of the bridge. Then step off on the west bank, where you'll find Rocambolesc, the Roca family's ice cream shop, just a few paces away.

### 27 | casa-maso | Casa Masó
Among all the coloured houses on the river, the white one is where Girona's great architect was born.

You are on Carrer de les Ballesteries, in the old town, outside number twenty-nine. On the street side it is fairly modest. To see it properly, though, you need its river front, so either picture it from the bridges or go in, because the house runs right through to the water. It's the white house in the row of colours on the Onyar.

This is the birthplace of Rafael Masó, who lived from eighteen eighty to nineteen thirty five and was the leading architect of Noucentisme in Girona. That was the Catalan movement of the early twentieth century, which turned away from the swirling, extravagant lines of Modernisme towards something calmer, more classical and more Mediterranean. The house was made by joining four houses that his family bought between the mid nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and what you see today follows the last changes he made, in nineteen nineteen.

Inside, the furniture and decoration of the period are still in place, with views right over the river and the city. Since two thousand and six it has belonged to the city council, and it's run by the Rafael Masó Foundation. Visits are by guided tour and must be booked ahead.

And here's the thing. Almost every one of those river houses is a private home, seen only from the outside. This is the one chance to stand at a window, looking out over the Onyar, from inside the most famous view in Girona.

[beat]

Afterwards, look for Masó's other work around the city. The Farinera Teixidor, his flour mill on Carrer de Santa Eugènia on the west bank, is the most striking, and it is a good fifteen minutes' walk away.

### 28 | farinera-teixidor | Farinera Teixidor
A flour mill is not supposed to look like this.

You are on Carrer de Santa Eugènia, on the west side of the city, a little way from the river. Look across at the building with the pale front, the bright ceramic details and the sharp pinnacles along the top. That is the Farinera Teixidor, the Teixidor flour mill.

It was designed by Rafael Masó, Girona's great architect, for the industrialist Alfons Teixidor. Work began in nineteen ten and nineteen eleven and carried on in stages until nineteen twenty three. It was a working factory, built to grind wheat, and Masó gave it the dignity of a public building.

Look up at the top of the front. The main pinnacle is said to take the form of an ear of wheat, the very thing the mill was built to turn into flour. Then look at the glazed ceramic pieces set into the walls. When the building was restored between nineteen ninety seven and nineteen ninety nine, a ceramicist remade and reglazed pieces to match. For years after that it was home to the newspaper El Punt.

And here's the thing. In the early twentieth century, Catalan industrialists often wanted their factories to say something about them, and their architects took them at their word. Masó treated a mill as a chance to show that ordinary work deserved beauty. That idea, that a factory could be a piece of art, is very much of its time, and you won't see it done better in this city.

[beat]

From here, walk back east towards the river and on into the old town. Masó was born in a white house on the Onyar, and you can find it from the bridges, which makes a satisfying end to the story.

### 29 | edifici-de-les-aligues | Edifici de les Aligues
The University of Girona is quite young, and it lives in one of the oldest university buildings in Catalonia.

You are in the Plaça de Sant Domènec, at the top of the old town. Face the long building with the carved Renaissance doorway and the stone front. That is Les Àligues, the Eagles, and today it is the seat of the university's rector.

The name comes from the eagle with two heads, the emblem of the Habsburg dynasty, who ruled Spain at the time. The first stone was laid in December fifteen sixty one, and by fifteen seventy two the old university of Girona had begun to teach here. Its roots went back further, to the fifteenth century, when a royal decree had set up a place of study in the city.

Look at the doorway and the front above it. That carved stone is the only part left from the sixteenth century. Over the following centuries the old university closed and the building declined, and by the late twentieth century much of it was in ruins. When the modern University of Girona was founded in December nineteen ninety one, the local architects Josep Fuses and Joan Maria Viader restored it, putting back its original L shape and treating the courtyard almost like a public square.

What's worth knowing is that this restoration is a big part of the reason the old town feels alive. Instead of building a campus out in the suburbs, the university came back into these old buildings. Students now fill the steps and the squares, and an old quarter that might have become a museum is still a place where people learn.

[beat]

Take a look at the university's own crest when you see one around town. Its design is based on this very front. Then cross the square to the old convent of Sant Domènec, just opposite.

### 30 | placa-de-sant-domenec | Plaça de Sant Domènec
This is the quietest square at the top of the old town, and it is the university's front room.

You are in the Plaça de Sant Domènec, at the top of the old town. On one side is Les Àligues, the Renaissance university building. On the other, a long flight of steps leads up to the old Gothic convent of Sant Domènec, now part of the university.

The convent was founded by the Dominican friars in the middle of the thirteenth century, and its church was consecrated in thirteen thirty nine. It has two cloisters, a church and the convent buildings, all Gothic. In the nineteenth century the army took it over and turned it into barracks, which is how a lot of the great religious buildings of Spain survived and suffered at the same time.

Around the turn of the millennium it became the home of the university's Faculty of Arts. So the friars' cloisters now hold lecture rooms and seminar rooms, and the stairs where friars once walked are where students sit to talk between classes.

And here's the thing. Look at how the city has used what it inherited. A Dominican convent, a Renaissance college, an army barracks, and now a university quarter. Girona didn't replace these buildings. It found new tenants for them, again and again, and the square has stayed alive because of it.

[beat]

If the convent is open, climb the steps and look into the Gothic cloister. Then walk to the back of the square and find a way up onto the city wall, which runs right behind these buildings and gives you a view over the whole of the old town.

### 31 | torre-gironella | Torre Gironella
This is the highest point of the old city walls, and it is where the story of Girona's defence began and ended.

You are at the top of the wall walk, at the eastern edge of the old town, among the ruins of the Gironella tower. Look out over the stone walls and towers falling away down the hill to the river, with the cathedral and the roofs below you.

The tower sits at the very top of the Roman wall. When the Romans strengthened the walls in the late third century, they added square towers, and one of them stood here. Around the year one thousand it became part of a castle, the castle of Gironella. Legend says the city was founded by a giant called Gerió, which is one story for where the name Girona comes from.

There's a darker chapter too. In thirteen ninety one, when anti Jewish riots swept through the cities of Catalonia, the Jews of Girona took refuge in this tower, and some were held here to be pressed into converting. The tower later fell into ruin, and in eighteen fourteen, at the end of the French occupation, Napoleon's troops blew up the main fortifications, Gironella among them.

What I find moving about this spot is that it holds both the city's strength and its failures. The walls that kept Girona safe through siege after siege are the same walls where one of its own communities could only hide. Up here, with the view at your feet, it is worth remembering both.

[beat]

From here, follow the wall walk back down the hill towards the cathedral. It runs for a good stretch along the old defences, and at every turn you get a different view over the roofs.

### 32 | basilica-de-sant-feliu | Basilica de Sant Feliu
Before Girona had a cathedral, it had this church, and it still guards the city's favourite saints.

You are in the Plaça de Sant Feliu, at the northern end of the old town, outside the basilica. Look up at the bell tower. It rises tall and pointed and then simply stops, short of a finished spire, which gives the skyline of Girona its slightly unfinished look.

Sant Feliu was the first cathedral of Girona, until the tenth century. It's named after Saint Felix, a martyr who died here at the start of the fourth century, and it grew from a funerary church over his tomb. It was built in stages from the twelfth to the seventeenth century, so it mixes a Romanesque core, a Gothic nave and a Baroque front. In twenty eleven the Pope made it a minor basilica.

Go inside and look at the walls around the altar. Set into them are eight Roman and early Christian stone coffins, from the third and fourth centuries, their carving including scenes of the hunt and stories from the Bible. Then look for the Gothic tomb of Saint Narcís, the city's patron saint. This is the tomb from the legend of the flies, which says that in twelve eighty five a swarm poured out of it to drive off a French army.

And here's the thing. This is a church built on a grave, over a Roman cemetery, outside the city's oldest walls. Christianity in Girona didn't start up the hill in the cathedral. It started here, among the dead, and the coffins on the walls are a plain reminder of it.

[beat]

When you come out, look nearby, close to the square, for the small stone lioness climbing a column. Tradition says that if you kiss her bottom you will return to Girona, and there's usually a little queue. It's up to you.

### 33 | placa-de-la-independencia | Plaça de la Independència
This square was built on the ruins of a convent, and it is named after a siege.

You are in the Plaça de la Independència, on the west bank of the river, right by the Pont de Sant Agustí. Stand by the monument in the middle. Around you, an even ring of arcades with restaurants and café tables under the arches, all in the same calm, neo classical style.

The square was designed by Martí Sureda on the site of the old convent of Sant Agustí, which was pulled down in eighteen forty one. That is why locals still call it the Plaça de Sant Agustí, and why the footbridge next to it has the same name. Its name was changed more than once over the century, and in eighteen sixty eight it became the Plaça de la Independència.

Now look at the monument. It remembers the defenders of Girona during the long siege of eighteen oh nine, when the city held out for months against Napoleon's armies before it finally fell. It was one of the most famous episodes of the Peninsular War, and it is still a source of great local pride.

What I like about this square is the contrast with the old town across the river. Over there everything is twisting and uphill. Here, everything is level, symmetrical and orderly. It's the nineteenth century city, laying out a square as a statement of reason and civic pride, and then filling it with restaurant tables, which is exactly what squares are for.

[beat]

For a final view, walk onto the Pont de Sant Agustí next to the square and look back at the coloured houses along the river. Then come back here for dinner under the arches, as Girona has done for well over a century.

### 34 | parc-de-la-devesa | Parc de la Devesa
Look up. Some of these plane trees are taller than the cathedral's vault is high.

You are in the Parc de la Devesa, on the western edge of the city centre, beside the river Ter, close to where the Onyar flows into it. Stand in one of the long straight avenues and look up into the canopy, a grid of grey and cream trunks rising as far as you can see.

This is the largest urban park in Catalonia, about forty hectares, and it is filled with roughly two thousand six hundred London plane trees. Most of them were planted in the eighteen fifties, so many are now around a hundred and seventy years old. By most accounts the tallest are more than fifty metres high, which makes them taller than the thirty five metre vault of the cathedral across the river. The park is protected as a site of national interest.

Have a look at how it's laid out. The trees stand in strict rows, forming high green avenues, rather like the nave of a church. In summer the shade here is total, and the park is where the city escapes the heat. You'll often find a local story that Napoleon ordered them planted, but there's no record that he did.

And here's the thing. Girona's old town gets all the attention, but this is its other great monument, and it was made by gardeners, not by masons. The plane trees are a living structure, a building that grows. The city looks after them with great care, replanting as the oldest begin to fail.

[beat]

From here, walk to the edge of the park, where the Ter flows past. A path runs along the riverbank, and it's one of the best walks in the city on a summer evening.

### 35 | auditori-de-girona | Auditori de Girona
On the edge of the old plane-tree park stands the city's concert hall, and it is named after one of Girona's own composers.

You are on the edge of the Parc de la Devesa, outside the Auditori, which is also Girona's conference centre, the Palau de Congressos. Look at the building in front of you, a modern hall set among the trees of the park.

Its main room is the Sala Montsalvatge, which holds one thousand two hundred and thirty six people, with space for up to three hundred more on stage. It's named after the composer Xavier Montsalvatge, who was born in Girona in nineteen twelve. There are smaller halls for chamber music and lectures too, so the building can host anything from a symphony to a congress.

Have a look at the programme if you're in town in the autumn. This is one of the main venues of the Temporada Alta festival, which brings theatre, music and dance to Girona and the towns around it every year. On a concert night the doors open onto the dark avenues of the park, and the walk in through the trees is half the occasion.

What I like is where the city chose to put it. Instead of squeezing a concert hall into the old town, it set it at the edge of its great park, among the plane trees. Nineteenth century gardeners made the avenues and twenty first century architects made the hall, and the two get along rather well.

[beat]

After a concert, walk back through the Devesa towards the river and across to the old town. With the cathedral lit up on the hill, it's one of the loveliest night walks in Girona.
