### 18 | banys-arabs | Banys Àrabs
These are called the Arab Baths, and they were built by Christians, centuries after Girona had stopped being a Muslim city.

You are at the north end of the old town, just below the cathedral, by the entrance to the baths. From outside, look for a small, squat building with a little round lantern rising from its roof. That lantern is the thing you have really come to see, and from inside it is even better.

The baths are first mentioned in eleven ninety four, when the king granted the cathedral an income from them. So these are Romanesque, a Christian building, but laid out on the pattern of the bathhouses of the Islamic world, which were themselves descended from Roman ones. Hence the name, and hence the slight confusion. They were damaged in twelve eighty five, when a French army besieged the city, and they worked as public baths until around the fourteenth century.

Go inside and the first proper room is the changing room, the apodyterium. In the middle is an eight sided pool ringed by eight slim columns with carved capitals, and above them that little lantern, letting down a soft circle of light. It is one of the most beautiful small rooms in Catalonia. From there you pass through the cold room, the warm room and the hot room, exactly as a Roman would have done.

And here's the thing. This single room carries three traditions at once. Roman bathing, the Islamic bathhouse and the Jewish ritual bath all fed into the way it was built. In a city where Christians, Jews and Muslims lived for centuries, a bathhouse turns out to be a very good portrait of the place.

[beat]

When you come out, look for the little gardens and the steps that climb towards the city wall nearby. From higher up, the view back down over that lantern is the best one you'll get.

### 19 | catedral-de-girona | Catedral de Girona
Look up. That is roughly ninety steps between you and the door, and behind the door is the widest Gothic nave in the world.

You are at the foot of the great stairway in the Plaça de la Catedral, in the old town. In front of you the steps rise to a tall Baroque front, with a bell tower above it. Take your time with the climb. Everyone does.

The stairway was finished in sixteen oh seven, and the Baroque front was begun at around the same time, though its top was only completed in nineteen sixty one. So the face you see took more than three and a half centuries to finish. If the scene feels familiar, it may be because the cathedral and these steps stood in for the Great Sept of Baelor in the sixth series of Game of Thrones.

Now, the inside. In fourteen sixteen the builders, led by Guillem Bofill and Antoni Canet, took a bold decision. Instead of a nave with two aisles, they covered the whole church with one single span. The result is a nave almost twenty three metres wide, the widest Gothic nave anywhere, and the vault rises about thirty five metres above the floor. When you walk in, there are no rows of columns. There is just one enormous room.

One more thing to find later, from the cloister: an older, plainer tower on the north side. That is the tower of Charlemagne, begun in the early eleventh century, the survivor of the Romanesque cathedral that stood here before. The builders kept it and used it to help brace the new one.

[beat]

Inside, find the museum and its Tapestry of Creation, a Romanesque embroidery about nine hundred years old, and one of the great treasures of Catalonia. Then come back out to the top of the steps and look down. The whole of the old town is laid out at your feet.

### 20 | sant-pere-de-galligants | Església de Sant Pere de Galligants
This monastery was built outside the city walls, by a stream, and it has outlasted almost everything inside them.

You are at the north end of the old town, outside a plain Romanesque church with a squat bell tower, beside the little Galligants stream. Look for the round window high in the front. That is Sant Pere de Galligants, and today it houses the Girona branch of the Museum of Archaeology of Catalonia.

The monastery dates back to nine ninety two, when the count of Barcelona handed control of this little quarter to its abbot. The church you see was finished around eleven thirty one. It ran as a monastery for centuries until the monks left in the eighteen thirties. Later it became a museum, and in nineteen thirty one it was declared a national monument.

Go in and find the cloister. Its double columns carry more than fifty carved capitals, with lions, sirens, strange beasts and scenes from the life of Christ. They are close cousins of the carvings in the cathedral cloister up the hill. The archaeology collection fills the church and cloister around them, objects from prehistory to the Middle Ages laid out among the arches.

What's worth knowing is why the building looks so solid. Outside the walls a monastery was exposed, and it needed to look after itself. It's tempting to read that into the church, which feels rather like a small fortress. It has survived sieges, the loss of its monks and two centuries of changing use, and it has done so by being very, very sturdy.

[beat]

From the front door, follow the stream uphill and away from the town. Within a few minutes you are in the valley of Sant Daniel, all streams and woods, and the city simply disappears behind you.

### 21 | museu-historia-jueus | Museu d'Història dels Jueus
For about six hundred years Girona had one of the most important Jewish communities in Europe, and it ended with a single document.

You are on Carrer de la Força, in the old town, outside number eight. This is the Bonastruc ça Porta Centre, named after the scholar better known as Nahmanides, one of the great Jewish thinkers of the thirteenth century, who was born in Girona. The building houses the Museum of Jewish History. All around you is the Call, the old Jewish quarter, a tangle of steep, narrow lanes.

Go in through the courtyard. The building is a set of old houses, and it is thought to stand on the site of the last synagogue of the Jewish community and the rooms around it. In twenty fourteen, archaeologists from the University of Girona lifted the floor of a space next to the courtyard and found a mikveh, a ritual bath, belonging to that synagogue, which was in use from fourteen thirty five until fourteen ninety two.

Then there is the document. In fourteen ninety two the Catholic Monarchs ordered all Jews to convert or leave, and the order was copied into the city's own register. The museum displays it, and the original is still kept in the city archive. Six centuries of shared life came to an end with it.

And here's the thing. For centuries afterwards the Call was simply forgotten, with houses built over the old streets. Only from the nineteen eighties did the city begin to uncover it again. So walking these lanes is, in a small way, an act of remembering.

[beat]

When you come out, wander up the narrow lane called Carrer de Sant Llorenç, just beside the museum. It's the steepest and narrowest part of the Call, and it feels much as it must have done in the Middle Ages.

### 22 | bolit-sant-nicolau | Bòlit Sant Nicolau
A tiny twelfth century chapel, and inside it, art that might have been made last month.

You are at the north end of the old town, a few steps from the monastery of Sant Pere de Galligants. Look for a small, low Romanesque chapel with a rounded end. That is the chapel of Sant Nicolau. Today it is one of the spaces of the Bòlit, Girona's centre for contemporary art.

The Bòlit was set up by the city in two thousand and eight to research, produce and show contemporary work. It doesn't have one big gallery; instead it uses several spaces around Girona, and this chapel is the most surprising of them. The exhibitions change through the year, and they are often installations made for this one room. In recent seasons artists have filled it with video, sculpture and new pieces that respond to questions of heritage and memory.

Have a look at the building itself first. The walls are thick, the windows small, and the end of the chapel curves round in the classic Romanesque apse. It was built to hold the dark, the candlelight and the quiet, and that is exactly what makes it so good for art today. There is nothing else to look at.

What I like is the boldness of the idea. A city this old could have treated a twelfth century chapel as something to be kept behind a rope. Instead it hands it to living artists and asks them to have a conversation with it. Some of the pieces work and some don't, but the building always gives as good as it gets.

[beat]

Afterwards, step next door into the cloister of Sant Pere de Galligants. Its carved capitals were the cutting edge of their own day, and it's a nice way to see that every age's art was once new.

### 23 | museu-del-cinema | Museu del Cinema
This museum tells the story of the moving image from long before anyone had a film camera.

You are on the west bank of the Onyar, outside the old headquarters of the city water company. Look up for the tower on its roof. Locals still call the building the Casa de les Aigües, the House of the Waters. That's the Museu del Cinema.

It opened on the eighth of April, nineteen ninety eight, after the city bought a remarkable private collection in nineteen ninety four. That collection belonged to Tomàs Mallol, a filmmaker and collector from Girona, and it holds around twenty thousand pieces. There are shadow puppets, magic lanterns, optical toys, early cameras and projectors, posters and films, the whole prehistory and history of cinema.

Have a look for the shadow theatres and the magic lanterns. Long before films, people sat in the dark and watched light play on a wall. Those objects show that the desire to see pictures move is centuries older than the cinema itself. Some of the devices can be tried, so you can make an image move with your own hands.

What's worth knowing is how personal all of this is. It began with one man's obsession, built up over decades, and it ended up in a building designed to store water. It is a quietly eccentric museum, and its strength is that it was collected by someone who simply loved how pictures came alive.

[beat]

Once you're done, walk the few minutes north to the river and stand on the Pont de Sant Agustí. The coloured house fronts reflected in the Onyar are a moving picture of their own, and the best show in town.

### 24 | museu-dart | Museu d'Art
The art museum of Girona lives in the palace of its bishops, right next door to the cathedral.

You are at the top of the old town, beside the cathedral, at the entrance to the old Episcopal Palace. That is the Museu d'Art. If you have just climbed the cathedral steps, you only need to turn and look along the square.

The museum began life in the monastery of Sant Pere de Galligants, before the bishops gave it this palace as its permanent home. Its collection is one of the most important in Catalonia for Romanesque and Gothic art, gathered from the churches and monasteries of the region.

Look out for its star piece, a portable altar from the monastery of Sant Pere de Rodes on the coast. It's a stone and wood altar clad in silver, more than a thousand years old, designed to be carried and set up wherever a service needed to be held. Look out too for a painted wooden beam from the monastery of Sant Miquel de Cruïlles, a rare painted survivor from the Romanesque centuries.

And here's the thing. Most of what is in this building was made to be seen in a small country church by candlelight, not on a museum wall. So when you look at it, try to picture it back in place, in a village chapel up in the hills. That's where it was meant to work its magic.

[beat]

The palace windows look out over the cathedral and the roofs of the old town, so do find one before you leave. Then walk round the side of the cathedral to the city wall, which is just behind it.
