## 01 · Casa Cacao

*332 words · ~133 s*

The building on this corner of the square smells faintly of chocolate, and that is entirely deliberate.

Plaça de Catalunya is the wide square laid over the river Onyar, just south of the old town. With the water behind you, find number twenty-three: a tall, pale building on the edge of the square, with a shop and a bar at street level. That is Casa Cacao.

It belongs to the Roca family, the three brothers behind El Celler de Can Roca, and it opened in February twenty twenty as two ideas under one roof. On the ground floor, Jordi Roca, the youngest brother and the family's pastry chef, runs a bean to bar chocolate workshop. Bean to bar means they take raw cocoa beans and do every step themselves, the roasting, the grinding, the long slow mixing, all the way to the finished bar. Upstairs, Anna Payet, who is married to the eldest brother, Joan, runs a hotel of fifteen rooms.

Now look up at the roofline. Breakfast is served on the rooftop terrace, with the old town spread out across the river, and the breakfast menu is Joan Roca's own. So for the price of a room you can eat a breakfast designed by a chef with three Michelin stars, which is rather a good trick.

What I like is where they put it. Not in a medieval palace up the hill, but in a mid twentieth century block on a busy square that most people cross on their way to somewhere else. The family took their most playful project and set it on the ordinary side of the river, and let the old town be the view.

[beat]

Before you go, walk to the corner of the square where the old stone bridge, the Pont de Pedra, crosses the Onyar. Stand in the middle of it and look upstream. That is your first proper look at the painted houses along the river, and it is a very good place to begin.

## 02 · Nord 1901

*307 words · ~123 s*

Behind this rather sober street front there is a swimming pool, and in the middle of an old city that is a genuinely rare thing.

You are on Carrer Nord, a narrow shopping street on the west bank of the Onyar, a few minutes' walk from the river. Find the hotel's entrance, and notice how little it gives away. A townhouse front, a discreet sign, and that is your lot.

The hotel takes its name from its street and its date, and the building it occupies was completely restored in two thousand and nine. It is run by the owner's family, and you can feel that in the scale of it: rooms, a few suites, and a handful of apartments in the old town nearby, rather than a long anonymous corridor.

The gift is out the back. The rooms on the inside of the block look over a walled garden with a pool, and the hotel describes it, quite fairly as far as I can tell, as the only garden with a swimming pool in the city centre. In a town where most of the old streets are barely wider than a car, a private green courtyard with water in it feels close to extravagant.

And here's the thing. Girona's centre is full of these hidden backs. The street gives you one face, the courtyard gives you another, and the city keeps its best rooms out of sight. This is simply one of the few where a stranger gets to sit in one.

[beat]

From here, walk east to the river and cross by the Pont de Sant Agustí, the pedestrian bridge just upstream. Halfway across, turn around and look back at the west bank. You will see how the whole of this side of town sits low and flat, waiting for you to climb the hill opposite.

## 03 · Hotel Museu Llegendes de Girona

*306 words · ~122 s*

Every old city has its stories, and this one decided to sleep inside them.

You are at the top end of the old town, on the street called Portal de la Barca, a short walk below the monastery of Sant Pere de Galligants. The hotel is in a restored eighteenth century building of fifteen rooms. Its full name, the Museum Hotel of the Legends of Girona, tells you exactly what it is about.

The theme is the folklore of the city, and Girona has plenty. The most famous is the legend of the flies of Saint Narcís. The story goes that when a French army besieged the city in twelve eighty five, soldiers broke open the tomb of the saint, and a cloud of flies poured out and drove them away. The fly has been Girona's mascot ever since, and you will see it on everything from sweets to street decorations.

Then there is the lioness, a small stone figure climbing a column by the basilica of Sant Feliu, a short walk south of here. Tradition says that if you kiss her bottom, you will come back to Girona. People queue to do it.

Here is what I find interesting about a hotel like this. In most cities legends are something you are told on a walking tour and then forget. Girona actually lives by them. The flies, the lioness, the giant who supposedly founded the city: they turn up in festivals, in shop windows and on the menus. A hotel built on them is not a gimmick so much as an accurate reading of the place.

[beat]

From the door, walk uphill to the church of Sant Pere de Galligants, just round the corner. Its cloister is full of carved stone creatures from the twelfth century, and they are every bit as strange as the legends.

## 04 · Pensió Bellmirall

*308 words · ~123 s*

This is a guesthouse with thick stone walls and a painter's eye, tucked into one of the quietest lanes of the old town.

You are on Carrer de Bellmirall, a narrow street just behind the cathedral and on the edge of the old Jewish quarter, the Call. Look for number three. The front is old, plain stone, which is exactly the point.

The guesthouse was set up in the nineteen sixties by the painter Isidre Vicens, who lived from nineteen eighteen to two thousand and sixteen and was, by most accounts, something of a free spirit. It has kept its own character for more than fifty years, and his paintings still hang in its rooms. The rooms themselves keep their original stone walls and old furniture. It feels less like a hotel than a house that happens to take in guests.

Have a look at the street around you. These lanes were laid out in the Middle Ages, and they twist because they were built to fit the hill rather than to be easy to walk. The houses are tall, the light comes down in slices, and the stone is warm in colour. Staying here, you wake up inside the very fabric the rest of the city comes to look at.

What I like about a place like this is that it has not been smoothed over. A lot of old town hotels strip a building back to bare stone and add a designer's touch. This one simply carries on being what it was, a small family guesthouse with a painter's pictures on the walls.

[beat]

Before you move on, walk to the end of the street and up to the city wall. A stretch of the wall walk runs very close by, and from up there you can look straight across the cathedral roof to the hills beyond.

## 05 · El Celler de Can Roca

*332 words · ~133 s*

On a quiet residential street on the edge of Girona sits a restaurant that has twice been named the best in the world.

You are in Taialà, a neighbourhood on the western edge of the city, on Can Sunyer street. Find the house at number forty-eight, set back behind its garden. There is no great fanfare at the gate, and that rather sets the tone.

The story starts about a hundred metres from here. In nineteen sixty seven the Roca parents opened a family restaurant called Can Roca, serving everyday food to the neighbourhood. In nineteen eighty six their three sons opened El Celler next door. Joan is the head chef, Josep the sommelier, and Jordi is in charge of desserts. The restaurant earned its first Michelin star in nineteen ninety five, its second in two thousand and two, and its third in two thousand and nine. In two thousand and thirteen, and again in two thousand and fifteen, it was ranked number one on the World's Fifty Best Restaurants list.

In two thousand and seven they moved into this house. Inside, the Barcelona interior designers Isabel López Vilalta and Sandra Tarruella, working with Ricard Trenchs, organised the dining room in a triangle around a small courtyard of silver birch trees, with glass walls on every side. So you eat around a little forest. The light comes through the leaves, and the seasons are part of the room.

And here's the thing. For all the stars, the family never left the street they grew up on. The parents' restaurant is still around the corner, still serving a set daily menu at ordinary prices. Few places in the world let you see the whole arc of a cooking family in a single stroll.

[beat]

If you have a few minutes, walk the short distance to the original Can Roca and look in. Same family, same neighbourhood, a very different bill, and it is the best way to understand where all of this came from.

## 06 · Restaurant Normal

*310 words · ~124 s*

The Roca brothers called this restaurant Normal, which is a fairly bold thing to do when you have three Michelin stars.

You are in the Plaça de l'Oli, the square of oil, in the old town. Find number one, a stone building from the seventeenth century, with the restaurant on the ground floor.

Normal opened on the ninth of July, twenty twenty one, as a celebration of everyday Catalan cooking, with Josep Roca directing it and Elisabet Nolla as head chef when it opened. The food is the kind of thing a Catalan family might cook at home, done very well.

The interior is worth a look on its own. It was designed by Andreu Carulla, a Girona designer who has worked with the brothers since two thousand and nine, and he made every piece of furniture for it. There are booths of French oak, and leather dining chairs based on the traditional lattice backed chairs of Spanish kitchens. Ceramic lamps were made by local potters. Then there's the detail I love: the countertops are terrazzo made from recycled glass from the brothers' own restaurant, El Celler. The signs are cut by hand into the limestone floor. Local ceramicists, carpenters and basket weavers all worked on it.

What's worth knowing is that this is a statement as much as a menu. After years of avant garde cooking, the most famous restaurant family in Spain opened a place devoted to the ordinary, and built it with craftspeople from the same corner of Catalonia. The furniture makes the same argument as the food. Normal, here, means made nearby, made carefully, and made to last.

[beat]

From the square, wander a few steps north into the streets around the cathedral. The stone walls you see there are the same stone that lines the dining room, and the old town is the restaurant's real reference.

## 07 · Divinum

*280 words · ~112 s*

Here is a restaurant with no hotel and no famous brand behind it, and that is exactly what makes its Michelin star interesting.

You are on Carrer de l'Albereda, a short, smart street just off the river near the Plaça de Catalunya. Find number seven, with the restaurant at street level.

Divinum is run by two people: Joan Morillo in the kitchen and Laura Tejero in the dining room, where she is also the sommelier. In November twenty twenty four the restaurant received its first Michelin star. When it was announced, they made a point of saying they had no hotel, no group and no brand behind them, just the two of them and their team. Joan Morillo's next remark was that they would now fight for a second.

The cooking leans on Catalan tradition with a slightly French accent, and reviewers tend to single out the wine as a real strength, which you would hope for in a place whose name is a pun on wine. Booking ahead is wise, particularly at weekends.

And here's the thing. Girona is famous for one restaurant family, and it is easy to imagine the city's food begins and ends with them. It doesn't. This is a small, independent kitchen that earned its star the long way, by being very good every single night, and it is part of the reason the whole province now counts as one of Spain's serious food destinations.

[beat]

When you leave, walk two minutes north to the river and take any of the bridges across to the old town. The kitchens in these streets keep a remarkably high standard, and there is good food on almost every corner.

## 08 · Cal Ros

*316 words · ~126 s*

This is one of the oldest restaurant names in the city, under some of its oldest arches.

You are in the old town just behind the river, where Carrer de la Cort Reial meets the stone arcades known as the Voltes d'en Rosés. Look for Cal Ros at number nine, tucked under the arches. Take a moment with the stone around you, because the arches are the point.

This building has a long history of feeding people. It is described as an old inn and a meeting place for merchants, and by most accounts the name Cal Ros has been served here for more than a century. In April twenty twenty five it began a new chapter, when the Gascons family, who run the Michelin starred Els Tinars, opened it as Fonda Cal Ros. Marc Gascons runs the kitchen, and his sister Elena runs the dining room.

The idea is very simple and very Catalan. The food comes in courses meant for sharing. Tomatoes hang from the ceiling, ready to be rubbed onto bread. Wine, and even beer, arrives in a porrón, the glass jug with a long spout that you pour straight into your mouth from a height. The house butifarra, the Catalan sausage, is made from Duroc pork, and there are dishes on a blackboard that change with the market each week.

What I like is that the family describes it as reviving something they did back in the nineteen nineties, and keeping the porrón because it was part of how they grew up. In a city now famous for its tasting menus, this is the other half of Catalan eating: loud, generous, shared, and a bit messy.

[beat]

Before you go in, or after, walk the length of the arcade. The vaulted walkway runs along the backs of the river houses, and it shows you how merchants once did business out of the rain.

## 09 · Rocambolesc

*283 words · ~113 s*

A pastry chef with three Michelin stars opened an ice cream shop, and he did it with a sense of mischief.

You are on Carrer de Santa Clara, on the west bank of the Onyar, right by the red iron bridge. Find number fifty. There will very likely be a queue, and that is a fair sign you are in the right place.

Rocambolesc opened in April twenty twelve. Jordi Roca, the pastry chef of El Celler de Can Roca, created it with Ale Rivas. The idea began as a dessert trolley. The plan was to take the restaurant's little cart of sweets out onto the streets of Girona. In the end they gave it a permanent home instead.

Have a look at the interior. It was designed by the Barcelona studio of Sandra Tarruella as a sort of theatre, with tubes and taps that make the counter look rather like a laboratory. That's no accident. Everything is developed in Jordi Roca's own pastry workshop, where they mix old techniques with new ones. Some of the toppings and shapes come straight from his restaurant desserts.

And here's the thing. A shop like this is the easiest way to taste what El Celler is about. You don't need a reservation months ahead or a serious bill, just a few minutes in a queue on a pavement by the river. It is the most democratic thing the family makes.

[beat]

With your ice cream in hand, step onto the red iron bridge a few paces away. From the middle you have the river houses on both sides and the cathedral above, which makes it one of the best spots in town to stand still and eat.

## 10 · La Fábrica

*302 words · ~121 s*

If you have ever wondered why there are so many people in Lycra drinking flat whites in this city, it begins here.

You are on Carrer de la Llebre, a short lane in the old town, just up from the river. Find number three, and you will probably find a row of very expensive bicycles leaning outside before you find the door.

La Fábrica opened in twenty fifteen. It was the first specialty coffee shop in Girona, set up by Christian Meier, a Canadian professional cyclist, and his wife Amber. They had moved here from Vancouver when Christian signed his first professional contract, because Girona had quietly become a training base for racing cyclists from all over the world. The mountains are close, the roads are quiet, and the weather is kind for most of the year.

Have a look at the walls when you go in. They are hung with cycling photographs by the Belgian photographer Kristof Ramon, and inside it feels cosy and fairly dark, a proper refuge after a long morning in the hills. The menu is substantial, because people who have just ridden a hundred kilometres are very hungry.

And here's the thing. This one small café helped to set the tone for a whole part of the city's life. Before it, Girona was a historic town that happened to have cyclists. After it, cycling culture had a home in the old town, with coffee to match, and other places followed. It is a reminder that a city's character can be changed by one couple with a good idea.

[beat]

When you leave, walk a few minutes to Espresso Mafia on Carrer de la Cort Reial. It belongs to the same couple and it is where they roast the coffee, so the two together tell you the whole story.

## 11 · Espresso Mafia

*258 words · ~103 s*

The name sounds like a joke, and the coffee is taken extremely seriously.

You are on Carrer de la Cort Reial, in the old town a short walk from the river, outside number five. It is a small, bright room. Look through the window and notice the colours: gold, white and wood.

This is the second café from Christian and Amber Meier, the Canadian couple behind La Fábrica round the corner. The founders' story is that it all started with a small roaster, three kilos at a time, and the search for somewhere to put it. That search became Espresso Mafia. They describe it as the first coffee roaster ever to work inside the city, and today it is where they roast for both cafés.

The design is deliberately spare. The owners say it is inspired by Japan, minimal and calm, and focused on nothing but coffee. La Fábrica is the one for a long breakfast. This one is for standing at the counter and paying attention to what is in the cup.

What's worth knowing is how unusual this is in an old town. Most cafés in a historic centre sell atmosphere; the coffee is almost an afterthought. Here the room is pared right back so the coffee can do the talking, and inside these medieval walls that restraint feels rather refreshing.

[beat]

From here, walk downhill to the river and onto the Pont de Sant Agustí, just to the south. With the cup still warm, it is the classic spot for the coloured houses along the Onyar.

## 12 · Federal Cafe

*276 words · ~110 s*

This street was once part of a Roman road, and these days it is where Girona comes for brunch.

You are on Carrer de la Força, the long, narrow street that climbs through the old town towards the cathedral. Find number nine, with a bright café on the ground floor and, on a good day, a few tables outside.

Federal opened here, by its own account, in twenty fifteen, and it has become one of the most recommended breakfast spots in the city. Its draw is a full day of brunch, from eggs to pastries, with plenty of vegetarian and vegan options, served in a light, airy room. It is the kind of place you fall into after a morning of museums and stay for longer than you planned.

Now look along the street in both directions. Carrer de la Força follows the line of the Via Augusta, the great Roman road that ran down the coast of Spain, and for centuries this was the main way through Girona. It also runs through the heart of the Call, the old Jewish quarter. Almost opposite is the Museum of Jewish History.

And here's the thing. You are having breakfast on one of the oldest streets in Catalonia, a street that has carried legions, merchants and pilgrims. The café itself is very much of now, but the setting is about two thousand years old, and that contrast is half the pleasure.

[beat]

When you've eaten, carry on uphill along the same street. Within a couple of minutes you will reach the foot of the great stairway up to the cathedral, which is quite a sight on a full stomach.

## 13 · Nykteri's Cocktail Bar

*291 words · ~116 s*

Girona has a cocktail bar with a cocktail named after its flower festival, and you are standing outside it.

You are on Carrer de Santa Llúcia, a short street on the west bank of the river, a few minutes' walk from the Plaça de la Independència. Look for number four. It is a small room, which is rather the point.

Nykteri's opened in twenty thirteen. It was founded by Mariona Vilanova, who has more than twenty years' experience mixing drinks. It is an intimate space, with classic cocktails, signature creations and a gin list of more than fifty.

Two drinks here tell you a lot about the city. One is called Temps de Flor, after the festival of the same name, when every May Girona fills its courtyards, stairways and churches with flowers. The other is a line of cocktails made with ratafia, the local herbal liqueur of Catalonia, made by steeping green walnuts and herbs in spirit. The bar uses one from a producer called Terrània. Ratafia is the sort of thing a grandmother keeps in a cupboard, and here it is in a coupe glass.

What I like about it is that a good cocktail bar ought to taste of where it is. A lot of them, the world over, could be anywhere at all. This one pours the region's flowers and its walnuts into the glass. On Thursday evenings there is usually a themed after work session with three drinks and a bite, which is a very civilised way to end a day of sightseeing.

[beat]

After a drink, walk down to the Plaça de la Independència, with its arcades and its tables. It's the nicest place on this side of the river to stay out a little longer.

## 14 · Cafè Le Bistrot

*297 words · ~119 s*

These are some of the best seats in the city, and they are on a staircase.

You are on the Pujada de Sant Domènec, the broad flight of stone steps in the old town that climbs towards the church of Sant Martí Sacosta. Halfway up, the café's tables spill out across the steps. Look up the stairs to the church front at the top, and down towards the old shops below.

Le Bistrot has been here since the late nineteen seventies, and it is one of the city's true classics. The thing to order is the pizza de pagès. It is made on a slice of the round country loaf of Catalonia, the pa de pagès, toasted on one side and baked with its topping on the other. There are about twenty variations.

Now have a look around you. You are sitting in the middle of a theatre set. The stairs climb to a seventeenth century church, the old walls rise on either side, and almost everyone who walks through the old town ends up passing these tables. It's one of the most photographed corners of Girona, and once you have sat here you understand why.

And here's the thing. Girona is a hill town, and a hill town is a town of stairs. Most cities treat a staircase as something to get up as quickly as possible. Here they put tables on it and turned it into a room. That might be the most Catalan piece of urban design in the whole city.

[beat]

Before you leave, look at the old theatre building next door, now a bicycle shop. Then carry on to the top of the steps, where the old university buildings begin. That corner is one of the quietest and loveliest in the old town.

## 15 · Llibreria 22

*284 words · ~114 s*

This bookshop is named after its own street number, which is the least pretentious name a bookshop has ever had.

You are on Carrer de les Hortes, on the west bank of the river, outside number twenty-two. Locals simply call it La Vint i Dos, the Twenty Two.

It was founded in nineteen seventy eight, in the first years after Franco, when Catalan culture was finding its voice again. One of its founders, Guillem Terribas, ran it for decades until two thousand and fifteen, and became one of the best known booksellers in Catalonia. The shop specialises in the humanities, and it is a meeting place as much as a shop. It has hosted so many book launches that it may well hold the record in Spain. Since nineteen eighty one it has run its own literary prize, the Just M. Casero prize for short novels.

Have a look at the shelves when you go in, particularly the Catalan literature and local history. This is where the city's writers come to launch their books and where their readers come to meet them.

What's worth knowing is that independent bookshops are a fragile thing everywhere, and this one has found a way to carry on. It recently joined forces with Abacus, the large Catalan cooperative, which now holds a majority stake. The shop has given up some of its independence to secure its future, and so far it still feels very much like its old self.

[beat]

From here, walk a couple of minutes east to the river and cross to the old town. If you've bought a book, the steps of the Pujada de Sant Domènec are a fine place to read the first chapter.

## 16 · Mercat del Lleó

*281 words · ~112 s*

If you want to know what Girona actually eats, rather than what it serves visitors, this is where you look.

You are on the west bank of the river, at the Plaça Calvet i Rubalcaba, outside the city's covered market. That is the Mercat del Lleó, the Lion Market.

It opened in the mid nineteen forties as the first municipal market in the city, and it has been feeding the town ever since. Inside you will find the produce of the region: fruit and vegetables from the market gardens, fish from the Costa Brava, cured meats and cheeses from the hills, and seasonal things like wild mushrooms in the autumn.

Have a look for the market bar when you go in. Bars inside the market have been serving traders and shoppers for most of its life, and they are the best place to eat something hot at the counter early in the day. This is the opposite of a tasting menu. You point at something, it is cooked, and you eat it standing up next to someone doing their weekly shop.

And here's the thing. Girona has become famous for its restaurants, but every one of the cooks you have heard of depends on what comes through markets like this one. The great kitchens rest on the stalls. If you want to understand the food scene here, a morning at the market will tell you more than a dinner.

[beat]

Come in the morning, when it is busiest. Then walk north along the river to the Plaça de Catalunya and up into the old town, and you will see the same produce turning up on menus all the way up the hill.

## 17 · Velodrom

*316 words · ~126 s*

This was a nineteenth century theatre, then a dance hall, then a workshop, and now it is a temple to the bicycle.

You are on the Pujada de Sant Domènec, by the broad steps in the old town, next door to the tables of Café Le Bistrot. Look at the building at number six, the old Odeon. It has been a place of leisure in Girona since the nineteenth century, first as a theatre and then as a dance hall. In more recent years it was a carpentry and chrome workshop. Then it closed, and for more than a decade it sat in decline.

A Girona businessman, Miquel Casals, bought it and spent about two million euros and four years restoring it. The architect, Bruna Vila, kept the original character. She preserved part of the painted ceiling, the oval structure of wrought iron, and the old sofas. In May twenty twenty five the Velodrom group, a cycling brand from Girona, opened its shop across both floors, timed for a big gravel race called The Traka.

Have a look inside if you can. You have an old performance hall full of beautiful bikes and kit, with a café and sofas, and plans for concerts, podcasts and book launches, all on the theme of cycling. Outside, the steps next door have been fitted with racks for bicycles.

What I like is how naturally it suits the city. Girona has become one of the best known cycling towns in Europe, and the riders needed a gathering place. Rather than build a new one, the city found an old hall that had always been about entertainment and gave it a new kind of audience.

[beat]

Now turn round and look up the steps towards Sant Martí Sacosta. On a summer morning you'll often see riders setting off from here into the hills, and it's worth a moment to watch them go.

## 18 · Banys Àrabs

*319 words · ~128 s*

These are called the Arab Baths, and they were built by Christians, centuries after Girona had stopped being a Muslim city.

You are at the north end of the old town, just below the cathedral, by the entrance to the baths. From outside, look for a small, squat building with a little round lantern rising from its roof. That lantern is the thing you have really come to see, and from inside it is even better.

The baths are first mentioned in eleven ninety four, when the king granted the cathedral an income from them. So these are Romanesque, a Christian building, but laid out on the pattern of the bathhouses of the Islamic world, which were themselves descended from Roman ones. Hence the name, and hence the slight confusion. They were damaged in twelve eighty five, when a French army besieged the city, and they worked as public baths until around the fourteenth century.

Go inside and the first proper room is the changing room, the apodyterium. In the middle is an eight sided pool ringed by eight slim columns with carved capitals, and above them that little lantern, letting down a soft circle of light. It is one of the most beautiful small rooms in Catalonia. From there you pass through the cold room, the warm room and the hot room, exactly as a Roman would have done.

And here's the thing. This single room carries three traditions at once. Roman bathing, the Islamic bathhouse and the Jewish ritual bath all fed into the way it was built. In a city where Christians, Jews and Muslims lived for centuries, a bathhouse turns out to be a very good portrait of the place.

[beat]

When you come out, look for the little gardens and the steps that climb towards the city wall nearby. From higher up, the view back down over that lantern is the best one you'll get.

## 19 · Catedral de Girona

*322 words · ~129 s*

Look up. That is roughly ninety steps between you and the door, and behind the door is the widest Gothic nave in the world.

You are at the foot of the great stairway in the Plaça de la Catedral, in the old town. In front of you the steps rise to a tall Baroque front, with a bell tower above it. Take your time with the climb. Everyone does.

The stairway was finished in sixteen oh seven, and the Baroque front was begun at around the same time, though its top was only completed in nineteen sixty one. So the face you see took more than three and a half centuries to finish. If the scene feels familiar, it may be because the cathedral and these steps stood in for the Great Sept of Baelor in the sixth series of Game of Thrones.

Now, the inside. In fourteen sixteen the builders, led by Guillem Bofill and Antoni Canet, took a bold decision. Instead of a nave with two aisles, they covered the whole church with one single span. The result is a nave almost twenty three metres wide, the widest Gothic nave anywhere, and the vault rises about thirty five metres above the floor. When you walk in, there are no rows of columns. There is just one enormous room.

One more thing to find later, from the cloister: an older, plainer tower on the north side. That is the tower of Charlemagne, begun in the early eleventh century, the survivor of the Romanesque cathedral that stood here before. The builders kept it and used it to help brace the new one.

[beat]

Inside, find the museum and its Tapestry of Creation, a Romanesque embroidery about nine hundred years old, and one of the great treasures of Catalonia. Then come back out to the top of the steps and look down. The whole of the old town is laid out at your feet.

## 20 · Església de Sant Pere de Galligants

*299 words · ~120 s*

This monastery was built outside the city walls, by a stream, and it has outlasted almost everything inside them.

You are at the north end of the old town, outside a plain Romanesque church with a squat bell tower, beside the little Galligants stream. Look for the round window high in the front. That is Sant Pere de Galligants, and today it houses the Girona branch of the Museum of Archaeology of Catalonia.
