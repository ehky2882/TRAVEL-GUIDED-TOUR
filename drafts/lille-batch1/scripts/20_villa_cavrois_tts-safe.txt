Veela Kavrwah

Air, light, work, sport, hygiene, comfort and efficiency were the principles behind this house, and you can see most of them at once from where you're standing.

The long, low villa of yellow brick is the veela kavrwah, and the best place to take it in is from the garden side, beside the long reflecting pool, where the whole sixty metre length of the house lies across the water in one horizontal line.

The house was designed by robair malay stevens and finished in nineteen thirty two for pol kavrwah, a textile industrialist who needed room for his seven children and the household staff. For its day it was remarkably up to date, with electric light in every room, radios, telephones between rooms, plentiful hot water and a twenty seven metre swimming pool.

The plan is the surprise, because for all its modern materials the villa borrows the layout of a French shatoh, with two symmetrical wings either side of a central hall, so it's a very traditional idea of a grand family home dressed in the language of the future.

Down at the water, the reflecting pool, seventy two metres long, had disappeared completely by the time the state stepped in, and it was only through archaeological digging in the garden that it could be found and rebuilt.

That rescue came late, because German troops had used the villa as barracks in the Second World War, and after its sale in nineteen eighty five it fell into serious disrepair and faced the threat of demolition. It was protected in nineteen ninety, bought by the state in two thousand and one, and reopened in June twenty fifteen after twelve years of restoration costing around twenty three million euros.

So the house that looks so serenely certain of itself very nearly vanished, and the calm you see in that long reflection was hard won.

Inside, tablets fill the gaps left by lost furniture with digital reconstructions, so carry one round as you walk through, comparing what survived with what had to be imagined back.
