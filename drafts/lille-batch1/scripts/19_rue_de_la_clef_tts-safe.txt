Roo de la Klay

This is one of the oldest streets in Lille, with a line that may go back to around the year one thousand, and its name comes from an old inn sign: the otel de la klay, the Inn of the Key.

Roo de la klay runs between boolvar karno and the little plass day pateenyay, and wherever you've joined it, the thing to notice first is the shape of it, a narrow cobbled lane of small shopfronts and cafes with flowers and greenery spilling from the windows.

Look for the street sign first, which comes with a mosaic in the shape of a key, the work of a street artist who signs as meefamosa. It's a small, playful nod to the name, and once you've seen it you'll find yourself looking for the key everywhere.

The boulevard tells its own story, since boolvar karno was cut through the old town from nineteen oh seven, replacing a narrow street called the roo dayz wahyay, and it's a reminder that much of medieval Lille was cleared in the name of progress, while this lane was simply left to carry on along its old course.

The name had its own close call, too, because during the Revolution the street was briefly renamed roo marah, after one of the radical leaders of the day, before quietly going back to being the street of the key.

Not so long ago this was a stretch that people tended to avoid, and today it's one of the favourite strolls in the old town, lined with independent fashion boutiques and a cafe or two along the way.

What survives here is less a set of famous buildings than the route itself, a path that people have been walking for close to a thousand years, which has outlasted inns, revolutionary renamings and the boulevards that swept away its neighbours.

At the far end, plass day pateenyay leads on into roo day shah bossoo, the street of the hunchbacked cats, a name that practically begs to be explored.
