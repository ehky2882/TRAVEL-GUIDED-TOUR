Méert

Lille's most famous sweet was invented by a Fleming, which tells you a good deal about a city that has sat on the edge of Flanders for most of its history.

Twenty-seven Rue Esquermoise, on the busy shopping street that runs away from the Grand Place, is the address, and you'll spot it by the elegant window displays and the small golden boxes stacked inside, with a tea room further back.

The gaufre is what most people come for, and it isn't the thick, crisp waffle you might be picturing, but a thin, oval wafer of brioche dough, baked between two irons, cut to shape and then filled by hand with butter, sugar and Madagascar vanilla, and the recipe is said to have stayed the same since 1849.

That was the year Michael Paulus Gislinus Méert, a confectioner from Flanders, took over the business and gave it his name, though sweets had been made at this address for a long time before him, and the house traces its own story back well into the eighteenth century.

Now look past the counter at the room itself, which dates from 1839, when the architect Charles Benvignat dressed it in gilding, coffered ceilings, fine woods, marble and decorative ironwork, so that buying a box of biscuits here feels a little like shopping in a jewellery box.

The customers matched the surroundings, since from 1864 Méert supplied King Leopold the First of Belgium, and later on General de Gaulle, who was born in Lille, had boxes of gaufres sent to the Élysée Palace.

[beat]

A famous patisserie can easily be treated as a museum, yet its achievement is quieter than that, because what's kept this place going is a single, very simple thing made in the same way for well over a century and a half.

Since 2010 there's been a branch in Paris too, but the gaufres taste best here, so take a box out onto Rue Esquermoise and wander up into the old town while they're fresh.
