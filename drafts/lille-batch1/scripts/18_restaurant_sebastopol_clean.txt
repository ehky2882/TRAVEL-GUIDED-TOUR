Restaurant Sébastopol

In 1903, with the city's main theatre lost to fire, Lille built itself a stand-in in just a hundred and three days, and the temporary theatre it put up is still standing on this square more than a century later.

Two buildings face each other across Place Sébastopol, and you'll want both in view: the Théâtre Sébastopol, the big, broad-fronted hall on the square, and, directly across from it at number one, the small restaurant that borrows its name, the Restaurant Sébastopol.

The theatre sets the scene, and it was designed by Léonce Hainiez, built on an empty plot that had been earmarked for a linen market, and opened on the thirtieth of November 1903 with two thousand seats, a figure since trimmed to about thirteen hundred and fifty for comfort.

The restaurant, by contrast, seats only around thirty and has been fully renovated into a warm, modern bistro. The chef, Anthony Dorard, took an unusual road to the stove, having started out as a pharmacy assistant before learning the trade through a series of mentors in eastern France, Lille and Bordeaux.

His cooking is the draw, because he likes to pair land and sea in ways that shouldn't work and somehow do, so you might find foie gras with cod, or black pudding with turbot. The short menu changes every week, with new dishes arriving each Thursday, and guest artists are regularly invited in.

[beat]

There's a nice symmetry in that, because on one side of the square stands a building meant to last a season that has lasted well over a hundred years, and on the other a kitchen that deliberately starts again every week, so permanence and reinvention are facing each other across the cobbles.

Once you've eaten, the Palais des Beaux-Arts is only a short stroll away on Place de la République, and its basement of model fortified towns makes a fine way to spend an afternoon.
