Opéra de Lille

On the night of the fifth of April 1903, Lille's old Grand Théâtre burned down, and it took the city twenty years, one architectural competition and a world war to put a new one in its place.

Find a spot on Place du Théâtre where you can see the whole of the pale stone palace with its columns and sculpted pediment, the Opéra de Lille. Next door, to the left as you face it, stands the Chamber of Commerce with its tall belfry.

Look up first at the pediment, the triangle at the top of the facade, where the sculptor Hippolyte Lefebvre placed Apollo, god of music and poetry, surrounded by the Muses. Lower down, carved reliefs by other sculptors fill out the allegory, so the front of the building announces its purpose before you've bought a ticket.

The architect was Louis-Marie Cordonnier, who won the 1907 competition, and the gift here is what you can't see. The ground was soft and damp, so he sank around one thousand and fifty piles some five metres down to hold the building up, and he wrapped a reinforced concrete frame in stone to make the new theatre fireproof.

The timing was cruel, too, because the building was finished in July 1914, just as war broke out, and the interior was left incomplete under occupation, so the official opening didn't come until October 1923. During the Second World War it was again requisitioned for German audiences, from 1941 until 1944.

[beat]

So this is a theatre built for peace that kept being overtaken by events, and that history gives its later revival real weight: closed for safety in 1998, it reopened in December 2003, just in time for Lille's year as European Capital of Culture.

Inside, the horseshoe auditorium seats a little over eleven hundred, and if you can't make a performance, Cordonnier's Chamber of Commerce next door deserves a long look too, since the same architect designed both.
