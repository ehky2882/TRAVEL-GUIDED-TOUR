Marshay de Vazem

The square underneath this market is named after a dance hall that closed in eighteen thirty five, which seems fitting for a place that still behaves, on a Sunday morning, like a party with groceries.

Plass de la noovel avontoor is where you're standing, and the thing to look for is the long covered hall of iron and glass at its edge, the al de vazem, with the open air stalls spread out around it. If it's a Tuesday, a Thursday or a Sunday morning, you'll know you're in the right place by the noise.

The hall went up in eighteen seventy three, designed by sayzar zholee with a nod to viktor baltar's great market halls in Paris, and it originally held two hundred and thirty two stalls, which gives you a sense of how much food this neighbourhood needed.

That need came quickly, because vazem only became part of Lille in eighteen fifty eight, when its fields were swallowed by textile factories and terraces of workers' housing, with grander bourgeois homes mixed in among them.

Then there's the name of the square, which goes back further still. In seventeen fifty eight a man named kordonyay opened a gang get here, an open air spot for drinking and dancing called the New Adventure, where people from very different walks of life rubbed shoulders, and although it was pulled down long ago, the name stuck.

On a busy Sunday as many as fifty thousand people pass through, and the stalls reflect a cosmopolitan neighbourhood: olives and dried fruit, pastries from around the Mediterranean, and the rotisserie chickens roasting over potatoes that many families carry home for lunch.

The noovel avontoor was a place where classes mixed over a drink, and more than two and a half centuries later the square is still doing that job, only now the meeting happens over a bag of clementines.

If you've time, seek out the nearby mayzon folee de vazem, a former textile factory reborn as an arts centre, which rounds off the neighbourhood's story neatly.
