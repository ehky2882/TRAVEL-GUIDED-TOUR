Atelyay Koomo

Koomo is the Japanese word for cloud, and the woman who gave this shop its name spent seven years as an architect before deciding she would rather fill rooms than design them.

Roo de lopeetal meeleetair is the street underfoot, and there are two shopfronts to find on it: atelyay koomo at number seven, and its younger sister, mayzon koomo, further along at number thirteen. Stand on the pavement between them and you have the whole enterprise in a single glance, the newer house having opened in February twenty twenty three.

The windows of number seven come first, and in them things tend to be small enough to carry home: notebooks and paper goods, children's toys, little plants in carefully matched pots, and bags. Aymeelee kah oh ree bessinoh opened it in twenty sixteen, mixing a Japanese taste for restraint with a Scandinavian fondness for pale wood and useful objects, and she has always leaned towards independent makers rather than big names.

Now look along to number thirteen, which turns the same eye on the rest of the home, kitchen, bathroom, living room and bedroom, so that the pair of shops reads like a floor plan that has been split across two addresses.

What sets the place apart, though, is the making, since under the banner of a club called Do It Together, the atelier has run hands on sessions in crochet, embroidery, weaving and graphic design, some of them for children as young as five.

And that, if you ask me, is the heart of it, because an architect is trained to think about how people actually live in a space, and in these two shops that training has quietly become a way of selling, in which buying a well made mug and learning to weave are treated as two halves of the same thing.

The idea has travelled as far as Brussels, where there are now two more koomo shops, but closer to hand roo de baytoon runs just round the corner, and a stroll north along it carries you towards the old heart of the city and the grahn plass.
