Place aux Oignons

Despite the name, nobody ever sold onions here, because the Place aux Oignons takes its title from a medieval mishearing: the Latin word for the lord's keep, the tower of the counts of Flanders that once stood nearby, slid over the centuries into something that sounded like the French for onions.

This small, cobbled square is the whole of it, tucked into the oldest part of Lille just a short walk from the cathedral, with restaurant terraces spilling across the stones and a ring of brick houses standing shoulder to shoulder around you.

The houses date mostly from the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries and are plainer than the showy Flemish fronts elsewhere in the old town. They were built for sayetteurs, weavers of a light woollen cloth, who set up their looms in the attics and in the cellars, where the damp air helped keep the threads supple.

Picture the square a century ago, when the weaving trade had gone and factory workers had crowded into the same buildings. By the middle of the twentieth century this was one of the poorest corners of the city, with courtyards where six or seven families might share a single tap and a single toilet.

The turnaround came slowly, since the square was brought into Lille's protected historic district in 1967, work began in the 1970s under the mayor Pierre Mauroy, and between 1985 and 1990 the houses were carefully rebuilt to keep their original character rather than being swept away.

That choice is the story here, because the city could easily have cleared the whole block, and instead it decided that these modest weavers' houses were worth saving, which is why one of Lille's most loved little squares is also one of its humblest.

When you've had your fill, follow the lanes back towards Notre-Dame-de-la-Treille, which stands on the very mound where that keep once rose, and see how close together the castle and the onions really were.
