La Vyay Boorss

In sixteen fifty one, Lille was a Spanish city, and its merchants had to write to King Philip the Fourth in Madrid for permission to build themselves a proper place to do business.

Make your way through one of the arched passages into the inner courtyard, and then turn slowly on the spot. A covered arcade runs all the way round you, lined with stalls and shelves of second hand books, and above it rise the brick and carved stone fronts of the building itself, with the grahn plass on one side of these walls and plass doo tay ahtr on the other.

The clever part is that what looks from outside like one grand palace is in fact twenty four separate houses, built side by side in sixteen fifty two and sixteen fifty three to a design by zhoolyan destray and arranged around this shared courtyard, so that each merchant had a house of his own while the trading happened in the middle.

Have a look at the decoration on the upper floors, which is laid on thickly in the Flemish manner, with carved figures, garlands of fruit and flowers, and lions of Flanders guarding the doorways. The colourful painted coats of arms above the upper windows are much newer than they look, since they honour the companies that helped pay for the restoration carried out between nineteen eighty nine and nineteen ninety eight.

Up on the bell tower, meanwhile, a figure of Mercury, the god of trade, keeps watch over the whole arrangement.

What I find rather lovely is how gently the building has changed its purpose. It was made for money, and these days its courtyard deals mainly in second hand books, flowers and games of chess, with tango dancers taking over on Sunday evenings in summer, so a monument to commerce has turned into one of the city's favourite places simply to linger.

Walk out on the far side and the opera house stands across the square, with the chamber of commerce and its tall belfry just beside it.
