# MIAMI W1 · 06 — The Tower That Printed the Boom
W1 · The Pitch · segment 6 · S1 · clean · 24 September 2026

---

Cross to the east side of Biscayne Boulevard, the bay side, and look back at the tower across the traffic. This is the side it was built to be seen from: when it was new, it could be picked out from miles away at sea.

In 1923 a former governor of Ohio named James M. Cox bought a small Miami newspaper. Three years earlier he had run for president at the top of the Democratic ticket, with Franklin D. Roosevelt as his running mate, and lost to Warren G. Harding. He came south, renamed the paper, and built it this headquarters facing the bay. The tower was dedicated on July twenty-fifth, 1925, and on the same day the paper put out an edition of five hundred and eight pages, thick with advertising for land: lots, subdivisions, whole towns that existed mostly on paper.

That edition is the key to reading the building. The boom ran on a simple machine. A promise was printed, the promise was bought, the price went up, and the next promise was printed. The presses downstairs were part of that machine, and the tower on top was its signboard, raised high enough for anyone arriving by water to see before they ever saw the town.

Within little more than a year the boom was faltering, and a hurricane in September of 1926 finished it; a great many of those advertised subdivisions never left the page. The newspaper survived, and so did the tower. Decades later, in the 1960s and early 1970s, it served as the federal center where arrivals from Cuba were helped to begin new lives, which is where its present name comes from, and a building raised to sell the city to newcomers spent a dozen years taking them in.

Stay on the bay side and walk south about three blocks, past the arena and the marketplace, to the bronze torch on its tall column at the edge of the park. The walk ends in Bayfront Park.
