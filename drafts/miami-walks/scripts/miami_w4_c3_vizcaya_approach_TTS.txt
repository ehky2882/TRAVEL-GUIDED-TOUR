# MIAMI W4 · C3 — The Vizcaya approach, from the South Miami Avenue gates

You're at the South Miami Avenue gates, and the walk arrives the way the workers did, from the Grove side. Behind these gates, from nineteen fourteen into the early nineteen twenties, a large workforce built a villa, a village and gardens for a man who would spend a handful of winters in them, and the museum's own research has traced records placing Bahamians among them as masons, gardeners and household staff. The soft local limestone in the garden walls and steps was cut and set by hands that, many mornings, had set out from Charles Avenue.

One of those workers has a name and a record. Eustace edge kum, born in the Bahamas, was hired while the house was still going up, before James deer ing had slept in it, and he stayed fifty two years, through the storms, the sale and the museum's opening. The museum has since found his grandson, part of a project to put names to the unidentified faces in its early photographs, and has sent staff to the islands for more.

Run the route backward in your mind, from these gates to the street where you began, and the Grove reads as one place with its work and its home at opposite ends of a road the workers built themselves. The estate is what that labor made for someone else; Charles Avenue is what it made for its own.

Whether you go in or turn for the station, you have walked the long way to work and the short street it came home to.
