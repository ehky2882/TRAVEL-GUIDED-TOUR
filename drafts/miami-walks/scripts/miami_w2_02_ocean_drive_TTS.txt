This time stay on the hotel side of the street, on the sidewalk beside the porches and caf ay tables, and instead of facing the row, look along it. From here the buildings stop being separate facades and become a rhythm: the same low height repeated block after block, the same short setback behind a raised front terrace, and the same flat roofline broken now and then by a small tower or a stepped cap.

One detail is worth fixing in your mind, because it comes back later in the walk. Over many of the windows runs a thin concrete ledge, sometimes a single strip and sometimes wrapped around a corner, built to keep the afternoon sun off the glass; the builders of the period called these eyebrows, and once you have noticed them, you will find them on nearly every building ahead.

The row has been on the National Register of Historic Places since the fourteenth of May, nineteen seventy nine, and the listing sounds like armor, though it never worked as one. A National Register listing is a recognition, a formal statement that a place matters to the nation's history, and on its own it places no limit on what a private owner does with private money, demolition included. The district's new status counted for a great deal on paper and very little against a wrecking crane, and within two years a large Deco hotel inside the district had come down on Collins Avenue, a few blocks north of where you stand.

So the row you are reading lengthwise survived something more fragile than a designation: a string of local fights, most of them lost, and a set of city rules rewritten after each loss until they finally held. The walk now takes you backward through those fights, from the most recent to the first.
