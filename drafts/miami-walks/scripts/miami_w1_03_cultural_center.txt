# MIAMI W1 · 03 — The Costume, Worn Again
W1 · The Pitch · segment 3 · S1 · clean · 24 September 2026

---

Stay at street level for this one, at the foot of the steps up to the Cultural Center's plaza, with the courthouse behind you across the avenue, and look at the colors: pale stone, terracotta-colored stucco, round arches in a long arcade. The building is from the early 1980s, and it is dressed as the 1920s.

Its architect was Philip Johnson, who had made his name with glass and steel. Asked for a library, a museum and a public square, he answered in the look Miami had claimed during the boom: the Mediterranean town of arcades, courtyards and warm walls, the same wardrobe the courthouse's neighbors had worn half a century before.

Why would a modernist reach backward? Because by then the costume belonged to the city. The boom had sold Miami to the country in stucco and arches, and the style had outlasted the bust, the Depression and a war; it was simply what a Miami civic building was expected to look like, and even a man famous for glass boxes took the hint.

You'll meet the originals over the next few stops: a movie palace, a church and a newspaper tower, all dressed from the same trunk within about a year of each other.

From here the walk heads east along Flagler Street past Miami Avenue, about a third of a mile, to a limestone tower on a dark stone base at the corner of Northeast Second Avenue: the Alfred I. duPont Building.
