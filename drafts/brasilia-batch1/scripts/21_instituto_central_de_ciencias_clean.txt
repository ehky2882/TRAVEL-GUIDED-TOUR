Instituto Central de Ciências

Students here call this building the Minhocão, the giant earthworm. That will make sense in about ten seconds.

Stand in the garden in the middle of the building, at the University of Brasília, Darcy Ribeiro campus, and look along it. There are two long, parallel wings of exposed concrete, low and dead straight, with a strip of garden running between them. They keep going, and going. The whole thing is roughly seven hundred metres long.

Oscar Niemeyer conceived it in 1963, and the work was developed with the university's own planning office, where the architect João Filgueiras Lima, known to everyone as Lelé, worked out how to build it. Building began in June 1963. It was finished in 1971, but the departments did not wait: psychology and biology moved in the moment the structure was standing.

Have a look at the concrete beams and frames. Much of it was made as identical prefabricated pieces and put together, rather than poured in place. That was very new in Brazil in the nineteen sixties, and it is the start of the story that made Lelé one of the country's great engineering architects. He would go on to build whole hospitals this way.

And here's the thing. A university in a planned capital could have been a cluster of grand pavilions. Instead the core of it is one enormous, repeating, deliberately ordinary building, a factory for teaching, with gardens down the middle and room to grow. Today it houses seven institutes and three faculties.

[beat]

Walk at least part of its length along the central garden. It is shaded, busy with students, and, I think, the best place in Brasília to feel what all that concrete was for. If you have more time, Lelé's hospital at Lago Norte, one of the buildings that grew out of this, is a short drive away on the lake shore.
