Kloobee doo Shoro de Brazeelya

Shoro is the music Reeoh de Zhanayro played before samba existed. And one of its most important homes is here, nearly a thousand kilometres inland, in a building by Oscar Neemyer.

Stand in front of the low, curved white building on the Cultural Promotion Sector, just west of the tee vee Tower. It is a semicircle, wrapped round an open courtyard. Inside are classrooms, a concert hall with a café, and four hundred and twenty seats.

The club began as an informal gathering of musicians. In nineteen sixty seven and nineteen sixty eight the great mandolin player Zhakob doo Bandoleeng spent time in Brazeelya and held evenings with local players. Nine years later, on the ninth of September nineteen seventy seven, they met at the home of the flautist Odette Ernest Jeeass and founded this club. Their first home was a disused cloakroom of the convention centre on this very spot.

By the late nineteen eighties it was falling apart, with equipment stolen and eviction on the cards. In nineteen ninety three, a mandolin player, Heko doo Bandoleeng, took it over and saved it. A music school followed, named after the guitarist Rafa ell Habello and said to be the first in Brazil devoted to shoro. And in November twenty eleven Neemyer's new building opened, on the site of the old cloakroom.

And here's the thing. Shoro is a music of small groups, a flute, a guitar, a mandolin, a tambourine, playing fast and close together, often in someone's front room. Brazeelya was built by people from all over Brazil, and this is one of the ways they brought home with them. In twenty twenty four shoro was recognised as part of Brazil's national cultural heritage, partly at this club's request.

In twenty twenty three Paul McCartney played a surprise concert here for about five hundred people. Check the programme and book ahead, as the hall is small. If you arrive early, sit in the courtyard and listen to the students practising.
