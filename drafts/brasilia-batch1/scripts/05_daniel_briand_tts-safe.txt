Daniel Bree on's father was a baker in France. At fifteen, Daniel had to choose between school and an apprenticeship in pastry. He chose pastry, and he has called it the turning point of his life.

You should be on the shopping street of Quadra one oh four North, the low row of shops that serves the blocks of flats around you, in front of Block A. Look for small tables on the pavement and, through the window, checked tablecloths, old mirrors and black and white photographs. That is the place.

It opened in nineteen ninety five. Bree on had married a Brazilian woman he met in Paris, and she wanted a French café in Brazil. He has said he started out working here without a permit, until the butcher next door rented him the shop. It has never moved. Some of his staff have been with him for sixteen or twenty years.

Start with the croissant, because that is the whole reason this place exists. The aim from the first day was a croissant in Brazeelya as good as one in Paris. At lunch the dish to order is duck leg confit, from the south west of France, with herbed potatoes, slices of apple and orange, and a green salad. And at Christmas there is a new boosh de no el every year.

And here's the thing. Loosio Costa planned every residential block in this city to have a small commercial strip like this one: a bakery, a pharmacy, a newsagent, all in walking distance of your front door. This café is that idea working as it was meant to: thirty years on the same corner, serving the same neighbours.

When you are done, walk into the block of flats behind the shops. Most of the buildings stand on columns, so you can cross underneath them, through the gardens, from one side of the super kwadra to the other. It is the best way to understand how people here actually live.
