## Clean script

Palácio Itamaraty

Its first name was the Palace of the Arches. Once you have seen it, you won't need to ask why.

Stand on the pavement of the Esplanada, opposite the Foreign Ministry, across its water garden. Ahead is a square glass building set back behind a colonnade of tall, slender concrete arches, all identical, all evenly spaced, rising straight out of a sheet of water. Floating on that water, in front of the entrance, is an abstract white sculpture.

The sculpture is Meteoro, the Meteor, by Bruno Giorgi. The water garden around it, and the gardens inside, are by Roberto Burle Marx. Niemeyer designed the palace, and Joaquim Cardozo did the structure, including, with the architect Milton Ramos, the great spiral staircase that curls up through the lobby.

The foundation stone went down in September 1960. The first official reception was in 1967, and the palace was formally opened in 1970. Inside, it is said to have the largest column free span in Latin America, about two thousand eight hundred square metres with no pillars at all. And it is full of art: Portinari, Volpi, Athos Bulcão, and around a hundred and fifty pieces of furniture by the designer Sergio Rodrigues.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is what this building is for. This is the Foreign Ministry, the house in which Brazil receives the rest of the world. It is the country's front room, and it was designed to make that first impression in total calm: no grand staircase outside, no flagpoles, just the arches, the water, and their reflection.

[beat]

You can see it for yourself. The ministry runs free guided tours, in English among other languages, and they need to be booked online in advance. If you can't get a place, walk east along this side of the Esplanada, under the trees, towards the Congress, and look back at the arches when the light is low and the reflections are at their sharpest.
