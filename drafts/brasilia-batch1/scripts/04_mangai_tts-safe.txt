Mangah ee

A restaurant from the far north east of Brazil, on the shore of a lake in the middle of the country. That is Brazeelya in a sentence.

Find the entrance on the lake side of the Sports Clubs Sector South, and stop before you go in. Ahead of you is a long, low building with the water behind it, and the arches of the Zhota Kah Bridge a little way down the shore. Inside, past the door, is a buffet that seems to go on for ever.

Mangah ee started in nineteen ninety in Paraeeba, in Brazil's north east, as a small shop selling produce from the sairtowng, the dry backlands. It grew into a chain that now runs from Zhwowng Pessoah and Natow to Heseefee and Sowng Powloo, and it calls itself the biggest restaurant of Brazilian cooking in Brazil. This lakeside branch, opened in two thousand and eight, was its first in the capital.

The buffet runs to more than two hundred dishes, priced by weight. Look for karnee de sol, beef salted and dried in the sun, often served on a hot griddle with melted kayzho de manchayga, a soft butter cheese. Then powng de makashayra, a bread made with cassava, and kooskooss, which here means a steamed corn cake, not the North African grain. Finish with kokahda, a coconut sweet, or ice cream served in half a coconut shell.

What's worth knowing is why this food is here at all. Brazeelya was built in the late nineteen fifties largely by workers from the north east, the kandangoos, who came by the tens of thousands and stayed. So this is not an exotic import. In a sense, it is the city's home cooking.

Come at lunchtime, take a table on the terrace and let the light move across the water. Afterwards, the lakeside paths lead along the shore towards the Zhota Kah Bridge, a good walk if the afternoon is not too hot.
