Bayroochee

People in Brazeelya have made a verb out of this bar. You don't go to the Bayroochee. You bayroochee.

Stand on the corner of the shopping street at Quadra one oh nine South, where the tables spill out under the trees. You will see the waiters before you see the sign: in their jackets, moving fast between plastic chairs. The shops on this strip are low and plain, and the bar takes up its corner as if it has always been there, which, for this city, it nearly has.

It opened on the sixteenth of April nineteen sixty six. Two brothers from Lebanon, Yoossef Sarkees Mahrahooree and Yoossef Sarkees Kahwye, had just arrived in Brazeelya and bought this corner shop to open an Arab restaurant. In December nineteen seventy, two of their waiters, brothers from Seeahrah called Bartoh and Sheeko, offered to buy them out. It has stayed with that family ever since. It is often called the oldest bar in Brazeelya still open.

Order a shoppee, which is a cold draught beer, and something from the Lebanese side of the menu, the esfeehas, small open pies, or the keebee. The house version of the keebee is called a keebayroochee.

And here's the thing. In a city planned down to the last kerb, nobody planned this. It grew out of a corner shop, and it became the place where every crowd in Brazeelya ends up at the same tables: students, civil servants, journalists, musicians. Henahtoo Hoosoo, of the band Lezheeowng Oorbahna, had a regular table here by a tree.

A second Bayroochee opened in twenty seventeen at one oh seven North, but this is the original. Before you leave, look at the walls inside, which are hung with newspaper stories about the bar itself. Then walk a few minutes west, through the super kwadra, and you reach the double you three South, the city's old main shopping avenue.
