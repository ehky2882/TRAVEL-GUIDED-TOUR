## Clean script

Igrejinha Nossa Senhora de Fátima

The first lady of Brazil made a promise. If her daughter recovered from her illness, she would build a church. The daughter recovered, and this is the church.

Stand on the grass beside the little church at Quadra 307 South, with the road behind you, facing its open front. It is tiny: one curved wall, open at the front, and over it a triangular roof slab that tilts upwards, held on three slim supports. Brasílians say it looks like a nun's hat, the starched, winged kind, and once you have heard that you can't unsee it.

The first lady was Sarah Kubitschek, the president's wife, and the daughter was Márcia. Oscar Niemeyer designed it and Joaquim Cardozo engineered it. The first stone went down in October 1957, and it was consecrated in June 1958, the first church of brick and concrete in the new capital. The story usually told is that it took a hundred days.

Now, walk round to the outside of that curved wall. It is covered in Athos Bulcão's tiles, blue and white, with a star and a dove repeated across them: the Star of Bethlehem and the dove of the Holy Spirit. It is one of the rare panels in his work that shows actual things rather than abstract shapes.

And here's the thing. Inside there were once frescoes by the painter Alfredo Volpi, of angels and flags. In the nineteen sixties, during a renovation, they were simply painted over in blue. In 2009 Francisco Galeno, who had studied with Volpi, was asked to paint the walls afresh, and he filled them with kites, flags and cotton reels, the things of everyday Brazilian life.

[beat]

This church was planned as the neighbourhood chapel for the superquadras around it. Walk into the superquadra next door, 308 South, the model superquadra, and you can see the whole idea at once: flats on columns, gardens by Burle Marx, a school, and this church, all within a few minutes' walk.
