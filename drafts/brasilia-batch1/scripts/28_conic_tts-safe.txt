Konnick

Loosio Costa wanted the heart of Brazeelya to have something like Times Square. This is what it turned into.

Stand in the open passage between the buildings of the Konnick, in the South Entertainment Sector, just beside the bus station. Around you is a cluster of blocky commercial buildings, stained concrete, signs stacked on top of signs, staircases and walkways going off in every direction. Across the road to the north, the sloping pyramid of the National Theatre.

Costa marked out this sector in nineteen fifty seven as the city's place of entertainment, cinemas, bars, theatres, all next to the bus station where everyone would pass. For a while, it worked. From the late nineteen sixties to the early eighties this was the cultural centre of Brazeelya. At its height the Konnick had thirteen buildings, ten bookshops, eight cinemas and six nightclubs.

Then the cinemas and the money moved out to the shopping centres, and the Konnick got a reputation. Look around you now, and you will find it all mixed together: evangelical churches upstairs from nightclubs, opticians and sex shops, record shops selling vinyl and turntables, skateboard shops, and screen printers selling t shirts. On some nights there is hip hop, reggae and drag, and there are fanzine fairs and em see battles.

And here's the thing. Brazeelya is a city where almost everything was planned, zoned and drawn in advance. The Konnick is the one place in the middle of it that slipped out of the plan, and so it is the one place where the city does whatever it likes. It has been called a stronghold of cultural resistance. To my mind, it is the most honest building in Brazeelya.

Keep an eye on your belongings, especially at night. By day, wander the galleries and look up at the painted murals. Then cross over to the National Theatre, and look back at the Konnick from its lawn, the official city and the unofficial one, facing each other across the road.
