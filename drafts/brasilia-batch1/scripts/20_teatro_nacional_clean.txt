Teatro Nacional Cláudio Santoro

The side of this building has a nickname. People call it O Sol faz a festa, the sun throws a party.

Stand on the lawn beside the theatre in the Cultural Sector North, just across the axis from the bus station, facing one of its long sloping sides. It is a pyramid with the top sliced off, a shape that recalls the temples of pre Columbian America. And the wall in front of you is covered in raised concrete blocks, in five different shapes, set in a pattern that seems at first to be random.

That wall is by Athos Bulcão, from 1966. It is about a hundred and twenty five metres long and twenty seven high, around seventeen hundred square metres of relief, and it is often described as the largest work of art built into a building anywhere in Brazil. The trick is the sun. As it moves, the blocks throw shadows of different lengths, so the wall looks different every hour of the day.

Oscar Niemeyer designed the building. Work began in 1960 and then stalled for years, and the full theatre only opened in 1981. Its main hall, the Villa Lobos, has around fourteen hundred seats. Inside the main foyer is a garden by Burle Marx, and works by Ceschiatti, Peretti and Athos again. It took the name of Cláudio Santoro, the composer and conductor who founded its orchestra, after his death in 1989.

And here's the thing. In 2014 the whole theatre was shut on safety grounds, and it stayed closed for about a decade. In December 2024 part of it reopened, starting with the smaller Martins Pena hall. The rest has been coming back slowly. A national theatre with its biggest stage dark for ten years is not the story anyone wanted. But the wall outside never closed.

Check the programme to see which halls are open. Then come back near sunset, and walk the full length of this side. The shadows on Athos's blocks are at their longest, and you will see why it got its name.
