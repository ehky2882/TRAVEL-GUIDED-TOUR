Kahza de Shah

At the very centre of Brazilian power there is a café, and most people walk straight over the top of it.

Stand on the Square of the Three Powers with the presidential palace, the Planowtoo, on one side, the Supreme Court on the other and Congress behind you. Look down rather than up. Set into the paving is a small, low pavilion, sunk half below the level of the square, so its roof barely rises above the stones.

Oscar Neemyer designed it in nineteen sixty five and nineteen sixty six, and it was finished in nineteen sixty seven. In his memoirs he called it a restaurant for the square. Because it is half buried, the people inside sit with their eyes at the height of the paving, looking out across the square as if from a hide. It opened in nineteen seventy five as a Chinese restaurant, closed in nineteen seventy nine, and spent most of the next forty years shut or hired out for events.

In June twenty twenty four it reopened as a café run by Senack, the national training service, and the kitchen is staffed partly by its cookery students. The menu leans on the Seh hahdoo, the savanna that surrounds Brazeelya. Look for peh kee, a strongly scented local fruit, and bah roo, a nut from the Seh hahdoo tree of that name. There are powng de kayzho, pamonya, a sweet corn parcel, and a tea they call say oo de Brazeelya, Brazeelya sky, which changes colour when you add a drop of lemon.

And here's the thing. Neemyer gave the most formal square in the country a place for nothing more important than a cup of tea, and then hid it, so that it never competes with the palaces around it. More than forty five thousand people came in its first hundred days.

Before you go in, look just beyond the café to the long white Pantheon of the Fatherland, a few steps away. Then, from your table, look out across the square at ground level. Few seats in Brazeelya give you that view of it.
