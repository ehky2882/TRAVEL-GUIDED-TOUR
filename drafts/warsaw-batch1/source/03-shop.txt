Vantage: on Marszałkowska at number ninety-nine, in front of the glass pavilion beside the Hotel Metropol; then round the corner on Nowogrodzka for the neon
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cepelia_Pavilion ; https://www.neonmuzeum.org/blog/cepelia---it--s-lit-- ; https://www.whitemad.pl/en/riddle-solved-empik-will-be-operating-in-the-cepelia-pavilion/ ; https://muzeumwarszawy.pl/en/obiekt/drawing-of-the-southern-facade-of-the-cepelia-commercial-pavilion/

For years this little building was so plastered in billboards that most people forgot there was architecture underneath. Then somebody peeled them off.

You should be on Marszałkowska, the city's great north–south avenue, at number ninety-nine, looking at a low pavilion of glass and metal. It sits beside the taller Hotel Metropol. Look at the ground floor and you'll see turquoise ceramic tiles.

It was finished in nineteen sixty-six, designed by Zygmunt Stępiński with Andrzej Milewski, as a shop for Cepelia. Cepelia was the state network of folk-art and craft cooperatives, set up after the war to sell Polish folk art and handicrafts. The pavilion was conceived as one big shop window. Here's the gift: the turquoise tiles are by the artist Stanisław Kucharski, and each one carries its own geometric pattern.

The nineteen-nineties were cruel to it. The windows vanished under advertising, a nightclub took the basement, and Cepelia sold the building. In twenty nineteen a fast-food chain wanted to move in. The heritage authorities said no, an appeal said yes, and in the end the chain walked away. The building was protected as a monument, restored by the architect Andrzej Chołdzyński, and reopened in December twenty twenty-four. It's now a branch of Empik, the country's big book and media chain.

[beat]

So, a fair warning: you won't find folk art for sale here any more. What you'll find is a rescued building doing a different job. And I think that's the right lesson. Warsaw spent the nineties and two thousands treating its modernist buildings as embarrassing leftovers. This pavilion is part of a slow change of heart. A city that lost so much of its old fabric is finally learning to value its younger buildings too.

Before you go in, walk round to the Nowogrodzka Street side, round the corner. Look for the neon cockerel, the old Cepelia emblem. And look up at the first floor, where a strip of the original wavy aluminium front has been left uncovered, like a window into nineteen sixty-six.

If you want to understand how Warsaw thinks about itself, don't start with a guidebook. Start with this shop.

You're on Mokotowska Street, at number sixty-five, in the city centre's south. Behind the shopfront is a bookshop, a small gallery, and the office of the foundation that runs them, all under one roof. The name is a bit of wordplay: bęc is the sound of something landing with a thump, and zmiana means change. Which tells you something about the spirit of the place.

The foundation was registered in two thousand and two by Bogna Świątkowska, who still leads it. Its job is to support art, architecture, design and urban culture, and a great deal of its work happens in public space rather than in galleries. It runs competitions for artists, architects and designers. It started a free magazine in two thousand and three, and it has run a free university of Warsaw, open to anyone.

Now, the gift, which is on the shelves. The foundation is also a publisher, with its own book series on contemporary art, graphic design, architecture and visual culture. Architecture and the city run all through the list. If you want a book on Polish architecture and design that you won't find at the airport, this is where you look.

[beat]

Here's why I think this matters. Warsaw had to be rebuilt by decree, from the top down, after the war. Places like this are the opposite. They're the city talking to itself from the bottom up, asking who public space belongs to, and what should be built next. It's a small shop with an outsized voice.

Have a browse, and ask about the events: there are often openings, talks and readings. Then walk west a few minutes towards Marszałkowska, and you'll find the city of grand avenues that so many of these books are arguing with.

=== 17 | pan-tu-nie-stal | Pan Tu Nie Stał | Pan Tu Nie Stał | 52.223017, 21.014392 | SHOP
Vantage: on Koszykowa Street at number thirty-four to fifty, outside the shop, in the arcade of the big nineteen-fifties block just off Plac Konstytucji

The name of this shop is a line every Pole of a certain age will recognise. "Pan tu nie stał." It means, "You weren't standing here, sir." It's what you shouted at someone pushing into a queue.

You're on Koszykowa Street, at numbers thirty-four to fifty, a few steps from Plac Konstytucji. Look up before you go in. This shop sits in one of the big, heavily decorated apartment blocks of the early nineteen-fifties, part of the socialist-realist district built around the square. The allegorical figures on this very building are by the sculptor Józef Gosławski.

The brand began in Łódź, a city an hour or so to the south-west, in two thousand and eight. Justyna Burzyńska and Maciek Lebiedowicz started a blog about the graphic design and typography of communist-era Poland. Readers loved it. They printed some T-shirts. They sold out. The shop followed, and this Warsaw branch opened in June twenty fourteen. The clothes are still designed and made in Łódź.

The gift is the joke in that name. The early nineteen-eighties, under martial law, were the years of empty shelves and endless queues. And here's a shop, selling things people actually want, named after the battle cry of the queue.

[beat]

What I like is the setting. The building around you was designed to show off the new socialist city. Seventy years on, it houses a shop that gently teases that era, using the lettering and graphics the era itself produced. That's how Warsaw tends to handle its past: not with a sledgehammer, but with a raised eyebrow.

Inside you'll find T-shirts, socks, mugs, ceramics, notebooks and postcards, a lot of it with old lettering and slogans. Then step out and walk the few metres to Plac Konstytucji, and look at the giant lamp standards and the carved workers on the façades. The originals of the joke are all around you.

=== 18 | hala-mirowska | Hala Mirowska | Hala Mirowska | 52.238837, 20.997370 | SHOP
Vantage: on Plac Mirowski, beside the brick halls, with a clear view of the west hall's façade
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir%C3%B3w_Halls
---
Look closely at the brickwork on these halls. Some of those marks aren't weathering. They're bullet holes, and they were kept on purpose.

You should be on Plac Mirowski, beside two long market halls of red brick, with tall arched windows. They stand side by side, east and west, full of stalls, shops and food counters, with more trade going on outside.

They were the city's first great covered market. The first stone was laid in October eighteen ninety-nine, and they opened in nineteen hundred and two, on the site of an old army barracks. More than five hundred stalls sold food and household goods, and before the war these were the largest market buildings in Warsaw.

Then the twentieth century arrived here very hard. In nineteen forty the ghetto wall was built right alongside. In August nineteen forty-four, in the first days of the Uprising, German troops seized the halls, and civilians from the neighbourhood were executed here. Both halls burned.

The afterlife is almost surreal. The east hall was patched up and used as a bus depot, and later it became a sports hall that hosted European boxing and world weightlifting championships. The west hall went back to being a market in nineteen sixty-two. In twenty eleven, when the west hall was restored, the bullet holes and damage on its walls were deliberately left in place.

[beat]

That decision, I think, is the right one, and it's a very Warsaw one. In a city that rebuilt its old town to look untouched, here's a building that's allowed to keep its scars. You can buy your apples and still read the history off the wall.

Walk the length of both halls outside, and look for the damage around the windows. Then go into the east hall: inside you'll find a small museum devoted to the boxing coach Feliks Stamm, a reminder of the years when these halls were full of shouting fans instead of shoppers.

=== 19 | bazar-rozyckiego | Bazar Różyckiego | Bazar Różyckiego | 52.252305, 21.040079 | SHOP
Vantage: at the market's gate on Targowa Street in Praga, looking into the rows of stalls and green metal pavilions
Sources: https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazar_R%C3%B3%C5%BCyckiego ; https://dzieje.pl/node/113059 ; https://www.national-geographic.pl/historia/bazarowe-eldorado/

For forty years, while the rest of the country ran on a planned economy, this market quietly ran on the free one. Everybody knew. Very few complained.

You're in Praga, on the east bank of the Vistula, at the gate of Bazar Różyckiego on Targowa Street. In front of you are rows of stalls and newer green pavilions of corrugated metal. Behind you is Targowa, one of Praga's main streets, with its trams.

The market is named after Julian Różycki, a pharmacist who bought this land in eighteen seventy-four. It opened officially in eighteen eighty-two. Here's the gift detail: around nineteen hundred and one, a kiosk was put up at the Targowa gate in the shape of a giant blue soda siphon, seven metres tall, selling the owner's soda water. It became the symbol of the place until it was removed in the nineteen-thirties.

The war years here are complicated. Under the occupation, the market was where you could buy what the Germans rationed. It was also where weapons sold off by German soldiers were bought up for the Polish underground. Most of the traders before the war were Jewish, around three hundred families, and in nineteen forty they were driven out into the ghetto.

[beat]

After the war, the communist state nationalised the market in nineteen fifty, and yet private trading carried on. In a country of state shops, this was an island of the free market: clothes, deals, and, people said, a section where you might buy back what had been stolen from you. Its hot tripe soup and its pyzy, the round potato dumplings, were famous.

Today it's in an odd, in-between state. Part of it belongs to the city, which has put up those green pavilions, and some of the new stalls sell vintage clothes and ceramics. Part has gone back to the founder's heirs. It may not look like much, but this is one of the most storied acres in Warsaw.

From here, head to Ząbkowska Street on the market's north side and walk east for a few minutes. You'll reach the red-brick vodka factory of Koneser, another of Praga's survivors, now reborn.
