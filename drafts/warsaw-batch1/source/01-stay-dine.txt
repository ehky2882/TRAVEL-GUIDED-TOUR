=== 01 | raffles-europejski | Raffles Europejski Warsaw | Raffles Europejski Warsaw | 52.241927, 21.015059 | STAY
Vantage: on the pavement of Krakowskie Przedmieście outside number thirteen, facing the hotel's long front; the Bristol is across the street behind you
Sources: https://www.raffles.com/warsaw/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Europejski ; https://www.luxurytraveladvisor.com/hotels/raffles-europejski-warsaw-opens-polish-capital
---
This building has been a hotel, a ruin, a military academy, a government office, a hotel again, and the subject of a twelve-year lawsuit. It's now a hotel once more, and I'd say it has earned the rest.

Stand on Krakowskie Przedmieście, the old Royal Route, with the long pale front of the Europejski in front of you. Behind you, across the street, is the Hotel Bristol. These two have been eyeing each other across this road for well over a century.

It opened on the first of January, eighteen fifty-seven, designed by Enrico Marconi, an architect of Italian family who did more than almost anyone to give nineteenth-century Warsaw its manners. In its day it was one of the grandest hotels in the Russian Empire. Then came the war. During the occupation it served Wehrmacht officers only, and when the Germans pulled out in the winter of nineteen forty-four, they left it a wreck.

Here's what I find rather wonderful. After the war, the new government took the building and turned it into a school for training army political officers. The grand staircase and the ballrooms were ripped out. Then, in the late fifties, somebody decided Warsaw needed its hotel back, and in nineteen sixty-two it reopened as a state-run Orbis hotel. The Rolling Stones, Marlene Dietrich, Lech Wałęsa: they all came through.

When communism ended, the heirs of the original owners went to court. It took them twelve years, and they won. The hotel closed in two thousand and five, and reopened under Raffles in twenty eighteen, with a hundred and six rooms.

[beat]

Go inside if you can, because the real gift is on the walls. Nearly five hundred works by more than a hundred and twenty Polish artists, chosen by Anda Rottenberg, once director of the Zachęta gallery, a short walk away. Tadeusz Kantor, Wilhelm Sasnal, Monika Sosnowska. It's quietly one of the better collections of Polish art in the city, and it lives in the corridors.

Before you leave, find Lourse, the pâtisserie on the ground floor. Warsaw's well-to-do took their tea and cakes under that name before the First World War. Order something sweet and you're keeping a very old appointment.

=== 02 | hotel-bristol | Hotel Bristol | Hotel Bristol, a Luxury Collection Hotel, Warsaw | 52.242404, 21.015724 | STAY
Vantage: on Krakowskie Przedmieście at the corner of Karowa, facing the hotel; the Europejski is across the street behind you
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Bristol,_Warsaw ; https://hoteldesigns.net/uncategorised/the-luxury-collection-hotels-resorts-debuts-in-poland-with-the-iconic-hotel-bristol-in-warsaw/
---
A concert pianist built this hotel. Not personally, with a trowel, but with his money. Ignacy Jan Paderewski, the most famous pianist of his age, bought most of the shares in the company behind it and gave the site to the project.

You're on Krakowskie Przedmieście, where Karowa Street drops away towards the river, looking at the Bristol's cream front and its corner dome. Turn round and the Europejski is right there across the road. Two grand hotels, by two members of one family. Enrico Marconi designed that one, and his son Władysław revised the plans for this one.

The story of the design is a small comedy. A competition was won by two architects from Kraków with an Art Nouveau scheme. Then the builders lost their nerve and switched to something safer, a neo-Renaissance front. So the outside is sober. The original interiors, though, were by Otto Wagner Junior, son of the great Viennese architect. The café opened in October nineteen hundred and one, the hotel a month later, with two hundred rooms and what the hotel's history calls the first lift in the country.

Now the gift. Look up at the balcony. On the second of January, nineteen nineteen, Paderewski came home to a newly independent Poland and spoke to the crowd from up there. He became Prime Minister, lived in this hotel, and resigned here at the end of that year. A hotel, briefly, doing the job of a state.

[beat]

And here's the remarkable thing. Most of this street was destroyed in nineteen forty-four. The Bristol survived the war and the Uprising largely intact, and reopened in nineteen forty-five. Then came nationalisation, a long state-run stretch, and in nineteen eighty-one it simply closed and stood empty for about a decade. Margaret Thatcher cut the ribbon on its reopening in nineteen ninety-three.

Marie Curie stayed here. So did Picasso, Marlene Dietrich and John F. Kennedy. The Café Bristol on the corner is the place to sit, with a slice of cake and a window onto the Royal Route. Then walk north a few minutes and you'll reach the Castle Square and the old town beyond it.

=== 03 | autor-rooms | Autor Rooms | Autor Rooms | 52.222336, 21.012410 | STAY
Vantage: on Lwowska Street outside number seventeen, a tall nineteenth-century tenement; the rooms are on an upper floor
Sources: https://www.wallpaper.com/travel/poland/warsaw/hotels/autor-rooms ; https://www.stylepark.com/en/news/autor-rooms-warsaw-boutique-minihotel ; https://www.mitmagazine.co.uk/Meeting-Event-Venues/Warsaw/Convention-Hotel/Autor-Rooms-p58319845

Most hotels begin with an investor. This one began with a group of graphic designers who couldn't find anywhere decent to put their friends.

Lwowska Street, outside number seventeen. You're looking at an ordinary-looking nineteenth-century tenement, the kind of building that survived in this pocket of the city centre when so much else didn't. There's no grand entrance and no doorman. Autor Rooms is one floor of a flat, somewhere above your head.

The people behind it run Mamastudio, a Warsaw design agency. Around twenty fifteen they took on this apartment, about two hundred square metres of it, and turned it into four bedrooms around one shared living room and kitchen, with the architect Mateusz Baumiller overseeing the work.

The gift is in the furnishing, because every single thing up there is Polish. The lights are handmade by Ultralight. The glasses, mirrors and even the door handles are by the designer Maria Jeglinska. The bedding is hand-dyed wool from Beza Projekt. The big oak table where everyone has breakfast was designed and built by a young pair of cabinetmakers called Segiet Oniszh. And if you fall for any of it, you can ask to buy it. The flat works as a showroom you happen to sleep in.

There's an honesty bar, a library, a terrace, and art on the walls that is for sale too.

[beat]

What I like about it is the scale. Warsaw is a city that has always been told to think big, with great avenues and great palaces. This is the opposite argument: four rooms, one table, and the idea that a city can be introduced to you by the people who make its everyday things.

You're also one street from the Faculty of Architecture of the Warsaw University of Technology, so expect students sketching nearby. Two minutes' walk away, on Koszykowa Street, you'll find Hala Koszyki, the old market hall that is now the area's dining room.

=== 04 | bar-mleczny-prasowy | Bar Mleczny Prasowy | Bar Mleczny Prasowy | 52.215796, 21.020592 | DINE
Vantage: on Marszałkowska Street at number ten to sixteen, facing the bar's glass front and neon sign; then inside at the counter
Sources: https://www.bryla.pl/bryla/1,85301,12662086,Nowy_Bar_Prasowy__wizualizacje_.html ; https://www.whitemad.pl/nowy-bar-prasowy-w-warszawie-drugie-wcielenie-legendarnego-baru-mlecznego/ ; https://freewalkingtour.com/milk-bars-in-warsaw-history-guidebook/

This milk bar is a year older than the Palace of Culture. And unlike the Palace, it was very nearly lost.

You should be on Marszałkowska, the long avenue that runs down the spine of the city centre, outside numbers ten to sixteen. Look for the glass front and the neon sign spelling Prasowy. Go in and stand by the counter, where the menu is written up on the wall.

A milk bar, bar mleczny, is a very Polish institution: a cheap canteen serving plain home cooking, originally meatless and dairy-heavy, hence the name. Prasowy opened in nineteen fifty-four and fed this neighbourhood for about half a century. In twenty eleven it closed, and that would usually be the end of the story.

It wasn't, because the neighbours made a fuss. Residents pressed the city until it ran a special competition for the premises, with one condition: whoever won had to keep it a milk bar. The rent was set at a fraction of what the shops next door paid. A new tenant took it on, and it reopened in twenty thirteen.

Now have a look around you, because the designers, Mikołaj Wojciechowski and Konrad Sojka, were careful about what to keep. Look down: part of the black-and-white mosaic floor is original. Look at the serving hatches where the plates come through. They stayed. And the menu on the wall is modelled on the old black board with its white letters.

[beat]

Here's why I'd send any visiting architect here before the museums. This place is a lesson in what preservation actually means. It isn't only a façade or a listed staircase. Sometimes the thing worth saving is a use: a room where anyone, of any income, can sit down to a plate of pierogi and a bowl of soup.

Order the pierogi, or whatever the soup of the day is, and pay at the till. Then walk north up Marszałkowska to Plac Konstytucji, a few minutes away, and you'll see the grand socialist housing district this little bar has outlived.

=== 05 | bez-gwiazdek | Bez Gwiazdek | Bez Gwiazdek | 52.241921, 21.021865 | DINE
Vantage: on Wiślana Street at number eight, a quiet side street in Powiśle running down towards the river
Sources: https://guide.michelin.com/at/en/masovia/warsaw/restaurant/bez-gwiazdek ; https://warsawinsider.pl/best-of-warsaw-2024-polish/ ; https://www.enprimeurclub.com/restaurants/bez-gwiazdek-warsaw-restaurant ; https://tablejourney.com/poland/warsaw/cuisine/polish-modern/
---
The name means "without stars". Which is a nicely dry thing to call a restaurant whose chef has worked in some of the most decorated kitchens in Europe.

Wiślana Street, number eight. You're in Powiśle, the old riverside district below the escarpment, on a small street that slopes down towards the Vistula. Bez Gwiazdek is a single, low-key room. Easy to walk past, which I suspect is rather the point.

The chef is Robert Trzópek, and the idea is simple and quite brave. There are sixteen regions in Poland. The menu changes roughly month by month, and each version takes you to one of them. One month it's Silesia, with karminadle, the region's meat patties. Another, it's the mountains of Podhale, and the smoked sheep's cheese called oscypek. The recipes are traditional. The technique is not.

Dinner is a tasting menu of around six courses, and there's a vegetarian version too. It's listed in the Michelin Guide, and it turns up on European lists of restaurants worth travelling for.

[beat]

What interests me here, as an architect, is the parallel with this city. Warsaw had to rebuild itself from drawings and memory after the war, and it chose to rebuild faithfully, but with modern materials and modern tools. This kitchen does exactly the same thing with the food of the whole country: faithful to the source, built with today's methods.

And there's something generous in it. Most Poles never get to eat their way round all sixteen regions. Come back in a few months and you'll eat in a different part of the country without leaving this room.

Book ahead; it's dinner only, Tuesday to Saturday. Afterwards, walk a couple of minutes up the street to Dobra and look for the University Library, with its green roof garden. From April to October the upper garden is open, and it looks right out over the river.

=== 06 | hala-koszyki | Hala Koszyki | Hala Koszyki | 52.222224, 21.011017 | DINE
Vantage: on Koszykowa Street, facing the arched brick gateway building of the hall, number sixty-three
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koszyki_Hall ; https://brickarchitecture.com/projects/hala-koszyki-jems-architekci ; https://eurobuildcee.com/en/magazine/2426-hala-koszyki-rises-again
---
Koszyki means "baskets", and the name has nothing to do with shopping. It comes from the wicker that was once used to shore up the city's old earth ramparts nearby.

You should be on Koszykowa Street, in front of the brick gateway building with its arched entrance and carved decoration. Look for the Warsaw mermaid among the carved cartouches. Behind this front is a long hall of steel and glass, and today, a food hall.

It opened on the second of March, nineteen hundred and nine, designed by the architect Juliusz Dzierżanowski, to give the growing south of the city a covered market. The decorated fronts on the street were the showpiece. The rest was much plainer than drawn.

In nineteen forty-four, during the Uprising, the hall burned. Its steel frame kept it standing, and it was rebuilt in nineteen forty-nine and listed as a monument in nineteen sixty-five.

Here's the uncomfortable part, and I think it matters. In two thousand and nine a developer bought the hall, and most of it was demolished. What survived were the two side wings and the northern wall along this street. A new owner then rebuilt it, to a design by the Warsaw practice JEMS Architekci, putting the original structural pieces back in. It reopened in October twenty sixteen.

[beat]

So is this the old market or a new one? Honestly, it's both, and that's the Warsaw condition. Almost everything here is a reconstruction of some kind. The real question is never "is it original?" but "was it done with care?" Walk inside, look up at the steel frame, and judge for yourself.

Inside, the hall is lined with counters, bars and restaurants, and it's loud and busy most evenings. Find the old steelwork overhead and follow it to the far end. Then, back on the street, look east along Koszykowa: the Faculty of Architecture is just along the road, so the students know this place very well.

=== 07 | elektrownia-powisle | Elektrownia Powiśle | Elektrownia Powiśle | 52.240774, 21.027832 | DINE
Vantage: on Dobra Street at number forty-two, in the square in front of the old boiler house; then inside the former engine room, now the food hall
Sources: https://warsawinsider.pl/elektrownia-powisle-opens/ ; https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/power-plant-a-multipurpose-complex-elektrownia-powisle-apa-wojciechowski-architekci ; https://dzieje.pl/node/140818
---
For more than a month in nineteen forty-four, the lights of a besieged city depended on the men who worked here.

Stand on Dobra Street, in front of the long brick halls of the old power station, with its big industrial windows. You're in Powiśle, the low ground by the Vistula, and the river is just beyond the buildings. The engine room is now a food hall. Go in, find a seat, and look up.

The plant opened in nineteen hundred and four as the city's first big power station. In nineteen fifty-five it got a new heating unit to keep a certain new building warm: the Palace of Culture, a mile or so to the west. It worked for nearly a century, and shut down in two thousand and one.

The gift, though, is the war. When the Warsaw Uprising broke out in August nineteen forty-four, sixty-three of the plant's own workers formed a fighting unit, under Captain Stanisław Skibniewski, known as Cubryna. They took the plant and held it, and kept electricity flowing to the insurgents' city. Twenty of them were killed. It finally fell on the fourth of September.

[beat]

So when you're sitting here with a bowl of noodles under the steel trusses, it's worth knowing that this building was once a front line. The conversion, finished in twenty twenty by APA Wojciechowski Architekci, kept the heavy bones: the steel supports, the brickwork, old neon signs, and thousands of preserved fittings. Around them came offices, flats and a hotel.

Is it a little polished for a power station? Perhaps. But it's honest about what it was, and the space under that roof is still quite something.

Before you leave, walk round the outside of the boiler house and look at the brickwork up close. Then head two minutes north along Dobra to the University Library, whose roof garden looks straight down over this whole quarter.
