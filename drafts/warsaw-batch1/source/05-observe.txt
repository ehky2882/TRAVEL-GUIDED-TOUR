=== 32 | rynek-starego-miasta | The Old Town Market Square | Rynek Starego Miasta | 52.249795, 21.011799 | OBSERVE
Vantage: in the middle of the Old Town Market Square, beside the mermaid statue, turning slowly to take in all four sides
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Town_Market_Place,_Warsaw ; https://clippings.devonzuegel.com/post/story-of-cities-28-how-postwar-warsaw-was-rebuilt-using-18th-century-paintings-cities-the-guardian ; https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/canaletto-room-royal-palace ; https://theculturetrip.com/europe/poland/articles/how-old-is-warsaws-old-town-actually
---
Almost nothing you can see here is older than about seventy-five years. And it's on the World Heritage list precisely because of that.

Stand in the middle of the square, by the statue of the mermaid. Around you, four sides of tall, narrow, brightly painted houses, each one different. Turn slowly. Each side has a name, after an eighteenth-century parliamentarian: Dekert on the north, Barss on the east, Kołłątaj on the west, and Zakrzewski on the south.

In the autumn of nineteen forty-four, after the Warsaw Uprising was crushed, the German army set about destroying the city deliberately, building by building. This square was blown up. When the war ended, the old town was a field of rubble.

And then the city did something extraordinary. Between nineteen forty-eight and nineteen fifty-three, it rebuilt this square to look as it had in the seventeenth century. Here's the gift. One of the key sources was a set of paintings by Bernardo Bellotto, an eighteenth-century Italian painter who painted Warsaw in minute detail. His views were used as references, and in some cases they were the only record of buildings that had vanished completely.

[beat]

There's a twist, though, and I love it. Bellotto wasn't a camera. He took artistic liberties, tidying and improving as painters do. So the city that was rebuilt is partly his idealised Warsaw. Look around you: in a sense you're standing inside an eighteenth-century painting that was turned back into a city.

That's why UNESCO listed the historic centre in nineteen eighty. Not for its age, but as a near-total reconstruction, an act of collective memory on a heroic scale.

The mermaid has been the city's emblem for a very long time; Konstanty Hegel's statue of her dates back to eighteen fifty-five. Before you go, walk to the Royal Castle, a few minutes south, and find the Canaletto Room. That's where Bellotto's paintings hang today. Stand in front of them, and you'll see the blueprints for the square you've just left.

=== 33 | plac-konstytucji | Plac Konstytucji and the MDM | Plac Konstytucji | 52.221690, 21.016461 | OBSERVE
Vantage: on Plac Konstytucji beside one of the giant lamp standards, looking around the square at the heavy stone façades
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Square,_Warsaw ; https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsza%C5%82kowska_Dzielnica_Mieszkaniowa
---
This square was designed to make you feel the future had arrived, and that it was built of granite.

You're on Plac Konstytucji, Constitution Square, beside one of three gigantic lamp standards, each with a cluster of lanterns. Around you, tall apartment blocks of heavy stone, with arcades at street level and sculpted figures of workers on their fronts. Marszałkowska runs straight through the middle.

This is the heart of the MDM, the Marszałkowska Housing District. It was built between nineteen fifty and nineteen fifty-two, by a team led by Józef Sigalin and Stanisław Jankowski, with Jan Knothe and Zygmunt Stępiński. The square was opened on the twenty-second of July, nineteen fifty-two, the same day the new communist constitution was adopted. Hence the name.

Look up at the reliefs. There are dozens of them across the district: builders, workers, mothers and children, rendered in heroic stone. This was socialist realism, the official style of the moment. The MDM was launched under a slogan promising that "the people will enter the city centre". Ordinary workers would live on the grandest avenue in the capital.

Now here's the gift. Those three huge lamp standards aren't just lamps. The planners realised that the view south from the square was lopsided and spoiled the symmetry. So they put up three giant candelabra to restore the balance. Pure stage scenery.

[beat]

It's easy to sneer at all this. But stand here a while and you may find it's actually rather a good urban space: generous pavements, arcades for shelter, shops on the ground floor, people living above. What it got wrong was the politics. What it got right, quite often, was the city.

Walk a couple of minutes to Koszykowa Street and look at the building at numbers thirty-four to fifty, with allegorical figures by the sculptor Józef Gosławski. Then look out for the Hotel MDM on the square. It was the first hotel to open in Warsaw after the war.

=== 34 | za-zelazna-brama | The Za Żelazną Bramą estate | Osiedle Za Żelazną Bramą | 52.238118, 20.999971 | OBSERVE
Vantage: among the long sixteen-storey blocks of the estate, north-west of the Palace of Culture, looking along one of the slabs with the Palace's spire beyond
Sources: https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osiedle_Za_%C5%BBelazn%C4%85_Bram%C4%85
---
Nineteen enormous blocks, sixteen storeys each, and more than twenty-five thousand people. This is what a socialist city centre was supposed to look like.

You're in the estate called Za Żelazną Bramą, "Behind the Iron Gate", just north-west of the Palace of Culture. Around you are long, flat-fronted apartment blocks, each a huge horizontal slab, set in rows with green space between them. Look between two of them and you'll probably catch the spire of the Palace in the gap.

The name comes from the old Iron Gate that once led into the Saxon Garden, nearby. The design came out of a competition in nineteen sixty-one, won by a group of graduates of the Warsaw University of Technology's Faculty of Architecture, among them Jerzy Czyż, Jan Furman and Andrzej Skopiński. It was built between nineteen sixty-five and nineteen seventy-two.

Here's the gift, and it surprises even Varsovians. Almost everyone assumes these are prefabricated panel blocks, the classic communist kind. They're not. They were cast in place, poured in concrete into formwork on site. Solid, monolithic buildings.

And another surprise. The architects' original competition design had eleven-storey blocks. The authorities pushed for taller ones, to squeeze in more people. The planners expected around eighteen thousand residents. More than twenty-five thousand live here.

[beat]

So what do you make of it? For many visitors this is the bleak image of the East. But walk through on a summer afternoon and you'll see trees, playgrounds, and people living ordinary lives in flats that are a few minutes' walk from the very centre of a capital city. That's something plenty of Western cities never managed for their working families. The blocks are hard on the eye. The idea behind them was not so foolish.

Walk south-east through the estate towards the Palace, then look back. The rhythm of the slabs from a distance is the essential Warsaw view: modernist utopia in the foreground, Stalin's palace behind.

=== 35 | ulica-katowicka | Katowicka Street, Saska Kępa | Katowicka | 52.231889, 21.052497 | OBSERVE
Vantage: on Katowicka Street in Saska Kępa, outside numbers nine to eleven, looking at the white villa with its ribbon windows
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katowicka_Street ; https://muratordom.pl/prezentacje-domow/dom-trojrodzinny-dwoch-wybitnych-architektow-z-przodu-jak-twierdza-z-tylu-otwarty-na-ogrod-aa-BJgi-ZjwU-nxp4.html ; https://culture.pl/en/artist/jozef-szanajca ; https://warsawinsider.pl/neighborhood-prowl-saska-kepa/
---
Most of central Warsaw had to be rebuilt after the war. This quiet street on the far bank still has its interwar houses, and that makes it a time capsule of the city's most hopeful years.

You're in Saska Kępa, a leafy district of villas on the east bank of the Vistula, on Katowicka Street. In front of you, at numbers nine to eleven, a crisp, plain-walled building with long horizontal windows. Look along the street and you'll see more: flat roofs, plain walls, clean geometry, set in gardens.

This street is a small catalogue of Polish interwar modernism. Thirteen of its buildings are on the heritage register. The one in front of you is by Bohdan Lachert and Józef Szanajca, built in nineteen twenty-eight and twenty-nine. It's a house for three families, and Lachert's own father paid for it.

Here's the gift. The house follows Le Corbusier's famous five points of modern architecture: walls freed from the structure, open plans, long ribbon windows. And one Polish architecture writer put it beautifully: from the front it looks like a fortress, and from the back it opens up completely to the garden.

[beat]

Walk on, and the names multiply. There's a villa by Szymon and Helena Syrkus, at number twenty-six, two of the most important figures of the Polish avant-garde. There are houses by Stanisław Barylski, Henryk Oderfeld and Marian Lalewicz, and a row of houses from nineteen twenty-six by Włodzimierz Gall. Several of the architects were linked to Praesens, the radical modernist group of the period.

What strikes me, every time, is the optimism. These architects were building for a new country, independent for the first time in over a century, and they believed design could change how people lived. Within twenty years the war would take almost everything. But not this street.

Walk the whole length of Katowicka, slowly, and look over the fences. Then wander the neighbouring streets of Saska Kępa: the quarter is full of cafés, and many more villas of the same era.

=== 36 | bulwary-wislane | The Vistula Boulevards | Bulwar Flotylli Wiślanej | 52.230966, 21.042417 | OBSERVE
Vantage: on the left-bank riverside promenade, on the wide steps going down towards the water, looking across the Vistula to the far bank
Sources: https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulwary_wi%C5%9Blane_w_Warszawie ; https://pik.warszawa.pl/miejsca/bulwary-wislane ; https://sarp.warszawa.pl/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Konkurs_Bulwary_ogloszenie_wynikow_prezentacja_popr.pdf ; https://www.wprost.pl/306719
---
For decades, Warsaw had a beautiful river and turned its back on it. Then, about ten years ago, it turned round.

You're on the Vistula boulevards, the long riverside promenade on the city side of the river. Find a place on the wide steps that lead down towards the water, and look across. On the far bank, almost no city at all: trees, sandbanks, wild-looking beaches. Behind you, the busy embankment road and the city rising up the escarpment.

That contrast is the gift. For a river running through a capital city, the Vistula has been left remarkably wild, and here the east bank was largely left to nature. So you sit on a city promenade, and look at what is practically a wilderness.

For a long time, this side was no fun at all. In the nineteen-seventies the riverbank was given concrete boulevards, but a busy road cut them off from the city, and few people came. Then the boulevards were rebuilt in stages, to a design by the landscape architects RS Architektura Krajobrazu. The sections opened one after another: a first stretch in twenty fifteen, more in the summer of twenty seventeen, and a section near the mermaid in twenty nineteen.

[beat]

Look down the steps before you go. Some of the old nineteen-seventies concrete steps survived the rebuild, and on them you can still find inscriptions scratched or painted in the communist years, some of them distinctly anti-communist. A little piece of the city's argument with itself, right at the water's edge.

What changed here wasn't just paving. A city that had spent its post-war years facing inward, towards its avenues and its monuments, discovered it had a river. On a summer evening, these steps are full: people sitting with drinks, cyclists, music, the sun dropping behind the city.

Walk north along the river towards the Copernicus Science Centre, and look for the boulevard sections named after the Vistula Flotilla, General Patton and Jan Karski. Stay until sunset if you can, when the light comes down across the city behind you onto the far bank.

=== 37 | zlota-44 | Złota 44 | Złota 44 | 52.231214, 21.002445 | OBSERVE
Vantage: on the square in front of the Palace of Culture's western side, looking west across Emilii Plater Street at the curving glass tower
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C5%82ota_44 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Libeskind
---
The architect of this tower was born in Poland, the son of Holocaust survivors. He left as a child, and came back half a century later to put one of the tallest towers in the city right next to Stalin's Palace.

Stand on the western side of the Palace of Culture and look across the road. You'll see a tower of glass and steel with a long, curving edge that sweeps up one side like a blade, or a sail. That's Złota 44, named after its address on Złota Street, Golden Street.

It was designed by Daniel Libeskind, born in Łódź in nineteen forty-six. His family left Poland when he was a boy, and he went on to design the Jewish Museum in Berlin and the master plan for the World Trade Center site in New York. This tower is a hundred and ninety-two metres tall, with fifty-two storeys of flats.

Here's the gift. Libeskind said the shape was inspired by an eagle's wing, a symbol of change and freedom, and of Poland itself, whose emblem is a white eagle. Varsovians, characteristically, call it Żagiel, "the sail".

The tower had a rough birth. Work began in two thousand and seven, then stalled in two thousand and nine, and the half-built skeleton stood here for well over a year like a broken promise. It was finally finished in the middle of the next decade. Inside, there's a twenty-five-metre swimming pool and a wine cellar for ten thousand bottles.

[beat]

Here's why I wanted you to see it from right here. Turn round and look at the Palace. Then back at Złota 44. In one glance, you have the two big stories of this city in the twentieth century and after: the tower that was imposed on Warsaw from Moscow, and the tower of a city that chose to join the global market. Neither of them is modest. Both are, in their way, about who gets to shape the skyline.

From here, cross over to Złota Street itself for a closer look at the base of the tower, or head back into the Palace for the Neon Museum on the fourth floor.
