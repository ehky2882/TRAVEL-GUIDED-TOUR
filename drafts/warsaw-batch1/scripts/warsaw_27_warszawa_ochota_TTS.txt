var sha va o ho ta station

This may be the most beautiful thing in Warsaw that hardly anyone looks at, and plenty of people walk past it every day.

You're at plats za vee shi, the busy junction where alayeh yeh ro zo leem skyeh crosses to va ro va Street. Beside you, sitting on the edge of the railway cutting, is a small ticket pavilion. Look at its roof: a thin concrete shell that sweeps up at the corners and dips in the middle, like a saddle, or a piece of paper lifted by the wind.

That shape has a name: a hyperbolic paraboloid. The gift is how little holds it up. The whole roof rests on just three supports. The engineer was vats waff za leff skee, who also worked on the famous saucer shaped spo dek arena in ka to vee tseh. The architects were ar sen yoosh roman ovich and pyotr shi man yak, and it was built in nineteen sixty two and sixty three, for the city's cross town railway line.

roman ovich designed a whole family of stations in Warsaw: po veesh leh, both shrood myesh cheh stations, stad yon, fs hod nya. Before the war, at twenty eight, he'd co designed Poland's pavilion at the New York World's Fair. These little buildings were where post war modernism let itself be light hearted.

Here's what I want you to take away. In a city full of monuments built to impress, the most inventive architecture is often the most modest. This pavilion is tiny, it sits by a traffic junction, and it does something structurally daring with almost nothing. It's now protected as a monument, and rightly so. The register covers not just the pavilion but the platforms, the canopies and the footbridges too.

Go down to the platforms and look back up at the roof from underneath. Then, if you have time, take a train a couple of stops east along the line to var sha va po veesh leh, and compare this roof with its upside down cousin.
