## Script

Most of central Warsaw had to be rebuilt after the war. This quiet street on the far bank still has its interwar houses, and that makes it a time capsule of the city's most hopeful years.

You're in Saska Kępa, a leafy district of villas on the east bank of the Vistula, on Katowicka Street. In front of you, at numbers nine to eleven, a crisp, plain-walled building with long horizontal windows. Look along the street and you'll see more: flat roofs, plain walls, clean geometry, set in gardens.

This street is a small catalogue of Polish interwar modernism. Thirteen of its buildings are on the heritage register. The one in front of you is by Bohdan Lachert and Józef Szanajca, built in nineteen twenty-eight and twenty-nine. It's a house for three families, and Lachert's own father paid for it.

Here's the gift. The house follows Le Corbusier's famous five points of modern architecture: walls freed from the structure, open plans, long ribbon windows. And one Polish architecture writer put it beautifully: from the front it looks like a fortress, and from the back it opens up completely to the garden.

*[beat]*

Walk on, and the names multiply. There's a villa by Szymon and Helena Syrkus, at number twenty-six, two of the most important figures of the Polish avant-garde. There are houses by Stanisław Barylski, Henryk Oderfeld and Marian Lalewicz, and a row of houses from nineteen twenty-six by Włodzimierz Gall. Several of the architects were linked to Praesens, the radical modernist group of the period.

What strikes me, every time, is the optimism. These architects were building for a new country, independent for the first time in over a century, and they believed design could change how people lived. Within twenty years the war would take almost everything. But not this street.

Walk the whole length of Katowicka, slowly, and look over the fences. Then wander the neighbouring streets of Saska Kępa: the quarter is full of cafés, and many more villas of the same era.
