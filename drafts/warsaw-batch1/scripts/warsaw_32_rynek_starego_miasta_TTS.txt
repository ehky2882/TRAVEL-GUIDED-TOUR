The Old Town Market Square

Almost nothing you can see here is older than about seventy five years. And it's on the World Heritage list precisely because of that.

Stand in the middle of the square, by the statue of the mermaid. Around you, four sides of tall, narrow, brightly painted houses, each one different. Turn slowly. Each side has a name, after an eighteenth century parliamentarian: deh kert on the north, barss on the east, ko won tai on the west, and za kshef skee on the south.

In the autumn of nineteen forty four, after the Warsaw Uprising was crushed, the German army set about destroying the city deliberately, building by building. This square was blown up. When the war ended, the old town was a field of rubble.

And then the city did something extraordinary. Between nineteen forty eight and nineteen fifty three, it rebuilt this square to look as it had in the seventeenth century. Here's the gift. One of the key sources was a set of paintings by bernardo bellotto, an eighteenth century Italian painter who painted Warsaw in minute detail. His views were used as references, and in some cases they were the only record of buildings that had vanished completely.

There's a twist, though, and I love it. Bellotto wasn't a camera. He took artistic liberties, tidying and improving as painters do. So the city that was rebuilt is partly his idealised Warsaw. Look around you: in a sense you're standing inside an eighteenth century painting that was turned back into a city.

That's why yoo nesko listed the historic centre in nineteen eighty. Not for its age, but as a near total reconstruction, an act of collective memory on a heroic scale.

The mermaid has been the city's emblem for a very long time; kon stanti hegel's statue of her dates back to eighteen fifty five. Before you go, walk to the Royal Castle, a few minutes south, and find the Canaletto Room. That's where Bellotto's paintings hang today. Stand in front of them, and you'll see the blueprints for the square you've just left.
