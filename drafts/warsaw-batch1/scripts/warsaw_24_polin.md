## Script

Polin. It's a Hebrew word, and it can be read two ways. It means "Poland". And, according to an old legend, it also means "rest here".

You're in Muranów, standing on the open square between two things. Behind you, the monument to the heroes of the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. In front of you, a pale, almost weightless box of glass fins and copper mesh. Look closely at the glass and you'll see the word POLIN printed into it, in both Latin and Hebrew letters.

The legend goes like this. When Jews fleeing persecution first arrived in this country, they heard a voice, or saw a sign, saying "Po lin", "rest here". So they stayed. And for nearly a thousand years, Poland was home to one of the largest Jewish communities in the world.

The building was designed by the Finnish architects Rainer Mahlamäki and Ilmari Lahdelma, who won the competition in two thousand and five. It opened in April twenty thirteen, and the main exhibition followed in October twenty fourteen. It was named European Museum of the Year in twenty sixteen.

*[beat]*

Now, this ground. Everything around you was the Warsaw Ghetto. The neighbourhood you're standing in was rebuilt after the war on top of its ruins. So the architects had a nearly impossible brief: a museum of a thousand years of life, on a site of death.

Their answer is inside. Go in, and the entrance hall splits the building in two with a towering, curving wall, like a gorge cut through rock. It stands for the rupture in the history of Polish Jews. Some see in it the parting of the Red Sea. And the museum's story isn't only about the end. It's about the thousand years before it.

Spend time with the main exhibition if you can; it deserves hours. When you come out, walk slowly round the monument. Then turn back to the museum and find the word Polin in the glass, in Hebrew letters, catching the light.
