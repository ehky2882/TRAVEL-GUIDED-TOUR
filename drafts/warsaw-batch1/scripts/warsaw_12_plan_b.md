## Script

On a warm night in Warsaw, the bar isn't really the bar. It's the pavement outside it.

You're standing on Plac Zbawiciela, below the windows of Plan B, at number eighteen Aleja Wyzwolenia. The bar is upstairs. Down here, the edge of the square fills with people holding drinks, perched on steps and kerbs. In front of you is the round square with its traffic, and the twin towers of the Church of the Holiest Saviour across the way.

Plan B is, in the words of the local city magazine, a dive bar with cult status: low light, strong drinks, loud music, and a crowd of students and seasoned night owls. It may well have been the first bar on this square, years before the cafés made it fashionable.

Now look at the middle of the square, because this is where the story gets good. In twenty twelve the artist Julita Wójcik's rainbow arrived here: a steel arch covered in artificial flowers, which had already stood in front of the European Parliament in Brussels. When it was rebuilt after one attack, more than twenty-two thousand flowers went back onto it. To some people it was a cheerful work of art. To others it was a provocation, sitting in front of a church. It was set on fire more than once, repaired, and attacked again. In twenty fifteen it was finally taken down for good.

*[beat]*

So this little circle of tarmac has hosted one of the sharpest public arguments in modern Poland: about art, faith, tolerance, and who gets to decide what a public square is for. And through all of it, people kept sitting on these steps with a drink, talking it over. That, if you ask me, is exactly what a square is for.

The rainbow hasn't come back, despite campaigns to return it. But keep an eye on the planters in the middle of the square in summer. The city has talked of a flowerbed in rainbow colours as a quieter nod to it. Then head up the stairs and see what the noise is about.
