## Script

Look closely at the brickwork on these halls. Some of those marks aren't weathering. They're bullet holes, and they were kept on purpose.

You should be on Plac Mirowski, beside two long market halls of red brick, with tall arched windows. They stand side by side, east and west, full of stalls, shops and food counters, with more trade going on outside.

They were the city's first great covered market. The first stone was laid in October eighteen ninety-nine, and they opened in nineteen hundred and two, on the site of an old army barracks. More than five hundred stalls sold food and household goods, and before the war these were the largest market buildings in Warsaw.

Then the twentieth century arrived here very hard. In nineteen forty the ghetto wall was built right alongside. In August nineteen forty-four, in the first days of the Uprising, German troops seized the halls, and civilians from the neighbourhood were executed here. Both halls burned.

The afterlife is almost surreal. The east hall was patched up and used as a bus depot, and later it became a sports hall that hosted European boxing and world weightlifting championships. The west hall went back to being a market in nineteen sixty-two. In twenty eleven, when the west hall was restored, the bullet holes and damage on its walls were deliberately left in place.

*[beat]*

That decision, I think, is the right one, and it's a very Warsaw one. In a city that rebuilt its old town to look untouched, here's a building that's allowed to keep its scars. You can buy your apples and still read the history off the wall.

Walk the length of both halls outside, and look for the damage around the windows. Then go into the east hall: inside you'll find a small museum devoted to the boxing coach Feliks Stamm, a reminder of the years when these halls were full of shouting fans instead of shoppers.
