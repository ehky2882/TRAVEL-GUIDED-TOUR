plats kon sti toots yee and the em dee em

This square was designed to make you feel the future had arrived, and that it was built of granite.

You're on plats kon sti toots yee, Constitution Square, beside one of three gigantic lamp standards, each with a cluster of lanterns. Around you, tall apartment blocks of heavy stone, with arcades at street level and sculpted figures of workers on their fronts. mar shaw koff ska runs straight through the middle.

This is the heart of the em dee em, the mar shaw koff ska Housing District. It was built between nineteen fifty and nineteen fifty two, by a team led by yoo zef see ga leen and stah nee swaff yan koff skee, with yan kno teh and zig moont stem peen skee. The square was opened on the twenty second of July, nineteen fifty two, the same day the new communist constitution was adopted. Hence the name.

Look up at the reliefs. There are dozens of them across the district: builders, workers, mothers and children, rendered in heroic stone. This was socialist realism, the official style of the moment. The em dee em was launched under a slogan promising that the people will enter the city centre. Ordinary workers would live on the grandest avenue in the capital.

Now here's the gift. Those three huge lamp standards aren't just lamps. The planners realised that the view south from the square was lopsided and spoiled the symmetry. So they put up three giant candelabra to restore the balance. Pure stage scenery.

It's easy to sneer at all this. But stand here a while and you may find it's actually rather a good urban space: generous pavements, arcades for shelter, shops on the ground floor, people living above. What it got wrong was the politics. What it got right, quite often, was the city.

Walk a couple of minutes to koshi kova Street and look at the building at numbers thirty four to fifty, with allegorical figures by the sculptor yoo zef go swaff skee. Then look out for the Hotel em dee em on the square. It was the first hotel to open in Warsaw after the war.
