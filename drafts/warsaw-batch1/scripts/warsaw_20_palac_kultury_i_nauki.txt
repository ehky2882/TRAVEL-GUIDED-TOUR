WARSAW — 20 — THE PALACE OF CULTURE AND SCIENCE
VISIT · single stop · British register · ~354 words (~2.4 min)
Google Maps: Pałac Kultury i Nauki · 52.231766, 21.005798
Vantage: on Plac Defilad in front of the Palace's main steps on the Marszałkowska side, looking up at the tower
---
There's an old Warsaw joke that the best view of the city is from the top of the Palace of Culture, because it's the only place in Warsaw where you can't see the Palace of Culture.

Stand on the big open square in front of the main steps, with the traffic of Marszałkowska behind you, and tip your head back. In front of you, a stepped tower of pale stone, rising in tiers to a spire. On either side, long wings with colonnades, statues and turrets. It's hard to miss. That has always been the problem.

It was a gift from the Soviet Union, designed by the Soviet architect Lev Rudnev in the manner of Moscow's great Stalin-era towers. Construction began in May nineteen fifty-two and finished in July nineteen fifty-five. Thousands of Soviet workers built it alongside Polish ones, and sixteen died in accidents. It was originally named after Stalin. After his death, his name was removed, from the colonnade, the lobby and even a sculpture.

Now look at the top edges of the wings and the tower: the decorative parapets. Here's the gift. To make the building look Polish, those parapets were modelled on the Renaissance houses of Kraków and Zamość. A Soviet skyscraper wearing a Polish crown.

[beat]

So what do you do with a building like this? Love it or loathe it, Warsaw didn't knock it down. Instead the city filled it: theatres, cinemas, museums, a swimming pool, a congress hall seating three thousand, where the Rolling Stones played in nineteen sixty-seven as the first major Western rock band behind the Iron Curtain. In two thousand, giant clock faces were added to the tower. Over seventy years it has slowly been tamed. If you ask me, it's become Warsaw's most useful argument.

There are more than three thousand rooms inside. Take the lift to the terrace on the thirtieth floor, a hundred and fourteen metres up, and look out over the whole rebuilt city: the river, the old town, the glass towers springing up around this one. From up there, as the joke promised, you can't see the Palace at all.
