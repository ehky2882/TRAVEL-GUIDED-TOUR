## Script

Look up at the word over the door. "Artibus". Latin, "for the arts". This building has had to fight for that word more than once.

You're on Plac Małachowskiego, a small square just off the Royal Route, facing a pale neo-Renaissance building with columns, a grand portal and sculpted figures. This is Zachęta, the National Gallery of Art.

The name means something like "encouragement". It comes from the Society for the Encouragement of Fine Arts, founded in Warsaw in eighteen sixty. The society held a competition for a building, won by the architect Stefan Szyller, and the front opened in December nineteen hundred. The allegorical figures around the portal are by the sculptor Zygmunt Otto.

Now here's the hardest fact about this building. On the sixteenth of December, nineteen twenty-two, the first President of the restored Polish Republic, Gabriel Narutowicz, came to this gallery. He was shot dead here by a painter called Eligiusz Niewiadomski. A painter, at an art gallery, murdering the head of state.

During the occupation the Germans turned the building into a "House of German Culture" for their propaganda. The collection was scattered. Then it was badly damaged in the Uprising.

*[beat]*

And it's still a place where Poland argues with itself. In two thousand, an exhibition here included a sculpture by Maurizio Cattelan of Pope John Paul the Second struck down by a meteorite. A member of parliament came in and attacked it, and the scandal cost the gallery's director, Anda Rottenberg, her job. That's quite a lot of drama for one building. But it tells you that art in Warsaw has never been mere decoration. People feel it matters enough to fight over.

Go in and see whatever's on. Zachęta shows contemporary art, and the shows are often sharp. Then, from the steps, walk a couple of minutes west into the Saxon Garden, the city's oldest public park, for a breather under the trees.
