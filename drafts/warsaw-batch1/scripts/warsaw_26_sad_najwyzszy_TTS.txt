The Supreme Court

Read the columns. On this building, the law is literally written in stone, and you're invited to stand among it.

You're on plats kra sheen skeeh, in front of Poland's Supreme Court. Ahead of you, a long colonnade of tall green pillars, and on top of the pillars, plants growing. Nearby stands the great bronze monument to the Warsaw Uprising.

The building was designed by marek boo jin skee and zbeeg nyeff ba doff skee, the same pair behind the University Library, with a team of twenty one architects. It was built between nineteen ninety six and nineteen ninety nine. There are seventy six pillars in all, and the greenery on top was, the architects said, inspired by the cities of the Incas. Look at the tops of the columns, and you'll see the scales of justice worked into them.

The gift is the writing. Eighty six legal maxims are carved on the pillars, in Latin and in Polish, chosen by a team of scholars of Roman law led by Professor veetold vo wod kyeh vich. Many come from the Roman jurists and the great Roman law books of Justinian. Walk along and read a few. noola poyna seeneh legeh: no punishment without law. yoos est ars bonee et eye kwee: law is the art of the good and the fair.

And here's why this building matters so much. It went up in the nineteen nineties, just after Poland came out of communism. A country that had lived for decades under a system where law bent to politics built its highest court out of the oldest principles of law it could find, and carved them into the street for everyone to read. That is a statement, and it's not a subtle one.

Now look at the pillars nearest the Uprising monument. On those, you'll find the anchor symbol of Fighting Poland, the emblem of the wartime underground. Then go round to the back of the building and find the three figures holding it up: caryatids standing for Faith, Hope and Love.
