For years this little building was so plastered in billboards that most people forgot there was architecture underneath. Then somebody peeled them off.

You should be on mar shaw koff ska, the city's great north, south avenue, at number ninety nine, looking at a low pavilion of glass and metal. It sits beside the taller Hotel metro pol. Look at the ground floor and you'll see turquoise ceramic tiles.

It was finished in nineteen sixty six, designed by zig moont stem peen skee with and zhay mee leff skee, as a shop for tseh pel ya. tseh pel ya was the state network of folk art and craft cooperatives, set up after the war to sell Polish folk art and handicrafts. The pavilion was conceived as one big shop window. Here's the gift: the turquoise tiles are by the artist stah nee swaff koo har skee, and each one carries its own geometric pattern.

The nineteen nineties were cruel to it. The windows vanished under advertising, a nightclub took the basement, and tseh pel ya sold the building. In twenty nineteen a fast food chain wanted to move in. The heritage authorities said no, an appeal said yes, and in the end the chain walked away. The building was protected as a monument, restored by the architect and zhay how jin skee, and reopened in December twenty twenty four. It's now a branch of em pick, the country's big book and media chain.

So, a fair warning: you won't find folk art for sale here any more. What you'll find is a rescued building doing a different job. And I think that's the right lesson. Warsaw spent the nineties and two thousands treating its modernist buildings as embarrassing leftovers. This pavilion is part of a slow change of heart. A city that lost so much of its old fabric is finally learning to value its younger buildings too.
