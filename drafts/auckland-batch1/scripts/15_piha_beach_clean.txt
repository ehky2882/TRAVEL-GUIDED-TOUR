Piha Beach

On a west-coast evening, with the swell rolling in off the Tasman Sea and the black sand steaming slightly after a hot day, Piha feels a very long way from the city it belongs to.

Look along the beach for Lion Rock, which is impossible to miss: the great dark outcrop standing out of the sand halfway along the beach, shaped, if you squint from the right angle, like a lion lying down with its head towards the sea. With the rock in front of you and the surf club somewhere along the beach behind, you're on South Piha, at the heart of the bay.

The rock's older name is Te Piha, which refers to the pattern the waves make as they break against it, and it gave the whole beach its name. It was once Whakaari pā, a fortified settlement of Te Kawerau ā Maki, the iwi whose territory this is, and archaeologists have found terraces and platforms on its slopes. In December 1919, a memorial to local sawmill workers who died in the First World War was unveiled on the rock as well.

Then look down at your feet, because the sand gets its colour from iron, carried and washed up the coast over thousands of years from Mount Taranaki and older volcanoes, which is why it heats up so fiercely in summer.

The surf here is what draws people, but it's also what makes Piha serious, and the beach's surf lifesavers became famous through a long-running television series about rescues on this very stretch of sand. The beach is beautiful and the water is powerful, and those two facts belong together; so if you swim, stay between the red and yellow flags, where the lifeguards can see you.

Walk south along the sand towards the cliffs, and at low tide you can reach The Gap, where waves pour through a cleft in the rocks into a sheltered pool beside Taitomo Island.
