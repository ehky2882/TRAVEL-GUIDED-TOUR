Takutai Square

Takutai means foreshore, and the name is meant literally, because about a hundred and fifty years ago the ground you're standing on was the bed of the harbour.

Stand on the edge of the lawn in the middle of the square, with The Hotel Britomart's brick tower and the old warehouses of Galway Street to the west, and the glassier modern office buildings closing off the eastern side. The square is about seventy metres long and fifty wide, and beneath your feet, out of sight, is Britomart train station.

Think first about what lies underneath, since the original shoreline ran roughly along today's Fort Street, and just to the east stood a headland that Māori called Te Rerenga-ora-iti and Europeans later named Point Britomart, after a navy ship that surveyed the harbour in 1848. In the 1870s and 1880s the headland was quarried away, and its rock and earth went into the reclamations that created this flat land, and eventually room for the railway.

Now find the fountain in the south-eastern corner: Te Rou Kai, a field of sixteen sculpted stones and steel pipi shells made in 2003 by three Māori artists, Chaz Doherty, Renata Blair and Bernard Makoare. Every so often, little jets pop water into the air, imitating the way pipi squirt water as they filter it, a reminder that this foreshore was once a rich source of shellfish for local iwi.

Put those two things together and the square reads differently. It looks like a smart lawn between office blocks, but it's really a piece of made ground laid over an old beach, and the artwork is there so that the beach isn't forgotten.

On a Saturday morning, the square fills with a market, and on any day you can walk a few minutes east towards Britomart Place, near the old line of the headland, where a memorial stone now marks the spot where it once met the sea.
