Cafe Hanoi

In twenty ten, Britomart was still finding its feet as somewhere to eat out, and Cafe Han oy was among the first of the new restaurants to open here, in those days, in the building on the opposite corner.

Twenty seven Galway Street is its home now, so face the heritage building on the pedestrian stretch of Galway Street with tall windows and a glassy conservatory that looks out towards Tah koo tie Square. With the brick tower of The Hotel Britomart close by on one side and the square's lawn within sight, you're in the right spot.

Begin with the move, because it's the story here. In September twenty twenty, to mark its tenth birthday, Cafe Han oy crossed the road into the space that had been eighteen eighty five, a well known nightclub, and its old room went to a new restaurant called Mister Morris. Chesh er Architects designed the new home around the faded elegance of Han oy's Old Quarter: high ceilings, soft light, an open kitchen with counter seats, and much of the building's original fabric left on show rather than covered up.

The cooking is northern Vietnamese, shaped by the French influence that runs through the city's food, which explains why a menu built on fuh, caramelised pork and shaking beef can also carry a charcuterie board of pat ay and duck pro shoo toh. The owners, Tony McGeorge and Krishna Boh tee tsah, later opened a second restaurant, Ghost Street, in the basement underneath.

The interesting part is how unusual that kind of loyalty is. A restaurant that had become part of the furniture could have left the precinct altogether; instead it went across the road and took a former nightclub as its address, and districts like this one are built on exactly that sort of commitment.

Before going in, cross to the edge of Tah koo tie Square and look back at the conservatory; from there you can see how the whole dining room opens towards the square, and it's at its best once the lights come on in the evening.
