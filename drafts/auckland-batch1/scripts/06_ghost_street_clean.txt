Ghost Street

Look for the bamboo curtain, because that's the only real clue that there's a restaurant down here at all.

Tuawhiti Lane is the narrow passage that runs beside The Hotel Britomart, and Ghost Street sits at basement level beneath twenty-seven Galway Street, so follow the lane until you find the stairs leading down and the curtain hanging across the entrance. If you can hear a busy kitchen below you and smell chilli oil, you've found it.

The name comes from Ghost Street in Beijing, a long stretch of restaurants famous for staying open late into the night, and the idea here was a moody, film-set version of old Beijing's hole-in-the-wall eateries. Downstairs that means recycled timber tables, black cement tiles, an open kitchen, and a long shared table lined with stools running between the building's central pillars.

Ordering works differently too, since you tick your dishes on a paper menu, and the sheet is clipped onto wires running overhead, so a dinner here comes with its own small washing line. Jasmine tea arrives as you sit down, and the cooking leans west, towards Chengdu in Sichuan and towards Xi'an, with lamb skewers, scallop wontons in Sichuan oil, and biang biang noodles, whose name is written with one of the most complicated characters in Chinese.

That basement is around a hundred and thirty years old, and before Ghost Street opened in 2021 it was the downstairs bar of the 1885 nightclub. What's worth noticing is how comfortably the old brick takes on each new identity, from Victorian warehouse to late-night club to Beijing alley, which says as much about Britomart's heritage buildings as it does about the restaurant.

On your way out, glance up at the dining room above, which belongs to Cafe Hanoi and its same owners; and if you've eaten well, the lane carries on through to Customs Street, which makes for a pleasant short cut back towards the waterfront.
