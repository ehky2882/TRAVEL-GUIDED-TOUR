Mooseo Hoomex

The art inside this building was gathered on the back of a juice company, and the man who collected it started by hanging it in a gallery at the fruit juice factory, on the industrial edge of the city.

Up on the raised stone plaza, face the pale building that sits above you on its thick columns, with its jagged roofline of sharp peaks like a row of saw teeth, and nearby, the shimmering, twisting form of the Mooseo Soomaya rising beside it.

Start with the stone, which is travertine from Halahpa, in Vairakrooss, a warm, slightly pitted limestone that changes colour through the day as the light moves across it, and the building was designed by the British architect David Chipperfield, opening in November twenty thirteen as his first in Latin America.

Then there's that roof, where the saw teeth aren't decoration at all, because each peak holds a skylight angled to bring soft, even daylight into the top floor galleries, which is exactly the light you want for looking at art and exactly the light that's hardest to manufacture.

The collection belongs to Ehoohaynio Lopess Alonso, heir to the Hoomex juice business, who bought his first Mexican artwork in nineteen ninety four and first showed his collection in two thousand and one at a gallery inside the company's factory in Ekatepek. It now runs to around two thousand eight hundred works, from Warhol and Koons to a whole generation of Mexican contemporary artists.

In effect, a private collection walked out of a factory and onto a public plaza, and the museum works very hard to feel open, with a plinth you can sit on and big timber doors that swing wide to the street.

Back out on the plaza, look across at the Soomaya next door, then decide for yourself which of the two buildings makes the better case for how a museum ought to look.
