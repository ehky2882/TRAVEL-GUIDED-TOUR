Bosque de Chapultepec

Something like twenty million visits a year are paid to this park, which covers roughly twice the area of Central Park in New York.

Beside the boating lake in the First Section, turn until the wooded hill with a palace on its summit is in front of you, rising above the trees to the east, with the water and its hired rowing boats at your back and the path running along the shore on either side.

That hill is the one that gave everything its name, since Chapultepec means grasshopper hill in Nahuatl, the language of the Mexica. It mattered to them for one very practical reason, which is that springs rose here, and in the fifteenth century an aqueduct was built to carry that water across the lake to their island capital, Tenochtitlan.

Look at the trees nearest the shore, the ones with shaggy, dark trunks, because they're ahuehuetes, Montezuma cypresses, and some in this park are thought to be several centuries old, while the most famous of them, known as El Sargento, is said to have been planted around 1460 and stood for over five hundred years.

The palace on the hill is Chapultepec Castle, a viceroy's summer residence that later became a military college and then a home for Mexico's heads of state, and in 1847, during the war with the United States, young cadets died defending it, a loss the country still marks as the Niños Héroes.

The point, then, is that Chapultepec isn't the city's garden so much as its source, because the water that made a capital possible came out of this ground.
