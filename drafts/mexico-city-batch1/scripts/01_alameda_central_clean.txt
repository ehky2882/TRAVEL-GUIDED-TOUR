Alameda Central

In 1592, a Spanish viceroy ordered poplar trees planted on what was then the western edge of the city, and the park that grew up around them has been here ever since, which is why the Alameda is usually described as the oldest public park in the Americas. Even the name comes from álamo, the Spanish word for poplar.

The long white crescent of marble columns in front of you is the Hemiciclo a Juárez. Stand on the park side of Avenida Juárez, facing north into the trees with the monument straight ahead, and off to your right, at the eastern end of the park, the tiled dome of the Palacio de Bellas Artes should be showing above the treetops.

Start with the crescent, twelve Doric columns unveiled in September 1910. At its centre sits Benito Juárez, the Zapotec lawyer turned president, while an allegorical figure of the homeland leans in to crown him with laurel. It was commissioned by Porfirio Díaz for the centenary of independence, and there's a curious twist in that, because Díaz had once taken up arms against Juárez, so the monument doubles as rather graceful political housekeeping.

Around you, the paths are laid out on French lines, with five fountains carrying figures from Greek and Roman myth, although not all of the park's past is so gentle, since the western end was once the Quemadero, where the Inquisition carried out its sentences, and it was only folded into the gardens in 1770.

Put those together and the Alameda starts to look less like a garden than a stage, where each government that held this city set out, in trees and marble, the story it wanted told.

Follow the paths west to the far end of the park, where the Museo Mural Diego Rivera holds his Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in the Alameda, painted in 1947 and crowded with four centuries of the people who strolled here, which is quite something to see once you've walked it yourself.
