Arka Teeaira

The water here is the colour of strong tea, the trees along its banks stand tall and narrow as pencils, and the field ahead of you is, quite literally, an island that somebody built by hand.

Sochimeelko, a short trahinaira ride south of the Kwaymanko landing, on the edge of one of Arka Teeaira's cheenampas: the boat should be tied up behind you, the canal running away to your left and right, and ahead, low raised beds of kale, chard, beetroot and lettuce laid out in neat green rows.

Begin with the ground under your feet, because a cheenampa is made by piling up layers of canal mud and plant matter until a field rises out of the water, a method farmers in this valley were using long before the Spanish arrived, and the canals that surround each bed irrigate it and fertilise it at the same time.

Arka Teeaira was founded in two thousand and nine by Loosio Oosobeeahga, a philosopher by training, and today it works with around thirty five farming families using regenerative methods, which is a fancy way of saying they're trying to put back into the soil what modern farming took out. Visitors come for sunrise breakfasts served from a trahinaira, for brunches of kaysadeeyas and cheelakeeless, and for long dinners cooked by guest chefs, Gabreeayla Kahmara of Kontramar among them.

What makes this matter, if you ask me, is that it isn't a museum of farming. A recent count found roughly two thousand two hundred hectares of cheenampas in Sochimeelko, with fewer than a quarter of them still worked and over a thousand abandoned, and the vegetables grown here go out every week to shops and restaurants across the city.

On the ride back towards Kwaymanko, watch the banks as they slide past, and you'll start to spot the difference between a living cheenampa, trimmed and planted, and an abandoned one gone over to reeds, which is the whole argument of this place written into the landscape.
