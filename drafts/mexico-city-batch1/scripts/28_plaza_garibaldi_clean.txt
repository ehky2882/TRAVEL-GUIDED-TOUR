Plaza Garibaldi

At almost any hour, several bands are playing in this square at once, a few metres apart, each trumpet section doing its best to drown out the next, and somehow it all comes out as one great noise of welcome.

Off the Eje Central, on the northern edge of the old centre, step into the open square and turn slowly, with the mariachis in their silver-studded charro suits gathered in groups around you, bars and dance halls lining the edges, and a row of standing figures of famous singers along one side.

The square got its name in 1921, after Giuseppe Garibaldi, grandson of the Italian hero, who fought in the Mexican Revolution, and the music arrived soon afterwards, when the Salón Tenampa opened around 1923 and brought in a mariachi band from Cocula, in Jalisco, the region where the style was born.

The Tenampa is still here, on the square, with murals inside celebrating the plaza's musical life, and the golden age of Mexican cinema did the rest, with stars like Pedro Infante and José Alfredo Jiménez filmed singing here, which is why their figures now stand among the others in the square.

The system is simple, because the musicians are independent, so you agree a price with a band for a song, or for a stretch of time, and they'll play it right there for you, at your table or in the middle of the plaza.

Garibaldi works, in other words, as a hiring hall and a public jukebox at once, where many of the most skilled musicians in the country wait to be chosen, and where a love song can be bought for the price of a decent lunch.

If you're hungry, the Mercado de San Camilito, just off the square, keeps its food stalls going around the clock with broths and dishes from Jalisco, and there's a museum of tequila and mezcal on the plaza for a little background with your drink.
