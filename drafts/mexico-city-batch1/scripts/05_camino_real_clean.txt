Camino Real Polanco

This is the Camino Real, and from the street it barely seems to want you to know it's here, which is entirely deliberate.

From the pavement on Mariano Escobedo, face the hotel and take in the walls first, tall and almost windowless, washed in Mexican pink and deep ochre, and then, in the forecourt before the entrance, a wide round pool of blue water that never stops churning.

Start with that fountain, which spins in a constant whirlpool rather than spraying upwards, and which has long been credited to the Japanese American sculptor Isamu Noguchi, although that attribution has been questioned, so it's safest to enjoy it as one of the city's great small mysteries.

The hotel opened in 1968, just in time for the Mexico City Olympics, and its architect, Ricardo Legorreta, was only in his thirties when he designed it. He worked with the sculptor Mathias Goeritz, and Luis Barragán is credited with suggesting the place be filled with art, which explains why there's a Rufino Tamayo mural in the lobby and a wall of perforated gold by Goeritz at the top of the main stairs.

There was once a nine-foot wall hanging by the textile artist Anni Albers here as well, made for the hotel and named after it, and it disappeared from view for decades before resurfacing in 2018.

The interesting bit is the blankness of those outer walls. Legorreta built the Camino Real as a kind of walled town, turned inward onto its own courtyards and gardens, so that the city outside drops away the moment you're through the door, and that sense of arrival is really the building's whole design.

If the doormen let you through, head inside to the lobby and climb the broad staircase to find the gold Goeritz wall, then look back down at the hallways, which are around nine feet wide and built, it seems, for making an entrance.
