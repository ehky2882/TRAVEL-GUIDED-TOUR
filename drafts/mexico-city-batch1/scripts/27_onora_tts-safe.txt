Onora

Everything in this shop was made by hand somewhere in Mexico, and yet hardly any of it looks the way you might expect Mexican craft to look, which is exactly what its founders set out to do.

Peer through the front windows at Lopay day Vayga three three zero, in Polanko, and you should see a calm, uncluttered room in muted beiges, blacks and greys, with woven throws and cushions, pottery on low tables and lacquered gourds catching the light.

Onora was started in twenty fourteen by Mareea Eladeea Haggerman, who is Mexican, and Maggie Galton, from New York, and they work with family workshops and communities in more than ten states, including Wahhahka, Cheeahpas, Pwebla and Meechoahkahn.

Look at the colours first, because they're the key to the whole place. Rather than the bright pinks and turquoises of a typical craft market, the founders asked artisans for a pared back palette, so that a Talavaira plate from Pwebla or an embroidered tunic from Wahhahka reads as something you'd put in a minimalist flat rather than a souvenir.

Then the making, since you'll find black pottery, rebosso shawls, wool throws, hammocks and stone molkahaytess for grinding salsa, each piece made using techniques that go back generations in the place it comes from.

Scale is the other thing they've thought hard about, since Onora keeps its orders deliberately small so that the artisans can carry on farming and taking part in the life of their villages, which means the business is shaped around the makers' lives rather than the other way round.

Ask the staff which pieces come from Meechoahkahn, home to some of the most striking pottery in the shop, then head west along the Polanko streets to Alehandro Doomah, where the perfume house Sheenoo bottles Mexico's plants in a very different way.
