Mairkahdo Hamaika

Around five thousand different kinds of flowers and plants pass through this market, arriving from all over Mexico, and it never closes, so you could buy a bunch of roses here at four in the morning if the mood took you.

Head in from Geeyairmo Preeayto, near the Hamaika metro station, and stop just inside the flower halls, where the aisles stretch ahead of you under high concrete roofs, with buckets of roses, lilies and gladioli stacked to shoulder height on either side, and florists working on arrangements taller than they are.

Trading here goes back a very long way, since this area was a marketplace around five hundred years ago, on the edge of the old lake, where goods arrived by canoe along the canals, and the market building you're in now was inaugurated in September nineteen fifty seven as part of a wave of modern public markets built across the city.

The numbers are worth a moment, because there are roughly one thousand one hundred and fifty stalls, and flowers are the heart of it, with something like two in every five selling loose blooms and a quarter more doing arrangements.

Then there's the food, in its own section, and it's here that the waraachay is reputed to have been invented in the nineteen thirties, a long oval of corn dough shaped like the sandal it's named after, piled with beans, salsa and cheese.

The thing about Hamaika, really, is that flowers in Mexico aren't a luxury, they're part of the daily business of life, for birthdays, funerals, saints' days and weddings, and in late October this whole place turns orange with marigolds for the Day of the Dead.

Stop at the food section as you leave and order a waraachay from one of the stalls, then carry your flowers out past the plant sellers, where you'll find everything from tiny cacti to small trees.
