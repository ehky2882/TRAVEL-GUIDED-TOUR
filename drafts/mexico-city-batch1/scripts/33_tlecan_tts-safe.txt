Tlaykahn

Tlaykahn means place of fire in Nahwatl, and inside, the low, reddish light and the haze of smoke, as if the name were being taken quite literally.

Step through the small doorway at Alvaro Obregonn two two eight, in Roma Nortay, and you'll be in a dim room with a long bar, facing, on the back wall, a great carved stone disc that looks as though it has walked straight out of a museum.

That disc is a replica of an Aztec carving of the god of death, and the whole room is designed to echo the galleries of the Mooseo Naseeonahl day Antropolohia, which tells you a good deal about the bar's ambitions.

It's run by Ellie Marteeness Bayo, who spent five years leading the drinks at Poohol, and her business partner Bairnardo Sairna, and she has said she wants Mexicans to feel proud and everyone else to be impressed, which is a fine mission statement for a bar.

The spirits come straight from small producers around the country, and alongside mezkahl you'll find lesser known agave and plant spirits, like bakanora from Sonora, sotol from the north and posh from Cheeahpas, many of them never exported at all.

The drink to order is the Taskalahtay Sour, a brick red cocktail inspired by taskalahtay, an old drink from the south made with toasted maize, cacao and acheeohtay, shaken with mezkahl and egg white and dusted with fermented cacao.

Tlaykahn took twenty third place in the twenty twenty five edition of the World's Fifty Best Bars, and the reason, really, is that it treats the country's drinking traditions with the seriousness usually reserved for its archaeology.

On your way out, stroll a few doors along Alvaro Obregonn to the Karla Fairnandess shop on the corner with Monterray, and you'll see the same idea, old Mexican craft treated as something entirely modern, applied to clothes instead of cocktails.
