Contramar

The most famous seafood restaurant in Mexico City sits more than two thousand metres above sea level, several hours' drive from the nearest beach, and it was dreamed up on holiday.

Through the wide-open front at Durango two hundred, in Roma Norte, you should be looking into a broad dining room with high ceilings, an open kitchen at the back and, at lunchtime, nearly every table taken, with waiters in white weaving between them.

Gabriela Cámara opened Contramar in August 1998, after a beach trip a couple of years earlier left her wanting that same easy seaside lunch back in the capital, and the room was built to feel like a palapa, the thatched beach shelters of the Mexican coast.

The dish everyone orders is the tuna tostada, thin slices of raw tuna on a crisp tortilla with chipotle mayonnaise, avocado and a tangle of fried leek, and it has been copied so widely that you've probably eaten an imitation somewhere without knowing where it came from.

The other signature is the pescado a la talla, a whole fish split open down the middle and grilled, with one half painted in red chilli adobo and the other in green parsley sauce, so it arrives at the table looking rather like a flag, and it's meant to be shared with tortillas and beans.

Contramar is really an argument about lunch, if you think about it. It's built for the long Mexican midday meal that can stretch from two until the evening, and its success helped persuade a whole generation of restaurants that lunch, not dinner, could be the main event.

Before you sit down, look for the Poemario, a blackboard where the restaurant chalks up a poem about the sea by a Mexican writer, then book ahead if you can, because on a Friday afternoon the queue for a table can run right out onto Durango.
