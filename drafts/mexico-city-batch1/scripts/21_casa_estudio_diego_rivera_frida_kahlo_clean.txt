Museo Casa Estudio Diego Rivera y Frida Kahlo

Here are two houses, one large and one small, one red and one blue, joined by a narrow bridge at roof height, and together they make about as clear a picture of a marriage as architecture has ever managed.

At the corner of Altavista and the street named after Diego Rivera, in San Ángel, face the boxy concrete buildings behind their living fence of tall organ cactus, with the bigger red and white house nearer you and the smaller blue one beyond it.

Both were designed by Juan O'Gorman, a young architect who'd fallen for the ideas of Le Corbusier, and they went up between 1929 and 1932, among the first truly functionalist buildings in Latin America, all bare concrete, exposed pipes and practical lines at a time when the rest of the street was colonial stone.

Look at the larger house first, which was Rivera's, and you'll see its sawtooth roof, built to pull in steady north light for his double-height studio, and the spiral staircase wound round the outside of the building like an afterthought.

Then the blue house, which was Frida Kahlo's, much smaller and more compact, with her kitchen, dining room and bedroom downstairs and a studio of her own above.

The couple moved in after returning from the United States in 1934, and while they didn't stay together here for long, Rivera kept working in his studio until his death in 1957, and it became a museum in 1986.

What O'Gorman built, in the end, was a way for two very strong-willed artists to live side by side, each with a door of their own and a bridge they could choose to cross, which says rather more than most biographies do.

Before you go in, look along the cactus fence to the third, smaller house right beside the complex, which O'Gorman built for himself, and then, when you're done, cross the road for a margarita at the San Ángel Inn.
