San Ahnhel Inn

The margarita arrives in a small silver jug sunk almost to its neck in a little bucket of ice, and you pour it yourself, a glass at a time, which turns a drink into a ritual.

Across the street from the red and blue houses of the Reevaira and Kahlo studios, facing the long, pale walls of an old hacienda with its arched gateway and heavy wooden doors, you should see glimpses of a courtyard and gardens through the entrance, shaded by old trees.

The land here was granted in sixteen sixteen, and in the eighteenth century it became the Asee endah day Goykoechaya, a working estate that produced poolkay, the milky fermented drink made from the sap of the agave, and the buildings you're looking at grew out of that farm.

In the early twentieth century it was turned into a hotel and given its slightly unlikely English name, the San Ahnhel Inn, and it's often said that Pancho Veeya and Emeeleeahno Zapahta, the two great generals of the Revolution, met here, a story of the sort that old houses like this one tend to gather.

It was declared a colonial monument in nineteen thirty seven, spent a few years housing a university's architecture school, and finally opened as a restaurant in June nineteen sixty three, and it's been one ever since, with a courtyard fountain, tiled details and gardens that make it a favourite for long family lunches and celebrations.

The pleasure of the place is the layering, because a seventeenth century land grant, a poolkay hacienda, an Edwardian style hotel with an English name and a grand Mexican restaurant all sit inside the same walls, and none of them has entirely gone away.

Walk through to the courtyard before you sit down and find the fountain, and if you're visiting in late summer, ask whether the cheeless en nogahda are on the menu, since this is one of the city's classic places to try them.
