Kurimanzutto

This gallery's very first exhibition lasted a single day and took place on a rented fruit stall in a food market, with the art priced to compete with the produce.

Behind a plain wall at Gobernador Rafael Rebollar ninety-four, in San Miguel Chapultepec, sits a long, low industrial building, and through the wide entrance you should glimpse a courtyard, and a sense of space that's unusual on a residential street like this one.

That market show happened in August 1999, at the Mercado de Medellín, when Mónica Manzutto and José Kuri, with ideas from the artist Gabriel Orozco, launched a gallery that had no gallery. For the next few years they staged projects wherever they could find room, in car parks, shipping containers and restaurants, and they built a reputation without a front door.

The front door finally arrived in 2008, when they moved into this building, a lumber yard dating from the fifties, refurbished by Alberto Kalach, the same architect behind the Biblioteca Vasconcelos, who kept the industrial scale and let the light pour in.

The artists they work with include Orozco himself, Damián Ortega and Abraham Cruzvillegas, part of a generation that helped put contemporary Mexican art on the international map, and since 2018 the gallery has had a space in New York as well.

There's a lovely paradox in all this, because a gallery that made its name by refusing walls ended up with some of the most admired walls in the city, and it still carries that market-stall instinct, the idea that art belongs out in the world rather than behind glass.

Once you've seen the show, walk a few streets over to Cantina El Bosque, which has been pouring drinks in this neighbourhood since 1937, and you'll have covered the old and new San Miguel Chapultepec in a single afternoon.
