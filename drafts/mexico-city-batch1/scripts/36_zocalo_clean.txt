Zócalo

Zócalo means plinth, and the name comes from a monument that was never finished, because in the 1840s a column to independence was planned for the middle of this square, but only its base ever got built.

Near the great flagpole in the centre of the square, turn so that the twin towers of the Metropolitan Cathedral are in front of you, which means the long red facade of the National Palace runs along your right, and ahead and to your right, just past the cathedral's eastern end, lie the excavated ruins of the Templo Mayor.

The square's official name is the Plaza de la Constitución, after the Spanish constitution of 1812, and it measures around two hundred and forty metres on each side, which makes it one of the largest public squares in the world.

Long before any of this, the ground beneath you was the ceremonial heart of Tenochtitlan, the Mexica capital, and the Spanish built their own city directly on top of it, using its stones, which is why the cathedral, the palace and the temple ruins all sit within a few hundred metres of each other.

Then look up at the enormous flag, which is raised each morning and lowered each evening by soldiers, then folded and carried into the National Palace in a ceremony that draws a small crowd.

Over the centuries this square has hosted military parades, concerts, Independence Day celebrations and protests of every kind, and it's still where people come to celebrate, to mourn or to be heard.

What strikes you, standing here, is that the Zócalo's emptiness is the point, a vast stage left open for whoever needs it next, whether that's the state, the crowd or simply a family out for a walk.
