Ticuchi

For more than fifteen years, this dark little building was home to Pujol, the restaurant that made Enrique Olvera's name, and when Pujol moved on, he turned its old dining room into something that only really comes alive after dusk.

Seen from the street, Petrarca two five four is almost blank, apart from a big window onto a working comal, the flat clay or metal griddle of Mexican kitchens, and cooks pressing tortillas behind the glass.

Ticuchi opened in August 2019 as Olvera's mezcal bar, and inside it's deliberately cave-like, with black walls, very low light and a domed ceiling meant to suggest a cenote, one of the sunken limestone pools of the Yucatán, and the name itself is usually linked to the bat, the animal of caves.

The drinks list leans on agave spirits from small producers, and the cocktails are built around them, but the food is the real surprise, because almost everything on the menu is made from vegetables and corn, such as mushroom barbacoa or tacos al pastor with roasted pineapple and not a scrap of pork.

So you're eating vegetable tacos in the room where Mexico's most famous tasting menu was born, which I find rather a lovely twist, and it suggests the building has simply changed its hours rather than its ambitions.

During the day, the same team has also run a hatch onto the street called La Ventana del Ticuchi, serving tamales made from heirloom corn, daily broths and coffee for people to eat standing on the pavement, which is Olvera's cooking at its most casual.

[beat]

To see where the story went next, Pujol's current home is a short walk away on Tennyson, behind its own discreet garden wall, and it's worth strolling past even if you haven't got a booking.
