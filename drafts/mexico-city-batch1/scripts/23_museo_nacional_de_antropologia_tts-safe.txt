It was April nineteen sixty four when this enormous stone figure was hauled into the city on a specially built trailer, and on the day it arrived, a downpour flooded several neighbourhoods, which is quite a welcome for a statue usually identified as Tlahlok, the god of rain.

The carved giant standing on the forecourt in front of you, on the Paseo day la Reforma side of the museum, is the one to find, around seven metres tall and weighing roughly one hundred and sixty five tonnes, with the long, low museum building stretching away behind it.

That monolith was brought here from Kwatleenchan, in the State of Mexico, where it had lain for centuries, and its move was a national event, followed by crowds along the route.

Now head inside, through the entrance hall, to the central courtyard, and you'll see the museum's other great object, which isn't an artefact at all. It's the parahgwass, the umbrella, a vast concrete roof held up by a single column, with a curtain of water pouring from its rim, and the bronze reliefs on that column were made by the Chahvess Morahdo brothers.

The museum was designed by Paydro Rameeress Vaskess with Horhay Kampoosahno and Rafa ell Meehahress, and the whole thing was built in nineteen months, opening in September nineteen sixty four, which in a city of slow building projects is a remarkable pace.

Underneath the engineering, the building tells a national story, laying out Mexico's ancient cultures in halls around that courtyard on the ground floor and the living indigenous peoples of the country upstairs, so that the past and the present are stacked one above the other.

When you're ready, walk straight across the courtyard to the hall at the far end, the Mehsheeka hall, where the great Sun Stone hangs on the wall, and give yourself a good few minutes in front of it.
