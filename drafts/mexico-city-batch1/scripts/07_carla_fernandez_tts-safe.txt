Karla Fairnandess

Most of the clothes in this shop start life as simple squares and rectangles, the same geometry that indigenous garments in Mexico have used for centuries, and that is the quiet secret behind everything on the rails.

Look across Alvaro Obregonn from its tree lined central walkway, near the corner with Monterray, and the shop should be facing you, past the benches and sculptures, with rails of garments in black, white and bold blocks of colour behind the windows.

Look closely at the cut, because Karla Fairnandess spent years studying traditional clothing across Mexico, cataloguing how each piece is folded and joined, and she published what she learned in a book called The Barefoot Designer, so the patterns you see here are research turned into tailoring.

The making comes next, since much of the embroidery and weaving is done in partnership with artisan cooperatives, many of them led by women, through a travelling workshop she set up called Tayair Flora. Recent collections have drawn on makers from Yookatahn and Gwanahwahto, and you'll find small handmade pieces too, like bracelets turned from the wooden whisks used to froth hot chocolate.

She describes much of her clothing as unisex, one size and one age, meaning it's designed to be worn by anyone, at any point in their life, which is a pointed thing to say in an industry that usually sells the opposite.

What's really going on here, then, is preservation disguised as fashion, a way of keeping endangered techniques alive by making sure people keep paying for them.

If you'd like to see the bigger picture, the brand's flagship sits at Marsaya seventy two in Koloneea Hwahress, about a twenty minute walk north, in a concrete building designed by the artist Paydro Rayess, with floors set with mosaics inspired by Aztec iconography and a rooftop given over to weaving workshops.
