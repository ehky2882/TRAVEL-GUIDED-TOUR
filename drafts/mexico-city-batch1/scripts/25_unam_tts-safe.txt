National Autonomous University of Mexico

The ground this campus stands on was poured out by a volcano, Sheetlay, around two thousand years ago, and when the university came to build here, it turned the black lava rock into walls, paths and gardens.

Out on the wide lawns at the heart of Seeoodahd Ooneebairseetahria, find a spot facing the tall, windowless block covered from top to bottom in coloured stone mosaic, with the slab like Rektoreea tower off to one side.

That block is the Central Library, and the mosaic, by Hwan Ogorman, covers nearly four thousand square metres across all four sides, made from more than one hundred and fifty kinds of natural stone gathered from all over Mexico, so the colours you see are the country's own rocks rather than paint.

Each face tells part of the story of Mexican culture, with the ancient world on one side and the colonial era on another, and Ogorman said he wanted the library to look different from every other building on campus, to give it a properly Mexican character.

Now look over to the Rektoreea, where David Alfaro Seekaiross made a mural in what he called sculpture painting, raised forms of concrete covered in mosaic, between nineteen fifty two and nineteen fifty four, and angled so it could be read by people driving past on Aveneeda Eensoorhentess.

The campus was planned by Mario Pahnee and Enreekay del Moral, with more than sixty architects, engineers and artists involved, and it was finished in nineteen fifty four, before becoming a World Heritage Site in two thousand and seven.

Notice, too, that the art isn't tucked away in galleries but put right on the outsides of the buildings, for everyone who passes, which is a rather beautiful idea of what a public university is for.

If you've the energy, cross Eensoorhentess to the Estahdio Oleempeeko Ooneebairseetahrio, where Deeaygo Reevaira's stone relief wraps the front of the stadium, and you'll have seen the work of three of Mexico's great muralists in a single walk.
