Xinú Perfumes

Xinú means nose in Otomí, one of Mexico's indigenous languages, which is a disarmingly direct name for a perfume house.

Black walls and a black staircase rising to an upper floor are what to look for at Alejandro Dumas one six one, in Polanco, an interior that feels almost like a theatre with the lights dimmed.

The house was founded by Verónica Peña, and every fragrance is built from Mexican plants, starting with Aguamadera, which combines agave, cedar, lime and guaiac wood, a smell somewhere between a cantina and a forest.

Then there's Copála, with pink pepper, vanilla from Papantla in Veracruz, mesquite and copal, the resin that's burned as incense in churches and ceremonies across the country, so if you've smelt the Zócalo on a festival day, this one might feel oddly familiar.

The third, Oronardo, brings together marigold, Mexican tuberose and the night-blooming cereus, a cactus flower that opens only after dark.

Look closely at the bottles, too, because they were designed by Esrawe Studio and Cadena y Asociados, in certified sustainable wood and hand-blown Mexican glass, and they're made to be kept and used as a vase or a small sculpture once the perfume has gone.

The quiet idea behind Xinú is that Mexico's botany is as rich a source for perfume as the lavender fields of Provence, and that you can bottle a country's landscape in the same way you might bottle a memory.
