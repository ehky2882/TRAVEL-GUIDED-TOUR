Museo Tamayo Arte Contemporáneo

This museum exists because a Mexican painter decided that his own country deserved to see the art of the rest of the world, and then went out and bought it.

Set back from Paseo de la Reforma at number fifty-one, half hidden among the trees on the park side, is a low, stepped building of grey concrete, and that's what you should be facing, with broad terraces climbing in tiers like a pyramid that someone has pressed flat.

The painter was Rufino Tamayo, born in Oaxaca in 1899, and with his wife Olga he gave the collection and the idea for a public museum of international modern art, which opened here in 1981.

Its architects were Teodoro González de León and Abraham Zabludovsky, and if you go up close, run a hand over the walls, because that concrete was mixed with crushed marble and then chipped back by hand, giving it a rough, stony texture that reads almost like carved rock.

The stepped shape is no accident, either, since the architects took their cue from the platforms and terraces of pre-Hispanic sites, so a building designed for the most up-to-date art in the world is also quietly nodding to the oldest architecture in the country.

Inside, the Tamayos' collection runs to more than three hundred works by around one hundred and seventy artists, with names like Picasso, Rothko and Henry Moore, alongside the contemporary shows that now fill most of the galleries.

Tamayo's great gesture, I always think, was the reversal, because instead of a Mexican artist sending his work out to the great museums abroad, he brought the world's art home and put it in a park where anyone could walk in.
