Lahgo

Look at the roof first, because it's the whole reason this building is famous, a great curved sheet of concrete that dips in the middle and sweeps up at the edges towards the lake.

On the shore of the Lahgo Maiyor, in the Second Section of Chapooltepek, with the water on one side of you and the low glass building under its saddle shaped roof on the other, you should be able to see diners on the terrace and the lake stretching out behind them.

That roof is what engineers call a hyperbolic paraboloid, and it was designed in nineteen sixty four by Alfonso Rameeress Ponsay, who was just twenty four at the time, working with Layoneedess Gwadarrahma, and he had studied under Faylix Kandayla, the master of thin concrete shells.

For decades this was the Restowrantay del Lahgo, one of the city's grand places for a special occasion, but a renovation in the late nineties carved up the open interior, and after the pandemic it closed for good.

In twenty twenty two it reopened as Lahgo Ahlgo, after the architects of the studio Nahso stripped back the later additions, and it now has two halves, Lahgo, a restaurant and caffay cooking with local and sustainable ingredients, and Ahlgo, a free contemporary art gallery programmed by Galaireea oh em ar.

What's changed, and it matters, is who the building is for. It used to be a place for a smart dinner with a view, and now you can wander in off a running path, look at new art for nothing and sit by the lake with a coffee, which makes it feel like it finally belongs to the park.

Before you go inside, walk a little way along the shore so you can see the roof side on, where the curve is clearest, and if you've got your trainers on, the Peesta el Sopay running track loops through the trees nearby.
