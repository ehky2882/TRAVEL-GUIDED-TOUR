One of the great photographers of the twentieth century, Manwell Alvaress Brahvo, once lived in this building, back when it was a crowded tenement rather than a hotel.

Repooblika day Gwatemahla runs right behind the Metropolitan Cathedral, and you want the narrow pavement outside number twenty, facing a nineteenth century facade of stone and tall shuttered windows, with the cathedral's bell towers rising over the rooftops to one side and the Templo Maiyor a short walk ahead.

The building's past comes first, since it was built as a grand family house in the late eighteen hundreds, and when wealthy residents moved out of the centre, it was carved up into a vaysseendahd, a block of small rented rooms around shared courtyards, which is how countless families in the Sentro lived for generations.

Next, the conversion, carried out for Groopo Abeeta by the architects Horhay Ambrosee and Gabreeayla Etchegarai and opened in twenty twenty. They kept the old bones and added steel, concrete, timber and basalt, and there are now twenty five rooms, most of them looking onto small skylit patios, with Alvaress Brahvo's photographs hung on the walls.

Then head up to the rooftop, which has a bar and a plunge pool, and from there you can see the cathedral, the National Palace, the Templo Maiyor and the Torray Lateeno Amaireekahna in one sweep.

There's a debate in the Sentro about conversions like this one, with some welcoming the restoration of neglected buildings and others worrying about who gets to live here afterwards, and both views come from people who care about the same streets.

If you've time, go up to the roof for a drink before sunset, and look down at the excavated walls of the old temple precinct just to the north east, the heart of the Mehsheeka city lying open almost beneath your feet.
