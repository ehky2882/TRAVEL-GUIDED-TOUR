Tlecan

Tlecan means place of fire in Nahuatl, and inside, the low, reddish light and the haze of smoke, as if the name were being taken quite literally.

Step through the small doorway at Álvaro Obregón two two eight, in Roma Norte, and you'll be in a dim room with a long bar, facing, on the back wall, a great carved stone disc that looks as though it has walked straight out of a museum.

That disc is a replica of an Aztec carving of the god of death, and the whole room is designed to echo the galleries of the Museo Nacional de Antropología, which tells you a good deal about the bar's ambitions.

It's run by Eli Martínez Bello, who spent five years leading the drinks at Pujol, and her business partner Bernardo Serna, and she has said she wants Mexicans to feel proud and everyone else to be impressed, which is a fine mission statement for a bar.

The spirits come straight from small producers around the country, and alongside mezcal you'll find lesser-known agave and plant spirits, like bacanora from Sonora, sotol from the north and pox from Chiapas, many of them never exported at all.

The drink to order is the Tascalate Sour, a brick-red cocktail inspired by tascalate, an old drink from the south made with toasted maize, cacao and achiote, shaken with mezcal and egg white and dusted with fermented cacao.

Tlecan took twenty-third place in the 2025 edition of the World's Fifty Best Bars, and the reason, really, is that it treats the country's drinking traditions with the seriousness usually reserved for its archaeology.

On your way out, stroll a few doors along Álvaro Obregón to the Carla Fernández shop on the corner with Monterrey, and you'll see the same idea, old Mexican craft treated as something entirely modern, applied to clothes instead of cocktails.
