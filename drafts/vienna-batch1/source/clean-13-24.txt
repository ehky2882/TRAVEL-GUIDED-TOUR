=== 13 cafe-museum
Café Museum

In April eighteen ninety-nine, a young critic who hated ornament got his first big job. This is it.

You're at the corner of Operngasse and Friedrichstraße, a few minutes from the State Opera. Look across Friedrichstraße and you'll see the white cube and golden dome of the Secession. Behind you, on the corner, is a large café with big windows on two sides. That's Café Museum.

The building itself dates from eighteen seventy-two. The interior was given to Adolf Loos, who was then better known for his writing than his buildings. It was his first major commission, and he treated it as a manifesto. Smooth walls, brass strips, marble tables, bentwood chairs, mirrors, and bare light bulbs. No garlands, no gilding. A plain room for people who had better things to look at.

And here's the joke. Loos despised the Secession, the gold-domed temple of ornament across the road. Yet the Secession artists adopted his café as their local. Klimt, Schiele, Kokoschka, Otto Wagner, Karl Kraus and Robert Musil all drank coffee here.

Loos's room didn't last. In nineteen thirty and thirty-one, Josef Zotti, a pupil of Josef Hoffmann, remodelled it with cosy red booths. In two thousand and three it was rebuilt to look like Loos's original, a reconstruction that critics found neither authentic nor comfortable. Since it reopened in twenty ten, the look has gone back towards Zotti: red velvet benches and round globe lamps.

What's worth knowing is that this is a room the city has argued over for a hundred and twenty-five years. Few cafés can say that.

[beat]

When you leave, cross Friedrichstraße to the Secession and read the gold lettering over its door. Then look back at the café's plain corner, and you'll be standing exactly between the two sides of the argument.

=== 14 strandbar-herrmann
Strandbar Herrmann

Twenty years ago this was a patch of builders' wasteland. Then somebody tipped a load of sand on it.

You're on the Danube Canal, on the Landstraße bank, at the point where the little Wien river flows into it. Just upstream, across the mouth of the Wien river, you'll see the Urania, the domed observatory and adult education centre. On your side, along the embankment, are deckchairs, sand, and a long wooden bar. That's Strandbar Herrmann.

It opened in the summer of two thousand and five, the first of the canal's sand bars. The founder, Alexander Kaiser, had seen beach bars in Berlin and thought the idea would work here, on a neglected stretch at the mouth of the Wien river. Within days of opening, people were queuing for deckchairs.

The name has nothing to do with the owner. The little park it sits in, the Herrmannpark, is named after Emanuel Herrmann, a Viennese economist who is generally credited with inventing the postcard. I find that rather fitting for a place most visitors leave having taken a photograph.

Here's the bigger point, and it's an urban-planning one. For decades, the Danube Canal was the city's back yard: fast traffic on top, concrete walls below, nothing to stop for. The beach bars proved that people would come down to the water if you gave them a reason. The city followed, and today the canal banks are one of the liveliest public spaces in Vienna.

It's seasonal, roughly spring to early autumn, so check before you make a special trip.

[beat]

Before you leave, walk upstream along the canal path towards Schwedenplatz. The walls are covered with graffiti, much of it legal and repainted every few weeks, and on a summer evening the whole bank turns into a promenade.

=== 15 lobmeyr
J. & L. Lobmeyr

If you've looked up at the chandeliers in the Vienna State Opera, or at the Met in New York, you've already met this shop.

You're on Kärntner Straße, number twenty-six, the busy pedestrian street running from the Opera to St Stephen's. In front of you is a dignified old shopfront with the name Lobmeyr above it and glass glinting in the windows.

Josef Lobmeyr founded the business in eighteen twenty-three, selling Biedermeier glass round the corner on Weihburggasse. His son Ludwig turned it into one of the great names in European glass, worked with the builders of the Ringstraße, and in eighteen sixty-four helped found what's now the Museum of Applied Arts. In the eighteen eighties Lobmeyr made some of the first electric chandeliers in the world, in collaboration with Thomas Edison.

Then came the moderns. In the first decades of the twentieth century the firm worked with Josef Hoffmann, Adolf Loos and the artists of the Wiener Werkstätte, and several of those designs are still made today. The most famous is the drinking set designed by Loos, plain, heavy-bottomed tumblers with a faint cut pattern in the base.

My favourite detail is a goblet. In nineteen ten, when the second mountain spring water line reached Vienna, Emperor Franz Joseph switched it on in the City Hall and took the first sip from a Lobmeyr crystal goblet.

The family still runs the company, six generations on. And on the second floor of this building, up the stairs, is a small glass museum.

[beat]

Go in and climb to the museum. Then, back outside, walk a couple of minutes towards St Stephen's and turn right into Weihburggasse. That's the street where Josef Lobmeyr opened his first shop in eighteen twenty-three, and the firm has never moved more than a few hundred metres from it.

=== 16 knize
Knize

On the Graben, Vienna's grandest shopping street, there's a shopfront by Adolf Loos that has barely changed in more than a century.

You're on the Graben, at number thirteen. Find the shopfront with the name Knize spelled out above it, in a band of polished dark stone around the door and windows. Turn round and the Plague Column, with its swirling cloud of saints, is behind you in the middle of the street.

The firm was founded in eighteen fifty-eight by a Czech tailor, Josef Kniže, who made riding clothes, sporting wear and liveries for Vienna's grand households. Between nineteen ten and nineteen thirteen, Adolf Loos designed this shop. It has survived almost unaltered, which is rare for any Loos interior, let alone one still doing the job it was built for.

Loos thought ornament belonged to the past, but he never thought luxury did. Look at the way the front is made: plain, dark, beautifully proportioned, the lettering doing almost all of the decoration. Inside, the rooms are lined in wood and feel more like a gentleman's club than a shop.

The client list is a little startling. Marlene Dietrich had her stage tailcoats made here. Oskar Kokoschka, Billy Wilder, Josephine Baker and Georgia O'Keeffe are all on it. Loos even designed a second Knize shop on the Champs-Élysées in Paris in the late nineteen twenties.

And the craft continues. The art of making tailcoats to measure is on Austria's list of intangible cultural heritage, and Knize is one of the last workshops still doing it by hand, in the master workshop here on the Graben.

[beat]

Before you go, walk a few doors along to the Graben's crossing with Kohlmarkt, then look down Kohlmarkt to the Michaelerplatz. The plain building facing the palace gate at the end is Loos's Looshaus, the one that so offended the Emperor.

=== 17 muehlbauer
Mühlbauer

In nineteen hundred and three, Julianna Mühlbauer opened a small hat workshop in Floridsdorf, on the far side of the Danube. Her great-grandson runs it now, and the hats are still made by hand, here in the middle of Vienna.

You're on Seilergasse, number ten, a narrow street running between Stephansplatz and the Neuer Markt. In front of you is a clean, spare shopfront with the name Mühlbauer above it. That's the flagship, opened in two thousand and five.

It was designed by the Berlin architects Kühn Malvezzi, who are better known for galleries and museums. You can see why they got the job. A good hat shop has the same problem as a good gallery: a small number of precious objects that need to be seen properly, with nothing competing with them.

The hats themselves are the point. Klaus Mühlbauer, the fourth generation, designs the collections with two in-house designers. The workshop is near Schwedenplatz, and most of what's sold here is made in and around Vienna. Felt, straw, fabric and fur are shaped over wooden moulds, the way hatters have always done it. And most hats ordered are made to fit the person who ordered them.

What I find quietly impressive is the combination. A family business more than a hundred and twenty years old that has become one of the most respected names in headwear, without either fossilising or forgetting what it's for.

It's worth knowing they have a second shop in Neubaugasse, in the seventh district, if you're over that way.

[beat]

From here, walk to the end of Seilergasse and onto the Neuer Markt. The fountain in the middle, by Georg Raphael Donner, has four figures representing Austrian rivers, and the Capuchin church on the corner holds the imperial crypt, where the Habsburgs are buried.

=== 18 augarten-wien
Augarten Wien

Since seventeen eighteen, Vienna has been making its own porcelain. Only Meissen, in Saxony, is older in Europe.

You're on Spiegelgasse, number three, a narrow lane between the Graben and the Albertina. In front of you is the Augarten flagship shop, with white porcelain in the windows, much of it marked underneath with a small blue shield.

The story begins in May seventeen eighteen, when Emperor Charles the Sixth gave a man called Claudius Innocentius du Paquier the exclusive right to make porcelain in Austria. In seventeen forty-four the manufactory passed to Maria Theresa, and from then on every piece carried that blue shield, the old Babenberg arms, under the glaze, as a mark nobody could forge. The empress loved figurines and flowers, and you'll still see both in the windows.

The old imperial manufactory closed in eighteen sixty-four, beaten by industrial competition. In nineteen twenty-three it was revived in the baroque palace in the Augarten park, where it still is today. The new firm brought in the modern Viennese designers: Josef Hoffmann, Michael Powolny and others.

Look for one design in particular. In nineteen twenty-nine Hoffmann designed a mocha service shaped like a ribbed melon. It is still made, and the curves are notoriously difficult to paint, so if you see it decorated in fine stripes, someone with a very steady hand has spent a long time on it.

Here's the thing about Augarten. It isn't really a shop selling antiques. It's a workshop that still makes three hundred years of designs, alongside new ones.

[beat]

If you'd like to see it being made, the manufactory and its museum are in the Augarten park itself, in the second district, with guided tours of the workshop. And while you're in the park, look for the two concrete flak towers rising out of the trees. They make a very strange neighbour for a porcelain palace.

=== 19 postsparkasse
Austrian Postal Savings Bank

Look closely at the walls. They're covered in metal bolt heads, thousands of them, and for most of the last century people thought they were decoration.

You're on Georg-Coch-Platz, a small square just off the Ringstraße. In front of you is a vast building filling a whole city block, its walls faced in square slabs of pale marble and studded in a regular grid with small metal caps. On top of the façade stand two winged figures in shining metal. That's the Postal Savings Bank, by Otto Wagner.

It was built between nineteen hundred and four and nineteen hundred and six, with an extension in nineteen ten to nineteen twelve, in reinforced concrete, a material that was still very new. The façade is covered in thin marble slabs because marble is easy to clean. And the bolts? A study of the materials in twenty twenty-four found they really were how the thin slabs were fixed over the mortar. Form and function, almost literally nailed together.

Now look up at those figures. They're by Othmar Schimkowitz, over four metres tall, and they're among the first sculptures ever cast in aluminium. Aluminium turns up all over this building, in the entrance columns and the heating fans inside, because in nineteen hundred and six it was the modern metal.

Inside, up a flight of stairs, is the banking hall: a great room under a glass roof, with a floor of glass blocks that lets daylight through to the post rooms below.

The bank has moved out. The building now belongs to universities and research institutions, including the University of Applied Arts, and the banking hall has been opened to the public again.

[beat]

Go in and see the hall if it's open; check the times before you come. And on your way in, look at the slim columns holding up the entrance canopy. They're aluminium too, and if you ask me they're the most modern thing on the whole square.

=== 20 looshaus
Looshaus

The Emperor was said to dislike this building so much that he kept his curtains closed.

You're on Michaelerplatz. Behind you is the great curved gateway into the Hofburg palace, with its domed entrance and heroic statues. In front of you, on the corner, is a building in two halves: the lower floors faced in green-veined marble with a row of tall columns, and above that, several storeys of perfectly plain white plaster with simple square windows. That's the Looshaus, by Adolf Loos.

It was commissioned in nineteen hundred and nine by the tailors Goldman and Salatsch, after a competition that hadn't produced anything they liked. Loos hadn't even entered. When the scaffolding came down in nineteen ten, Vienna was outraged. Opposite the imperial palace, a building with no decoration at all. A critic called it inappropriate nakedness. The city stopped the works.

Loos defended himself in public lectures, and in the end a compromise was reached: he added window boxes, so the bare front would at least have flowers. You'll often see them there today. The building was finished in nineteen twelve.

Now look at the lower part again. The marble is cipollino, from the Greek island of Euboea, and it's anything but plain. Its veins are as rich as any carving. The columns echo the portal of St Michael's Church, right next door. Loos wasn't against beauty. He was against putting on a costume. Fine materials for the shop, honest plaster for the flats above.

And here's the irony. The building that scandalised Vienna is now one of the city's most admired. A bank has owned it since nineteen eighty-seven and restored it carefully.

[beat]

Before you move on, walk to the middle of the square and look down into the excavation of Roman and medieval walls. Then look up at the Looshaus and the palace together, the two halves of an argument that Vienna never quite finished.

=== 21 secession
Secession

Above the door, in gold letters, it says: to every age its art, to every art its freedom. This building was the manifesto.

You're on Friedrichstraße, a few steps from the Naschmarkt. In front of you is a squat, white, almost temple-like building with very few windows, and on top a great openwork dome of gilded metal laurel leaves. The Viennese have a nickname for it, the golden cabbage. That's the Secession.

It was built in eighteen ninety-seven and eighteen ninety-eight by Joseph Maria Olbrich, then barely thirty, for a group of young artists led by Gustav Klimt who had walked out of the official art establishment. They wanted a building for exhibitions, and they wanted it to look like nothing Vienna had seen.

Now look at the details. Below that gold motto are three faces of gorgons, standing for painting, sculpture and architecture. Along the walls are owls, attributed to Koloman Moser. And the dome is a laurel crown, the symbol of victory, lifted up and made into architecture.

Then go inside, down to the basement, and you'll find Klimt's Beethoven Frieze. He painted it in nineteen hundred and two for an exhibition celebrating Beethoven, and its theme is the Ninth Symphony: humanity's search for happiness, ending in an embrace. It was meant to be temporary. It survived, and it's now back where it began.

And here's the thing. The Secession is still run by artists, as it has been since eighteen ninety-seven. It isn't a museum to a movement. It's the movement, still going.

You'll also find the building on the back of the Austrian fifty-cent coin, if you have one handy.

[beat]

When you come out, walk round to the side facing the Naschmarkt and look for the bronze of Mark Antony in his chariot, pulled by lions. Then cross over to the market, and look at the Otto Wagner houses on the far side of it, numbers thirty-eight and forty Linke Wienzeile.

=== 22 otto-wagner-pavilion-karlsplatz
Otto Wagner Pavilion Karlsplatz

This is a railway station that the city nearly threw away.

You're on Karlsplatz, in the open space between the Ring and the great baroque dome of the Karlskirche. In front of you are two small, matching pavilions facing each other, built of thin white marble panels held in a frame of green-painted iron, with gilded sunflowers spreading across the edges of their roofs. Pick one and walk up to it.

They were built in eighteen ninety-eight and ninety-nine, as entrances to the new Stadtbahn, Vienna's city railway. Otto Wagner designed the whole network, with help from his assistant Joseph Maria Olbrich, who went on to design the Secession. Most of Wagner's stations are plastered; these two are different, a steel framework hung with marble slabs.

Now look at the colours. The ironwork is painted a particular apple green, the signal colour of the whole Stadtbahn, and you'll see it on Wagner's bridges and railings all over the city. The gold sunflowers are an Art Nouveau signature, and they're what makes these two stand out from their plainer sisters.

In the nineteen seventies, when the line was rebuilt as the underground, the pavilions were scheduled for demolition. There was a public outcry. They were taken apart piece by piece, restored, and put back up in nineteen seventy-seven, about a metre and a half higher than before, to sit above the new tunnels.

One is now a café. The other belongs to the Wien Museum and has a small permanent exhibition about Otto Wagner.

[beat]

From here, walk across to the Karlskirche and look at its two giant columns, carved with spirals like Trajan's in Rome. Then turn round and look back at Wagner's little pavilions. Nearly two centuries apart, and both trying to make Vienna feel like the centre of the world.

=== 23 kirche-am-steinhof
Kirche am Steinhof

This church was designed for patients of a psychiatric hospital, and almost every detail in it was thought through for their sake.

You're on the hill of the Baumgartner Höhe, at the top of the grounds of the old Steinhof hospital, in the west of the city. In front of you is a white church faced in marble slabs, with a gilded dome and two slender towers, each topped by a seated saint. That's the Kirche am Steinhof, Otto Wagner's church of St Leopold, completed in nineteen hundred and seven.

The hospital around you was built as a whole town for the mentally ill, laid out down the slope below, and the church was its crown. The figures on the towers, by Richard Luksch, are Saint Leopold and Saint Severin, two saints with deep roots in Austria. The angels over the entrance are by Othmar Schimkowitz, who also made the aluminium figures on Wagner's Post Office Savings Bank.

Inside, the windows and the mosaics are by Koloman Moser, and they're glorious. But the cleverest things are practical. The floor slopes down about twenty-six centimetres from the entrance to the altar, so that everyone can see. The corners are rounded, so there's nowhere for dirt to gather. Instead of holy water standing in basins, the holy water runs, for hygiene. And there were separate entrances for men and women.

What's worth knowing is that Wagner saw no contradiction in any of this. A modern church could be beautiful and sensible at once. That's the whole of his philosophy in one building.

Opening hours are limited, so check before you make the climb.

[beat]

When you've seen it, turn round and look back down the slope over the hospital pavilions. On a clear day you can see right across Vienna, and it's worth a long, slow look.

=== 24 haus-wittgenstein
Haus Wittgenstein

One of the twentieth century's great philosophers spent two years designing a house for his sister. He once had a ceiling raised by three centimetres because the proportions weren't quite right.

You're on Kundmanngasse, in the third district, at number nineteen. Behind a garden wall you'll see a stark, white, blocky house of several stacked volumes, with tall plain windows and no decoration of any kind. That's Haus Wittgenstein.

It was commissioned in nineteen twenty-five by Margaret Stonborough-Wittgenstein, a wealthy patron of the arts and the subject of a famous portrait by Klimt. She hired the architect Paul Engelmann, a pupil of Adolf Loos, and then invited her brother Ludwig, the philosopher, to help. Ludwig became so involved that Engelmann later considered him the real author.

Ludwig worried at every detail. He designed the windows, the doors, the radiators and the door handles himself. Each window had a heavy metal screen, about a hundred and fifty kilos, that he arranged to move on a pulley. And that ceiling: raised thirty millimetres, because it was wrong. The house was finished in December nineteen twenty-eight, in time for the family to spend Christmas there.

Even he had doubts. Ludwig said the house had good manners but no primordial life or health.

It nearly didn't survive. In nineteen sixty-eight it was sold to a developer to be pulled down. A campaign saved it, it became a national monument in nineteen seventy-one, and it's housed the cultural department of the Bulgarian Embassy ever since. Access to the inside varies, so check ahead.

[beat]

Stand back from the wall for a moment and look at the proportions of the windows against the white walls. Then walk ten minutes or so to the Rochusmarkt, a cheerful local market square, and remind yourself what life looks like when nobody is measuring it in millimetres.
