=== 25 wotrubakirche
Wotrubakirche

From the bottom of the hill it looks like a pile of giant concrete blocks that someone forgot to tidy away. It's a church.

You're on the Georgenberg, in Mauer, on the south-western edge of Vienna, where the city turns into woods and meadows. In front of you, at the top of the slope, is a jumble of rough grey concrete blocks of every size, stacked at angles with gaps of glass between them. That's the Church of the Most Holy Trinity, which everyone calls the Wotrubakirche.

It was designed by the sculptor Fritz Wotruba, with the architect Fritz Gerhard Mayr turning his model into buildable plans. Construction ran from August nineteen seventy-four to October nineteen seventy-six. Wotruba died in nineteen seventy-five and never saw it finished.

Here are the numbers, because they're part of the experience. There are a hundred and fifty-two blocks, all of them bare concrete. The biggest weighs about a hundred and forty-one tonnes. The tallest rises just over thirteen metres.

Now walk round it slowly. No two sides look the same, and the gaps between the blocks are filled with plain glass. That's the trick of the place. From outside it seems closed and heavy, almost hostile. Inside, light falls in through those gaps in long, overlapping shafts, and the whole thing feels unexpectedly gentle.

And here's the thing. Wotruba wasn't trying to make a building look like a sculpture. He made a sculpture you could hold a service in. It's a small, local parish church, still in use, and this year it celebrates fifty years of services.

[beat]

If the door is open, step inside and stand still for a minute while your eyes adjust. Then come out and take the path into the meadows on the Georgenberg. The view back to the church, with the Vienna Woods behind it, is the one to take home.

=== 26 karl-marx-hof
Karl-Marx-Hof

This single building is about a kilometre long. It was built to house five thousand people, and to make a point.

You're on Heiligenstädter Straße, in the nineteenth district, at the square called Zwölfter-Februar-Platz, a short walk from Heiligenstadt station. In front of you is a vast wall of housing in red and ochre, broken by enormous arches, with flagpole towers rising above the central block. That's the Karl-Marx-Hof.

It was designed by Karl Ehn, a pupil of Otto Wagner, and built between nineteen twenty-seven and nineteen thirty for the city's Social Democratic government, the period known as Red Vienna. It holds one thousand three hundred and eighty-two flats, each between thirty and sixty square metres. Modest homes, but with running water, light and air, which most working families in Vienna had never had.

Now here's the number that matters most. Only about eighteen and a half per cent of the site is built on. Everything else is gardens, courtyards and playgrounds. And the estate came with laundries, baths, kindergartens, a library and doctors' surgeries. It's not just housing. It's a piece of a city.

The architecture was political, too, and people read it that way. In February nineteen thirty-four, during Austria's brief civil war, workers barricaded themselves inside, and the army shelled the building until they surrendered. The damage was repaired in the nineteen fifties.

Today the Karl-Marx-Hof is still council housing, still lived in, and still owned by the city. In one of the old communal laundries, there's a small museum about Red Vienna called the Waschsalon. It's open on limited days, so check before you go.

[beat]

Walk through the great central arches into the courtyard and stand among the trees for a moment. Then look back at the façade from inside. From the street it looks like a fortress; from in here, it looks like a garden with a very long house around it.

=== 27 werkbundsiedlung
Werkbundsiedlung

In the summer of nineteen thirty-two, Vienna opened seventy brand new houses to the public as an exhibition. People still live in them.

You're in Hietzing, in the thirteenth district, at the foot of the hill called the Roter Berg. Around you, along gently curving lanes, is a loose village of small white and pastel houses, most of them plain flat-roofed boxes with big windows facing their gardens. That's the Werkbundsiedlung, the Vienna Werkbund Estate.

The idea was to show the modern way of living, and to show it could be affordable. The overall plan was drawn by the architect Josef Frank. He brought in thirty-two architects, plus Margarete Schütte-Lihotzky, the designer of the famous Frankfurt kitchen. Many were Viennese, some came from abroad, and the list reads like a roll call of modernism: Adolf Loos, Josef Hoffmann, Gerrit Rietveld from the Netherlands, Richard Neutra from Los Angeles, Hugo Häring from Berlin, André Lurçat from Paris.

Now look at how Frank arranged them. He deliberately avoided uniformity. The houses sit strictly parallel to the lanes, which gives the place order, but they vary in size and type, so it feels more like a small settlement than a showroom. At the western tip, a taller house by Oswald Haerdtl stands guard like a watchtower.

The houses were designed for people of modest means, and that's still the spirit. The city owns forty-eight of the seventy, and they're let as council homes. The estate has been protected since nineteen seventy-eight, and in twenty twenty it received the European Heritage Label.

Please do remember that these are people's homes. Look from the lanes, and leave the gardens alone.

[beat]

If you'd like more than the outsides, the estate has its own guided tours and an audio guide app. Otherwise, wander until you find the Loos houses and the Rietveld houses, then compare them. Two of the century's great architects, a few metres apart, solving the same small problem in completely different ways.

=== 28 library-and-learning-center
Library & Learning Center

A building that leans out over its own front door at a startling angle, and somehow doesn't fall over. That's Zaha Hadid.

You're on the campus of the WU, Vienna's University of Economics and Business, on the edge of the Prater park. In front of you is the campus's central building: a huge, sculpted block with black and white bands wrapped around it and a great slanting front that leans forward over the plaza. That's the Library and Learning Center.

It was designed by Zaha Hadid Architects and opened in October twenty thirteen, the centrepiece of a new campus of seven buildings for around twenty-four thousand students. Hadid took the most ordinary part of a university, the library, and gave it the most dramatic form on the site.

Now look at the front. The façade leans outward at around thirty-five degrees, and the upper part, where the main library sits, cantilevers over the square, giving the readers inside long views across the campus and the park. The surface is made of panels of fibre-reinforced concrete, cut with narrow, canyon-like slots of glass.

If you can, go in. The interior is a tall, canyon-like atrium, with ramps and walkways crossing through it, so you can see the whole life of the building at once: people climbing, reading, meeting.

And here's the thing worth knowing, standing underneath. Not long after it opened, a couple of the concrete cladding panels came loose and fell. Nobody was hurt, the panels were replaced, and the building carried on. It's a useful reminder that even the most futuristic architecture is still made of heavy things held up by fixings.

[beat]

Before you go, walk a full circle round the campus. Each of the other buildings is by a different practice from around the world, and they make a deliberately mismatched set. Then step out into the Prater's long chestnut avenue, the Hauptallee, a few minutes away.

=== 29 prunksaal
State Hall of the Austrian National Library

The first time you walk into this room, the usual reaction is to stop dead and look up. Allow yourself that.

You're on Josefsplatz, a quiet square inside the Hofburg, the old imperial palace. In front of you is the long baroque front of the former court library, with the bronze equestrian statue of Emperor Joseph the Second in the middle of the square. The entrance to the State Hall is from the square itself.

The hall was built between seventeen twenty-three and seventeen twenty-six for Emperor Charles the Sixth. It was designed by Johann Bernhard Fischer von Erlach, the great architect of Viennese baroque, and when he died, his son, Joseph Emanuel, finished it.

Inside, the numbers do a lot of the work. It's almost eighty metres long and twenty metres high, lined with about two hundred thousand old books, with galleries running round above them. The court painter Daniel Gran covered the ceiling and the great central dome with frescoes, completed in seventeen thirty. In the dome the emperor is shown being raised to glory, surrounded by allegories of everything a ruler hoped to be known for.

Under the dome stands a marble statue of Charles the Sixth himself, dressed as Hercules, patron of the Muses. And around the oval centre of the hall is the library of Prince Eugene of Savoy, the military commander and collector, about fifteen thousand volumes in their original bindings. Near them are four great Venetian globes, each more than a metre across.

What's worth knowing is that this was never just a library. It was an argument about power, made with books. Knowledge, collected, ordered and housed by an emperor.

[beat]

When you come out, look across Josefsplatz to the Palais Pallavicini and the Palais Pálffy opposite. Then walk round to the Michaelerplatz, where the Looshaus stands facing the palace, and you'll go from the height of the baroque to the birth of the modern in about ninety seconds.

=== 30 hofmobiliendepot
Hofmobiliendepot

This is where the Habsburgs kept their furniture when they weren't using it. And they owned an extraordinary amount of furniture.

You're on Andreasgasse, in the seventh district, just off Mariahilfer Straße, Vienna's long shopping street. In front of you is a large, plain, rather functional building. That's the former court furniture depot, now the Furniture Museum Vienna, the Möbel Museum Wien.

In seventeen forty-seven Maria Theresa appointed the first Inspector of Court Chattels. His job was to keep track of the imperial furnishings, repair them, and move them. The court didn't furnish each palace permanently; when it travelled to its summer residences or hunting lodges, the furniture travelled with it. Look out for the travelling thrones.

That's the charm of the place. It isn't laid out as a palace, it's laid out as a store. Chandeliers, mirrors, clocks, even spittoons. The cradle of Crown Prince Rudolf. The furniture used for the signing of the Austrian State Treaty in nineteen fifty-five, the moment Austria regained its independence.

The museum is especially strong on Biedermeier, the quiet, comfortable, beautifully made furniture of the early nineteenth century. Vienna's cabinetmakers were at their peak then, and the chaises longues and sofas here are a lesson in how to make something simple feel luxurious. Some of the rooms were set up in the nineteen twenties as model middle-class interiors.

And then the moderns. Among the complete rooms on display is the gentleman's study designed by Adolf Loos for the apartment of Georg Roy. If you've been to Loos's buildings in the old city, this is a chance to see what he did indoors.

What I like most is the honesty. Most palaces show you furniture as it was meant to be seen. This shows you how it was actually kept.

[beat]

From here, wander the side streets of Neubau, the seventh district you're already standing in. It's full of small design shops and cafés, and it's the right neighbourhood to come down to earth in after an afternoon with emperors.

=== 31 haas-haus
Haas-Haus

Stand in front of St Stephen's Cathedral and turn round. The building behind you was, for a while, the most hated in Vienna.

You're on Stephansplatz, beside the great west front of the cathedral. Turn your back on it and look across the square, to where the Graben begins. On the corner is a building of pale stone and glass with a rounded, mirrored bay curving out towards the square. That's the Haas-Haus, by the Viennese architect Hans Hollein.

It opened in September nineteen ninety. The site has a long history. The first Haas-Haus, built in the eighteen sixties for a carpet firm, was one of Vienna's first department stores; it was destroyed by bombing at the end of the Second World War. A plain replacement went up in the fifties, and Hollein's building replaced that.

When the design was unveiled, there was uproar. A modern building, in glass, on the most sacred square in the city? Even more contested, the papers said, than the building it replaced.

Now look closely at that curved, mirrored bay. Stand in the right place and you'll see the cathedral reflected in it. That's Hollein's answer to his critics. He didn't try to compete with St Stephen's, and he didn't try to imitate it. He made a building that hands the cathedral back to you, broken into panes and slightly distorted, as if seen through a modern eye.

And here's the thing. Thirty-odd years on, most people walking past don't notice it at all. That might be the best compliment a building on this square can get.

[beat]

If you can, go up to the restaurant or café on the upper floors and look out of the window. You'll be eye to eye with the cathedral's patterned tile roof, and you'll see it the way Hollein must have pictured it from his drawing board.

=== 32 flakturm-augarten
Flakturm Augarten

In the middle of a baroque garden, among the chestnut trees, there's a concrete tower about fifty metres tall. Nobody knows what to do with it.

You're in the Augarten, in the second district, Vienna's oldest baroque park. Walk along the main chestnut avenue until you can see it over the trees: a massive, round, windowless concrete tower with a ring of platforms near the top. That's the flak tower, the Flakturm, and there's a second, square one elsewhere in the same park.

They were built during the Second World War, finished by nineteen forty-four, to designs by the German architect Friedrich Tamms. Vienna got six of them, in three pairs. In each pair, one tower, like this one, carried the anti-aircraft guns, and the other carried the radar and the fire control. They were also shelters for thousands of people during the air raids.

Look at the scale of it, and the walls. Reinforced concrete several metres thick. After the war the Soviet army tried a test explosion inside this one. The interior partly collapsed, but the walls stood. And because the towers are so close to housing, nobody has ever dared to blow them up properly.

So they've stayed. Both Augarten towers are now protected monuments. Over the years there have been plenty of proposals to give it a new use. None has stuck. So far, it has simply stood here, slowly being reclaimed by birds.

What's worth knowing is that this is one of the most honest things in Vienna. A city of palaces and coffee houses also has to carry this.

[beat]

When you've seen it, walk across the park to the baroque palace on its far side. That's the home of the Augarten porcelain manufactory, with a small museum and workshop tours. Few places put the elegant and the brutal quite so close together.

=== 33 yppenplatz
Yppenplatz

If the Naschmarkt is Vienna's market for visitors, this is the one for the people who live here.

You're on Yppenplatz, in Ottakring, the sixteenth district, just outside the old line of the Gürtel. Around you is a square lined with cafés and restaurants, with market pavilions and stalls on one side. Running off it, south along Brunnengasse, is a long street of market stalls. That's the Brunnenmarkt, and the square is its upper end.

The market goes back to the late eighteenth century, when this was the village of Neulerchenfeld, outside the city walls. Food sold inside Vienna was taxed; out here it wasn't, so traders and customers came out to the villages. The square had its own market by the end of the nineteenth century, and over time the two grew together.

Today the Brunnenmarkt runs for nearly a kilometre and has around a hundred and seventy stalls. It's often described as one of the longest permanent street markets in Europe, and it's certainly the most international market in Vienna, with a large share of Turkish and Balkan traders among many others.

Now look round the square itself. Over the past twenty years, Yppenplatz has become one of the city's liveliest corners, with bakeries, cafés and restaurants spilling outside on fine days. The interesting bit is that the market never left. The new places have settled in around it rather than replacing it.

Saturday is the day to come. In the morning, farmers from around Vienna set up on Yppenplatz with honey, cheese, vegetables and wine, right beside the regular stalls.

[beat]

Before you go, walk the whole length of the Brunnenmarkt, from here down to Thaliastraße, and back. Buy something you can't name, ask the stallholder how to cook it, and you'll have done the Brunnenmarkt properly.

=== 34 friedhofskirche-zentralfriedhof
Church of St Charles Borromeo, Central Cemetery

In the middle of a city of the dead, there's a church with a sky-blue dome. Vienna's relationship with its cemeteries is famously affectionate, and this is its centrepiece.

You're in the Central Cemetery, the Zentralfriedhof, in Simmering, at the far south-east of Vienna. From the main gate, walk straight ahead up the long central avenue. At the end, raised up on a terrace with broad steps, is a great domed church in pale stone. That's the Church of St Charles Borromeo.

It was designed by the architect Max Hegele, a pupil of Otto Wagner, and built between nineteen hundred and eight and nineteen hundred and eleven. Hegele had his design worked out years earlier, by eighteen ninety-nine, but construction took almost a decade to begin. It's considered his masterpiece, and one of the finest Jugendstil churches in Vienna, alongside Wagner's own Kirche am Steinhof.

Locals often call it the Lueger church, because the crypt beneath it holds the tomb of Karl Lueger, mayor of Vienna at the turn of the twentieth century. He's a deeply controversial figure today, as an openly antisemitic politician, and that's part of what you're standing in front of too.

Now climb the steps and go in, if it's open. The interior is a complete work of art, painted and decorated by a team of artists, and the dome is painted a deep sky blue. Coming in from the grey of the cemetery, it's quite something.

The three flights of steps leading up to the church are often said to represent three paths to God: scripture, the contemplation of nature, and the contemplation of the good and beautiful.

[beat]

Afterwards, find the musicians' graves near the main avenue, Group thirty-two A. Beethoven, Schubert, Brahms and Johann Strauss are all there, within a few metres of each other. Come early on a misty morning if you can, when the cemetery is at its most beautiful.

=== 35 kahlenberg
Kahlenberg

From up here you can see the whole of Vienna at once: the old city, the river, the plain beyond. In sixteen eighty-three, an army stood on this spot and looked down at a city under siege.

You're on the summit of the Kahlenberg, at the north-western edge of the city, in the Vienna Woods. Walk to the viewing terrace. Below you, vineyards drop away down the slopes to the villages of Grinzing and Nussdorf. Beyond them, the whole city spreads out, with the spire of St Stephen's in the middle and the Danube on your left.

In September sixteen eighty-three, Vienna had been besieged by the Ottoman army for two months. The relief army, under the Polish king Jan Sobieski, gathered on these hills, and on the twelfth of September came down through the vineyards to break the siege. The little baroque church of St Joseph behind you, the Josefskirche, recalls it. It was destroyed in the siege, rebuilt by seventeen thirty-four, and today it has a memorial chapel to Sobieski.

Now look at the view as an architect would. You can read the shape of the city from here. The tight knot of the old centre, ringed by the Ringstraße. The big outer belt of nineteenth-century districts. Then the Danube, and the modern towers of the Donau City across it. Vienna grew outward in rings, and from up here you can count them.

The terrace is open at all hours and it's free. Sunset is the busiest time; early morning is the most beautiful.

[beat]

Don't take the bus back down. Take one of the footpaths down through the vineyards towards Grinzing or Nussdorf, and stop at a Heuriger on the way for a glass of the wine you've just walked past. That, more or less, is how the Viennese spend a Sunday.
