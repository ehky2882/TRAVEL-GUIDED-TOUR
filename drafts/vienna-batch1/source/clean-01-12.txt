=== 01 hotel-altstadt-vienna
Hotel Altstadt Vienna

A hotel is usually one person's idea, repeated sixty times. This one is sixty-two ideas, and almost none of them agree.

Kirchengasse, number forty-one, on the slope of Spittelberg. You're looking at a tall, pale patrician house with a modest front door and nothing in particular to announce it, which is rather the point. Behind you and downhill are the narrow lanes of Spittelberg itself, all Biedermeier fronts and cobbles.

The story starts with one man, Otto Ernst Wiesenthal, who wanted his own hotel and finally got one about thirty-five years ago. Instead of fitting out sixty-two identical rooms, he handed them to different hands. Some were designed by the Milanese architect Matteo Thun, others by the fashion designer Atıl Kutoğlu, and the newest is a suite by Elfrid Wimmer-Repp themed on Sigmund Freud. No two rooms here are alike, and they're not meant to be.

Then there's the art. Up the stairs and along the corridors you'll find Warhol, Brigitte Kowanz, Prachensky, Attersee, Helnwein. Some of it is on loan from museums, some belongs to Wiesenthal himself. It isn't hung like a gallery. It's hung like a house where someone collects rather more than the walls can comfortably hold.

What I like, as an architect, is that the building doesn't try to be anything. The old house does the old-house job, high ceilings and deep windows, and lets the rooms argue with each other inside it. It's a very Viennese idea: a calm façade, and a great deal going on behind it.

[beat]

Before you head off, walk down to Spittelberggasse and wander the lanes for ten minutes. The little Biedermeier houses are the reason this neighbourhood is protected at all, and they're at their best early in the morning, before the bars open.

=== 02 the-guesthouse-vienna
The Guesthouse Vienna

Some hotels shout from across the street. This one would rather you noticed the door handles.

Führichgasse, number ten, in the old city. Find the hotel's front, a plain townhouse with a brasserie at street level, then turn round. Directly opposite, up on its raised terrace, is the Albertina, the great palace of drawings and prints. The brasserie windows look straight across at it.

The design is by Sir Terence Conran and his studio, Conran and Partners, and their brief, as the hotel puts it, was no theatrical gestures and no design for design's sake. What they did instead was fill the rooms with Vienna's own modern furniture. There are pieces from the Carl Auböck workshop, the family studio that has been making brass and leather objects here for generations. There's seating by Wittmann. There are designs by Oswald Haerdtl. And beside a small library of Austrian literature, there's Friedrich Kiesler's cantilever chair.

That last one is worth a moment. Kiesler left Vienna in the nineteen twenties and became one of the great experimental architects of New York. Finding his chair here, next to the books, feels a little like a homecoming.

The brasserie downstairs is open every day of the year, from early morning to late evening. Bread comes in fresh each morning from the organic bakery Öfferl, the coffee is a house roast from the old Viennese roaster Naber, and the wine list sticks to Austria.

Here's the thing about it. The Guesthouse isn't really a Conran interior in Vienna. It's a Viennese interior, edited by an Englishman who knew when to stop.

[beat]

If you have twenty minutes, climb the ramp to the Albertina terrace opposite. Stand by the balustrade and look back at the hotel. The State Opera is a few steps away, and the view over the Burggarten palm house is one of the best free ones in the city.

=== 03 grand-ferdinand
Grand Ferdinand

The Ringstraße was built to impress, and most of it still does. This building was put up long after the party, and it's all the better for it.

Schubertring, numbers ten to twelve. Stand on the far side of the Ring and look back across the tram lines. You'll see a long, flat, very upright façade with rows of identical windows: the Grand Ferdinand. The Opera is a few minutes' walk one way along the Ring, the Stadtpark a few minutes the other.

It went up in the early nineteen fifties as an office building, and you can tell. It's tidy and rhythmical, with none of the stucco and statues of its nineteenth-century neighbours. That plainness is exactly why the façade and the marble foyer behind it are now protected. When the Vienna architects Atelier Heiss turned it into a hotel, opening in twenty fifteen, they kept the front and the entrance hall and rebuilt much of what lay behind.

Inside, the owner, the hotelier Florian Weitzer, did something nicely contrary. He hung handmade chandeliers by Lobmeyr, the glassmakers round the corner on Kärntner Straße since eighteen twenty-three, under ceilings about seven metres high. Post-war office, imperial crystal.

Then there's the roof. Eight floors up is a heated infinity pool, open every day of the year, for hotel guests only. There's also a rooftop restaurant and a breakfast room with views across to St Stephen's. And downstairs, Meissl and Schadn serves the schnitzel that Viennese families argue about.

And one more resident: the hotel keeps a horse. He's called Pferdinand.

[beat]

When you've seen it, walk along the Ring into the Stadtpark. The gilded statue of Johann Strauss playing his violin is a few minutes away, and beyond it the Wien river runs between Jugendstil stone embankments, one of the city's loveliest bits of engineering.

=== 04 hotel-daniel-vienna
Hotel Daniel Vienna

Look up at the roof. There's a full-size sailing boat on it, and it's bent like a banana.

You're on Landstraßer Gürtel, a few minutes' walk from the Belvedere, with the new main railway station close by. In front of you is a crisp glass block, its façade a grid of glass and pale anodised aluminium. That's Hotel Daniel, and the curved boat on top is by the Austrian artist Erwin Wurm.

The building is older than it looks. It was designed in nineteen sixty-two by Georg Lippert and Roland Rohn as offices for a pharmaceutical company, and it's considered the first building in Austria with a curtain wall, a façade hung on the frame like a coat rather than built up like a wall. That's why the front and the staircase are now protected. When Atelier Heiss converted it into a hotel in twenty eleven, they kept the original window frames, which were still in good order after half a century.

Inside, they were honest about it. The concrete ceilings were left raw, and you can still see the marks where the old office partitions once stood. The rooms are deliberately compact, slotted between the existing columns, with walnut bedheads and green mosaic showers.

The ground floor is one big open room: lobby, lounge and a bakery, open to anyone walking in off the street. And out in the grounds there's a silver American caravan from nineteen fifty-two, converted into a single hotel room with its own bathtub.

What I like about it is the attitude. Nobody pretended this was a palace. They took a good modern building, cleaned it up, and let it be itself.

[beat]

From here it's five minutes to the gate of the Upper Belvedere. Walk through the garden and look back down towards the city; on a clear day the spire of St Stephen's sits right in the middle of the view.

=== 05 steirereck
Steirereck

In nineteen hundred and three, this was where the Viennese came to drink a glass of milk in the park.

You're in the Stadtpark, on the path along the Wien river, with the river's stone embankment beside you. In front of you, set into the bank, is a pale, villa-like pavilion, and wrapped around it a cluster of low, angular extensions in a slightly hazy, reflective metal. That's Steirereck, and the old building at its heart is the Meierei, the park's original dairy.

The Meierei opened as a milk-drinking hall, a very respectable idea for a public park at the time. It was badly damaged in the Second World War, rebuilt, and listed in two thousand and four. Then in January two thousand and five, the Reitbauer family moved their restaurant in here after about thirty-five years elsewhere in the city.

The newer parts you're looking at came later. The Viennese studio PPAG architects won a competition to extend and renovate the building, and finished in twenty fourteen. They describe their pavilions as finger-like, and they designed them, they say, by working outward from individual tables. Every table sits right against the façade, with its own view of the park. The metal cladding is meant to look as if it's coated with dew. Look closely and the trees seem to blur into it.

And on the roof, where you can't see it from here, there's a herb garden.

That's the clever bit, if you ask me. A temple of serious cooking that has decided to look like part of the park rather than a monument in it.

[beat]

If a full dinner isn't on the cards, the Meierei on the lower level is a separate, more relaxed place with a great reputation for cheese. Or simply follow the river path a little further and look back: from the far bank, the mirrored pavilions almost disappear into the green.

=== 06 cafe-prueckel
Café Prückel

Most Viennese coffee houses want you to feel you've stepped back into eighteen ninety. This one stopped the clock at nineteen fifty-five.

You're at the corner of the Stubenring and Dr-Karl-Lueger-Platz, with the Ringstraße trams going past and the Museum of Applied Arts, the MAK, across the Ring. On the corner in front of you is a café with a long run of big, plain windows and an awning over the terrace. That's Café Prückel.

It opened on Christmas Day, nineteen hundred and three, under another name entirely, Café Lurion, after its founder, Maxime Lurion, a former European cycling champion. The building above was by Jakob Gartner, and the original interior was all historicist plush.

Then, in nineteen fifty-five, the architect Oswald Haerdtl was asked to modernise it. He took out the pillars and made the window openings much bigger. The result is the room you can see through the glass: light, open and quietly fifties. It's a post-war café interior that Vienna now protects as a monument, which tells you how far opinion has travelled. The rooms at the back went the other way and were rebuilt in Jugendstil style in the nineteen eighties, so you can sit in two eras at once.

Downstairs, there's a third story. In November nineteen thirty-one, the actress Stella Kadmon opened a cabaret in the basement called Der liebe Augustin. Until nineteen thirty-eight it was a stage for artists who had fled Nazi Germany, and the cellar has been a small theatre, on and off, ever since.

[beat]

Go in, take a window seat on the Ring side, and order a Prückel Creme: a small mocha with a little jug of cream on the side. Then, when you leave, walk two minutes down the Stubenring to Otto Wagner's Post Office Savings Bank, which this room would make an excellent argument with.

=== 07 zum-schwarzen-kameel
Zum Schwarzen Kameel

There has been a shop selling good things to eat on this spot since sixteen eighteen. The camel came with the founder.

You're on Bognergasse, number five, a narrow street just off the Graben. In front of you is the shop with the camel in its name, and through the windows a long counter, usually with a small crowd standing at it. The restaurant is on the floor above.

In sixteen eighteen, a man called Johann Baptist Cameel bought the house and opened a spice shop under his own name. Kameel, camel: a pun that has outlived him by four centuries. Two hundred years later the Stiebitz family added a wine tavern, the business became a supplier to the imperial court, and Beethoven is listed among the guests. The painter Ferdinand Georg Waldmüller drew the camel logo you're looking at.

In nineteen hundred and one the whole building was rebuilt, and the interior was done in Viennese Jugendstil by the firm Portois and Fix. That's the restaurant upstairs, and the house rightly calls it a Jugendstil jewel.

Downstairs is where the Viennese actually go. The counter is famous for very small open sandwiches, ham carved by hand, and an array of spreads. People stand, eat two or three, have a glass of wine and leave. It's an eleven-in-the-morning habit as much as an evening one.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that the Kameel isn't preserved because someone decided it was heritage. It's preserved because people never stopped coming.

[beat]

Before you go, walk ten paces to the end of Bognergasse and look into the square Am Hof, one of the largest in the old city. And on your way back to the Graben, have a look at the Engel Apotheke at number nine, with its gilded Jugendstil angels on the façade.

=== 08 gasthaus-poeschl
Gasthaus Pöschl

This is what Vienna means by a Beisl: a neighbourhood inn with no pretensions, wooden panelling, and a schnitzel that fills the plate.

You're on Weihburggasse, number seventeen, a step or two from the corner of Franziskanerplatz. In front of you is an unassuming shopfront with the name above it, and through the windows wood panelling, small tables, and very little else. Just round the corner to your left is the little square with the Franciscan church and its fountain.

The man behind it is Hanno Pöschl, an actor you may have seen in Before Sunrise, and a Viennese institution in his own right. He's run the Kleines Café on the square round the corner since the nineteen seventies. Around the turn of the millennium he took over this old wine room and made it a Gasthaus, and according to the city's weekly, Falter, it was the architect Hermann Czech, his collaborator on the café, who did the conversion.

That's the interesting bit, if you're an architect. Czech is famous for interiors that barely seem designed at all, rooms that look as though they've simply always been like that. Here that's exactly what you get. Nothing is new for the sake of being new. The kitchen sends out the classics, the room stays out of the way, and the Viennese come back for both.

The menu changes with the seasons, but this is the place for the dishes you came to Vienna for, done properly. It's open from lunchtime until late most days, and it's small, so it's worth booking.

[beat]

When you come out, walk round the corner onto Franziskanerplatz and stand by the Moses fountain in the middle. On the far side, at number three, is the Kleines Café, the other half of this story. Sit outside if there's a table free.

=== 09 mayer-am-pfarrplatz
Mayer am Pfarrplatz

In the summer of eighteen seventeen, a deaf composer rented rooms in this house and worked on his Ninth Symphony.

You're on Pfarrplatz in Heiligenstadt, in the vineyard country at the edge of Vienna. In front of you is a long, low old house with a gateway and a courtyard full of tables behind it. To one side is the little parish church of St Jakob. That's Mayer am Pfarrplatz, and the house is the one where Beethoven lived that year.

It's a Heuriger, which is the most Viennese institution of all: a wine tavern run by the people who grow the wine. Wine has been made here, they say, since sixteen eighty-three. The vines are on the hills right behind you, and the wine in your glass came from them.

Beethoven came to Heiligenstadt more than once. It was a rural village then, and he hoped the spa next door would help with his failing hearing. It didn't. Fifteen years earlier, in eighteen oh two, he had written his despairing letter to his brothers in this same village, the Heiligenstadt Testament. By the time he stayed in this house, he was working on the symphony that ends with the Ode to Joy.

His rooms are on the first floor, reached by a small staircase from the terrace, and they're marked to this day.

Now have a look at the courtyard itself. This is how a Heuriger works: a buffet of hot and cold food, wine by the quarter-litre, long tables you share, and in the evenings, often, a pair of musicians playing old Viennese songs. Nobody's in a hurry.

[beat]

When you're done, look for the Beethovengang, a path a few minutes' walk away that follows a little stream through the trees towards the vineyards. It's named after the walks he took there, and it's still the nicest way to leave Heiligenstadt.

=== 10 kleines-cafe
Kleines Café

The name means the small café, and they weren't exaggerating.

You're on Franziskanerplatz, a tiny square in the old city with the Franciscan church on one side and a fountain of Moses in the middle. Find number three. There are two doors and two little rooms, one slightly higher than the other, and in good weather a scatter of chairs and tables out on the square. That's the Kleines Café.

It was one of the first works of the architect Hermann Czech, done in stages from nineteen seventy. He fitted out the lower room first, then added the upper room in nineteen seventy-three and seventy-four. If you ask me, he's the architect's architect in Vienna, and this is where his reputation started.

What he does here is make a very small space behave like a larger one. The proportions are tight, rather like a submarine. But mirrors are placed to stretch the room, and every centimetre has a job. It doesn't look designed. It looks as though it has always been here, which is the hardest effect of all to achieve.

Now look down, if you're inside the lower room. In nineteen seventy-seven Czech laid old gravestones in the floor, face down, inscriptions hidden. It was a reply to the sculptor Karl Prantl, who had proposed paving Stephansplatz with discarded gravestones.

The café belongs to the actor Hanno Pöschl, and if it seems oddly familiar, that may be because it appears in Before Sunrise.

[beat]

Take a table on the square if you can, then walk round the corner onto Weihburggasse. Gasthaus Pöschl, at number seventeen, is the same owner and the same architect, a quarter of a century later.

=== 11 das-loft
Das LOFT

Wait until it gets dark, then look up at the top of the tower beside the canal. The ceiling is glowing.

You're on Praterstraße, on the Leopoldstadt side of the Danube Canal, at the foot of the bridge. In front of you is a tall, dark glass tower. That's the hotel now called SO/ Vienna, which opened as the Sofitel in December twenty ten. The bar and restaurant on the top floor, the eighteenth, is Das LOFT.

The building is by the French architect Jean Nouvel. From down here it reads as a crisp, almost severe block of dark glass. The surprise is overhead. The ceilings of the top floor are covered by a huge video and light installation by the Swiss artist Pipilotti Rist: slow, shifting colour, a bit like looking up through a kaleidoscope. At night it shines out through the glass and you can see it from the street, and from half the old city.

Go up, and the view is the other half of the experience. The Danube Canal is right below you. Across the water is the whole of the old city, with St Stephen's Cathedral spire rising out of it, and the green hills of the Vienna Woods on the horizon.

Here's what I find rather lovely about it. Vienna is a city that's generally nervous of tall new buildings in the middle of its old views. This one turns the tables: it isn't a tower you look at so much as a place you look out from.

[beat]

Come back down and walk across the bridge to Schwedenplatz, then turn left along the canal towards the Urania. In summer the whole bank is lined with bars and benches, and the graffiti on the canal walls is some of the best in the city.

=== 12 cafe-sperl
Café Sperl

If you want to know what a Viennese coffee house was like in eighteen eighty, this is still one of the best answers.

You're on Gumpendorfer Straße, at the corner of Lehárgasse, a few minutes' walk from the Naschmarkt. In front of you is a broad corner café, and through the glass, with luck, the green of billiard tables. That's Café Sperl.

It opened in eighteen eighty as Café Ronacher, and was bought five years later by the Sperl family, whose name it has kept ever since. The interior was designed in the Ringstraße style by Wilhelm Jelinek and Anton Groß, and it has survived remarkably intact: parquet floors, marble tables, bentwood Thonet chairs, a crystal chandelier, and the carom billiard tables you can see from here. The last major renovation, in nineteen eighty-three, was done carefully, and the whole room is now protected.

Think about who sat here. Officers from the military academy round the corner. The composer Franz Lehár, whose name is on the side street. And between eighteen ninety-five and the Second World War, a regular table of artists, the Hagengesellschaft, while Josef Hoffmann and Koloman Moser met here with friends in the years before they founded the Vienna Secession.

And here's the thing. Nobody comes to Café Sperl for novelty. They come because it doesn't change. A coffee here comes on a small silver tray with a glass of water, and nobody will rush you if you sit with it all afternoon.

[beat]

When you leave, walk two minutes down towards the Naschmarkt and look at the two buildings by Otto Wagner on the Linke Wienzeile, numbers thirty-eight and forty. One has gilded medallions; the other is covered in painted majolica roses. They're the best argument going for crossing the road.
