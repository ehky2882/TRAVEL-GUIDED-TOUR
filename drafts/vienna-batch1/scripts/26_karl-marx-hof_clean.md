# Karl-Marx-Hof

This single building is about a kilometre long. It was built to house five thousand people, and to make a point.

You're on Heiligenstädter Straße, in the nineteenth district, at the square called Zwölfter-Februar-Platz, a short walk from Heiligenstadt station. In front of you is a vast wall of housing in red and ochre, broken by enormous arches, with flagpole towers rising above the central block. That's the Karl-Marx-Hof.

It was designed by Karl Ehn, a pupil of Otto Wagner, and built between nineteen twenty-seven and nineteen thirty for the city's Social Democratic government, the period known as Red Vienna. It holds one thousand three hundred and eighty-two flats, each between thirty and sixty square metres. Modest homes, but with running water, light and air, which most working families in Vienna had never had.

Now here's the number that matters most. Only about eighteen and a half per cent of the site is built on. Everything else is gardens, courtyards and playgrounds. And the estate came with laundries, baths, kindergartens, a library and doctors' surgeries. It's not just housing. It's a piece of a city.

The architecture was political, too, and people read it that way. In February nineteen thirty-four, during Austria's brief civil war, workers barricaded themselves inside, and the army shelled the building until they surrendered. The damage was repaired in the nineteen fifties.

Today the Karl-Marx-Hof is still council housing, still lived in, and still owned by the city. In one of the old communal laundries, there's a small museum about Red Vienna called the Waschsalon. It's open on limited days, so check before you go.
