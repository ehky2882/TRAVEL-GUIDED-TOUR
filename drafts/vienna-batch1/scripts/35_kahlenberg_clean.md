# Kahlenberg

From up here you can see the whole of Vienna at once: the old city, the river, the plain beyond. In sixteen eighty-three, an army stood on this spot and looked down at a city under siege.

You're on the summit of the Kahlenberg, at the north-western edge of the city, in the Vienna Woods. Walk to the viewing terrace. Below you, vineyards drop away down the slopes to the villages of Grinzing and Nussdorf. Beyond them, the whole city spreads out, with the spire of St Stephen's in the middle and the Danube on your left.

In September sixteen eighty-three, Vienna had been besieged by the Ottoman army for two months. The relief army, under the Polish king Jan Sobieski, gathered on these hills, and on the twelfth of September came down through the vineyards to break the siege. The little baroque church of St Joseph behind you, the Josefskirche, recalls it. It was destroyed in the siege, rebuilt by seventeen thirty-four, and today it has a memorial chapel to Sobieski.

Now look at the view as an architect would. You can read the shape of the city from here. The tight knot of the old centre, ringed by the Ringstraße. The big outer belt of nineteenth-century districts. Then the Danube, and the modern towers of the Donau City across it. Vienna grew outward in rings, and from up here you can count them.

The terrace is open at all hours and it's free. Sunset is the busiest time; early morning is the most beautiful.

Don't take the bus back down. Take one of the footpaths down through the vineyards towards Grinzing or Nussdorf, and stop at a Heuriger on the way for a glass of the wine you've just walked past. That, more or less, is how the Viennese spend a Sunday.
