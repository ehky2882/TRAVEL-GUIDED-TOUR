Otto Wagner Pavilion Karlsplatz

This is a railway station that the city nearly threw away.

You're on Karlsplatz, in the open space between the Ring and the great baroque dome of the Karlskirche. In front of you are two small, matching pavilions facing each other, built of thin white marble panels held in a frame of green-painted iron, with gilded sunflowers spreading across the edges of their roofs. Pick one and walk up to it.

They were built in eighteen ninety-eight and ninety-nine, as entrances to the new Stadtbahn, Vienna's city railway. Otto Wagner designed the whole network, with help from his assistant Joseph Maria Olbrich, who went on to design the Secession. Most of Wagner's stations are plastered; these two are different, a steel framework hung with marble slabs.

Now look at the colours. The ironwork is painted a particular apple green, the signal colour of the whole Stadtbahn, and you'll see it on Wagner's bridges and railings all over the city. The gold sunflowers are an Art Nouveau signature, and they're what makes these two stand out from their plainer sisters.

In the nineteen seventies, when the line was rebuilt as the underground, the pavilions were scheduled for demolition. There was a public outcry. They were taken apart piece by piece, restored, and put back up in nineteen seventy-seven, about a metre and a half higher than before, to sit above the new tunnels.

One is now a café. The other belongs to the Wien Museum and has a small permanent exhibition about Otto Wagner.

From here, walk across to the Karlskirche and look at its two giant columns, carved with spirals like Trajan's in Rome. Then turn round and look back at Wagner's little pavilions. Nearly two centuries apart, and both trying to make Vienna feel like the centre of the world.
