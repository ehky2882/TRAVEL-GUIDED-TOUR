## Script (clean)

Prélude. Fugue. Cantate. Sonate. Somebody here had a sense of humour about tower blocks.

Avenue de Flandre, in the nineteenth arrondissement, somewhere between the Crimée and Riquet metro stations. Above you, a group of towers whose tops step down in stages, like the pipes of a church organ. Hence the name: the Orgues de Flandre, the organs of Flandre. The tallest of them is Prélude.

They were designed by the German architect Martin Schulz van Treeck and went up through the nineteen seventies, the last of them finished in nineteen eighty. Altogether there are about one thousand nine hundred and fifty homes on six hectares.

Have a look at Prélude first. It's a hundred and twenty-three metres tall, with thirty-nine floors, and it's often described as the tallest residential building in Paris. Not long after it went up, the city tightened its rules on height, and nobody has built a taller home inside Paris since.

Now look at the way the towers are shaped. They aren't sheer slabs. The upper floors step back in tiers, so the skyline rises and falls as you walk past, like a chord being played. The names, every one of them a piece of music, finish the joke.

And this site has an older story. Before the towers, it was a nineteenth-century workers' estate known as the cité des Flamands. It was pulled down to make room, but its gateway, dating from eighteen fifty, was kept and moved about forty metres. It's now called the Porte des Flamands.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that the towers were not universally loved. They were one of the last great tower projects inside Paris, and they arrived just as the city was turning against towers altogether. Fifty years on, they're officially recognised as remarkable contemporary architecture, and Prélude and Sonate were re-clad in steel at the end of the twenty-tens.

[beat]

Cédric Klapisch filmed parts of Le Péril jeune around here in the nineteen nineties, if you want to see the place on screen.

Before you go, find the Porte des Flamands, the old gate that survived the demolition. Then walk south-west for a few minutes to the Bassin de la Villette, and look back. From across the water you get the whole skyline at once.
