## Script (clean)

Before these towers, this ground grew vegetables. So the nickname was always going to write itself.

Boulevard Pablo-Picasso, Créteil. Around you stand round concrete towers, each covered in curving balconies that overlap like leaves. Hence the name: les Choux, the cabbages. The university is to the north, and the Lac de Créteil is a short walk to the south. One of these towers is now a student residence.

They were designed by Gérard Grandval and built between nineteen sixty-nine and nineteen seventy-four: ten round towers, each about twenty-one metres across and most around fifteen floors high, plus one lower ring-shaped building. The plain they stand on was market-garden country, and it was also home to the Choucrouterie Benoist, said to be the biggest sauerkraut factory in the Paris region. Cabbages, very much on brand.

Have a look at the balconies. Each one is a curved concrete shell, and they're staggered across the facade in a chequerboard, so no balcony sits directly on top of the next. Grandval said he wanted forms that were austere in their mass but supple and plant-like in their shape.

Now imagine them green. Grandval meant every one of those balconies to hold planters, so the towers would be covered in plants: a vertical garden, decades before the idea became fashionable. The developer said no.

And here's the bit I like. In nineteen ninety-eight the central tower was rebuilt, and the town set aside a share of its flats for students, to mix the generations. Today it's run as a student residence with a hundred and seventy-two small studios, a few minutes from the university. So some of the people living in Grandval's cabbages were born a good forty years after he drew them.

[beat]

The towers have had more screen time than most estates. They turn up in Tellement proches and in Le Brio, and in two thousand and eight they were labelled as part of France's twentieth-century heritage.

Before you leave, walk south to the Lac de Créteil and look back across the water. And one small curiosity to take with you: this is boulevard Pablo-Picasso, and the Aillaud towers in Nanterre and the Arènes in Noisy-le-Grand both have Picasso addresses too. The painter turns up all over the post-war suburbs.
