## Script (clean)

"Versailles for the people." That was the line, and whether it was a compliment or a jibe rather depended on who was saying it.

Find a spot on the shore of the Lac de la Sourderie, in Montigny-le-Bretonneux, where you can see a long building running straight out across the water on a row of arches. That's Le Viaduc. Behind it, on dry land, are the blocks of Les Arcades du Lac, laid out in a grid of straight avenues and enclosed squares.

This was the work of the Catalan architect Ricardo Bofill and his practice, the Taller de Arquitectura, finished in the early nineteen eighties. It was their first major building in France, and it was social housing: three hundred and eighty-nine flats in the Arcades and seventy-four more out on the Viaduc, built quickly and cheaply for one of the new towns the state was raising around Paris.

Have a look at the plan first, if you can picture it from above. It's a French formal garden, the kind you'd find behind a château, except the clipped hedges have become buildings. The long walks between them are streets. And the cars were put underground, so those streets belong to people on foot.

Now the Viaduc. Bofill's office cited the Pont d'Avignon, the Roman aqueduct at Segovia, and the château of Chenonceau, which spans a river on arches of its own. Here the same idea carries flats instead of a royal gallery. And because the blocks are only eight to ten metres deep, every flat has windows on both sides.

The interesting bit is this. Most social housing of the time said, quite plainly, that it was social housing. Bofill took the language of palaces and gave it to people on modest incomes. Critics argued about whether that was generous or theatrical, and they still do.

[beat]

Stand here long enough and the argument starts to feel beside the point. The reflection of those arches on the water is the reflection of somebody's kitchen window.

From here, walk away from the lake into the Arcades themselves and find one of the inner squares. Then come back to the shore and look at the Viaduc from further along the bank. It's one of those buildings that changes completely with every hundred metres you walk.
