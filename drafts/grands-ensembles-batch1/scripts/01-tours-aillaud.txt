The Aillaud Towers

Clouds. That's what these towers were meant to be. Not so much the shape, though there's a bit of that. The skin.

Avenue Pablo-Picasso, Nanterre, on the edge of the Parc André-Malraux. In front of you, a cluster of rounded, lobed towers covered in tiny coloured tiles: blues, greens, pinks and white. There are eighteen of them, in a range of heights, and the two tallest reach about a hundred and five metres. Turn round and the glass of La Défense is less than a kilometre away. Quite a contrast.

Have a look at the facades first. Every one of those tiles is a little square of glass paste, about two centimetres across. The patterns were worked out by the architect, Émile Aillaud, with his son-in-law, the painter Fabio Rieti, and they show clouds, trees and sky, so the towers half dissolve into the weather around them. No two towers are dressed alike, either. The idea was that you could look up from the ground and pick out your own home.

Then the windows. Some are square, some round, some shaped like a drop of water. Look closely and you'll find that no window sits directly above another. Aillaud called it a very carefully arranged chance.

And here's the thing about the date. Work began here in nineteen seventy-three, the same year the French government called a halt to the vast estates it called grands ensembles. They had gone up in a hurry after the war, rows of identical slabs, and the country had decided it had built quite enough of them. These towers are among the very last, and they read like a reply to everything that came before. Aillaud wrote a memoir in nineteen seventy-five called Désordre apparent, ordre caché: apparent disorder, hidden order. That's the whole place, really. It looks like whimsy, and it's planned down to the last tile.

Not everyone was charmed. The story goes that President Giscard d'Estaing saw the first two towers going up and asked for the rest to be built lower. Look at the skyline and you can see where that wish landed: two giants, and everything else stepping down.

Before you leave, follow the winding footpath that runs between the towers. It's called the Serpent, and it was designed by Florence Rieti. Walk it slowly and keep looking up. The tiles change with the light, and on a grey afternoon the towers really do go a bit cloudy.
