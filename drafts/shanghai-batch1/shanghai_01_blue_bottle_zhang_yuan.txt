ATLAS · SHANGHAI · 01 · Blue Bottle Zhang Yuan Café
---
The coffee here is made inside a shed. The shed is inside a house. And the house is inside what was once the most famous garden in Shanghai.

You're in Zhang Yuan, off North Maoming Road, in a lane of grey brick houses with heavy stone frames round their front doors. Those stone-framed doors are the reason this kind of house is called shikumen, which means, more or less, stone gate. The café is in one of them, at two-forty North Maoming Road. Step inside and you'll see a low, sloping roof of steel standing in the middle of an old room, quite separate from the walls around it.

Start with the garden, because there's no sign of it now. Zhang Yuan opened to the public in eighteen eighty-five, and for a while it was the place to be seen in the city. People called it the number one public garden of Shanghai. There were operas and boxing matches here, an early photographer's booth in the open air, and, the story goes, one of the first electric lamps ever lit in China. Later, the garden was built over with these lanes, and it became a neighbourhood. It's now the largest and best-preserved shikumen complex in the city, reopened in twenty twenty-two after a long restoration.

Now the café. It was designed by the Shanghai studio Neri and Hu, and the rules were strict. The old brick walls, doors, windows and light wells all had to stay exactly as they were. So the architects didn't touch them. They put a little steel hut in the middle of the room, under a steep roof, and that's where the coffee is made. They called it a primitive shelter.

Have a look at the old columns too. They've been fitted with metal rods and small ledges, so you can lean on them, perch on them, or rest a cup there.

And here's the thing. That's exactly what people living in these lanes always did: a shelf nailed to a wall, a bench pushed into an alley, a bit of borrowed space. The café isn't copying the architecture. It's copying the habits.

[beat]

When you've finished, wander on through the rest of Zhang Yuan's lanes. Walk slowly, and look at the carvings above the stone doorways as you go. No two are quite the same.
