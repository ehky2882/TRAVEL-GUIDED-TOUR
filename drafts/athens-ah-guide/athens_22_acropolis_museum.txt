ATLAS · ATHENS · 22 · Acropolis Museum
---

Before you go in, look down. The museum is standing on stilts, and there's an ancient neighbourhood underneath it.

You should be on the open forecourt in front of the Acropolis Museum, at the foot of the Acropolis, on the pedestrian street of Dionysiou Areopagitou. The building is all concrete and glass, and the ground in front of you is partly glass too, with ruins visible beneath your feet.

That's the first gift. When they came to build here, on the site of an old gendarmerie barracks, the diggers found two layers of ancient Athens: houses and workshops from the classical city and, on top of them, the early Byzantine one. The architect, Bernard Tschumi, working with the Greek architect Michael Photiadis, lifted the whole museum off the ground on columns, placing each one where it would miss the ruins. Those columns go down to bedrock, and they rest on bearings designed to ride out a severe earthquake.

Now look up to the top floor. You'll notice it's turned at an angle to the rest of the building. It's rotated by about twenty three degrees so that it lines up exactly with the Parthenon, which you can see up on the hill. That top floor is the Parthenon Gallery, glass all round, roughly the size of the temple itself, and the sculptures of the frieze are arranged in it exactly as they once were on the building, so you can look from the carving to the temple and back again.

Getting the museum built took decades. The first design competition was in nineteen seventy six. The building finally opened in June two thousand and nine, and moving the sculptures the few hundred metres from the rock took three tower cranes and four months.

[beat]

And here's why the building matters. For years, one of the arguments for keeping the Parthenon sculptures in London was that Athens had nowhere fit to show them. This museum is Athens' answer, built in glass, in full view of the temple. Up on the top floor, look for the panels in pale plaster among the marble. They're copies, standing in for the originals that are still in London.

Before you leave, have a coffee on the museum's terrace if it's open. And for the full picture, walk back along Dionysiou Areopagitou to the stone paths of Pikionis on Philopappou Hill, five minutes away.
