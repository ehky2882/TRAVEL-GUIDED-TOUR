Come in the morning, wear shoes you don't mind getting wet, and listen. This is the noisiest building in Athens.

You should be on ah thee nahs Street, in front of the big covered market. It fills an entire block between ah thee nahs, soh foh kleh ooss, ev ree pee doo and eh oh loo. Go through the main entrance and you'll find yourself under a long iron and glass roof, with the fish hall in the middle and the meat hall off to one side.

Before this was built, Athens bought its food from shacks set up around the ruins of the Roman Agora. The market was planned in eighteen seventy six and paid for by a benefactor called yah nees var vah kees, a sailor from the island of psah rah who made his fortune in Russia, partly in the caviar trade. The market still carries his name. It opened in eighteen eighty four, and the glass roof and basements followed two years later.

Have a look at the fish hall first. The city's official guide reckons five to ten tonnes of fish come through here every day, much of it from islands like Naxos, Paros, skee ross, kah leem noss and see mee. The sellers shout, the ice slides about underfoot, and some of the stalls are very old indeed. The koh rah kees family has had a pitch here since nineteen twenty six.

Then the meat hall, which is not for the squeamish. Carcasses hang in rows under bright lights, and the butchers will sell you every part of the animal. In the small canteens round the edge, they serve pah tsas, a tripe soup Athenians swear by after a long night out. One of them, Epirus, stays open round the clock on Fridays and Saturdays through the winter.

What's worth knowing is that this is not a food hall. Nobody has tidied it up for visitors. It's a working market, and the people buying here are cooks and grandmothers, not tourists. That's exactly what makes it worth seeing.

When you've had enough noise, cross ah thee nahs Street to soh krah toose and the basement taverna dee por toe, or wander down ev ree pee doo, where the old spice shops will find you before you find them.
