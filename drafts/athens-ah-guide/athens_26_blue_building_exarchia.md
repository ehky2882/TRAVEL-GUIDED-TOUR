# The Blue Building (Μπλε Πολυκατοικία)

**Atlas · Athens · 26 · Observe** — single-stop tour  
**Address:** Arachovis 61, Exarchia Square  
**Pin (WGS-84):** 37.986442, 23.735477  
**Length:** 342 words, about 2:17 spoken

---

Le Corbusier stood in this square in nineteen thirty three, looked at this building, and said it was very beautiful. He wasn't easily pleased.

You should be in Exarchia Square, looking at the big modernist apartment block on the corner of Arachovis and Themistokleous streets. Long horizontal balconies, flat roof, clean lines. Athenians call it the Blue Building, the Mple Polykatoikia, whatever colour it happens to be when you're standing here.

It was built in nineteen thirty two and thirty three, designed by the architect Kyriakoulis Panagiotakos for a client called Kostis Antonopoulos, who wanted large, modern flats for the new middle class of Athens. It's really two separate six storey buildings, joined together at the top.

Here's the gift detail. The famous blue was chosen by a painter, Spyros Papaloukas. A dark, deep blue, across the whole façade, in a city of white and ochre. It's been repainted over the years, and photographs from the two thousands show it not blue at all. So judge it for yourself.

And look at how much was shared. The original building had entrance halls with concierges, a laundry, generous staircases, and a meeting room up on the roof, later divided into seven more flats. There was even a swimming pool planned, never finished. Down at street level, a café called Floral opened in nineteen thirty six.

The residents were a roll call of Greek culture: the actors Katina Paxinou and Alexis Minotis, the singer Sofia Vembo, with writers and politicians among them.

*[beat]*

Here's what makes this building matter. Athens is a city of apartment blocks, thousands upon thousands of them, most built quickly and cheaply. This is what the idea looked like at the very start, before it was made ordinary: shared, generous, and genuinely modern. Every polykatoikia in the city is a distant cousin.

Take a seat in the square and look up at the balconies for a while. Then walk round the corner on to Themistokleous Street, to see how the two halves of the building fit together from the side.
