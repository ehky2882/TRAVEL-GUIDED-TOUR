# Hotel Grande Bretagne

**Atlas · Athens · 05 · Stay** — single-stop tour  
**Address:** Vasileos Georgiou A' 1, Syntagma Square  
**Pin (WGS-84):** 37.976550, 23.735402  
**Length:** 338 words, about 2:15 spoken

---

In December nineteen forty four, there was enough dynamite in the sewers under this hotel to bring the whole thing down.

Syntagma Square. Face the big, pale building on the north side of the square, the one with the flags and the doormen. The Parliament is up the slope to your right. That's the Grande Bretagne, and it's been watching that building across the way for as long as Greece has had a capital here.

It began in eighteen forty two as a private mansion, built for a businessman from Lemnos called Antonis Dimitriou and designed by the Danish architect Theophil Hansen. It stood directly opposite the new royal palace, which is today's Parliament. In eighteen seventy four it became a hotel and took its name, and by eighteen eighty eight it had electric light.

Here's the twist. The building you're looking at is not that mansion. New wings came in nineteen thirty and nineteen fifty, and in nineteen fifty seven the original house was pulled down and rebuilt, with the architect Kostas Voutsinas keeping something of its style. What you see is a careful remembering of Hansen, not Hansen himself.

Then the war. In nineteen forty and forty one the hotel was the Greek general headquarters. Under the occupation, it was the German headquarters. When the Germans left, the British moved in. And on Christmas Day nineteen forty four, resistance fighters, among them the young Manolis Glezos, packed explosives into the sewers beneath it. The attack was called off when they learned who had just arrived: Winston Churchill.

*[beat]*

So when people call this the most famous hotel in Greece, it isn't really about the marble. It's that so much of the country's modern history happened, quite literally, across the road from the front door.

If you fancy it, cross the square towards the Parliament and the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. The guards in their pleated kilts change on the hour, and the view back to the hotel from there is the one Churchill would have known.
