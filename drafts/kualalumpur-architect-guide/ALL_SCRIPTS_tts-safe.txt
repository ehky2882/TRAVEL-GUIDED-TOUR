The Majestic Hotel

For fourteen years, the national art collection of Malaysia hung in a hotel.

Stand on the pavement of Jah lahn Sool tahn Hee shah moo din with the white domes of the old railway station behind you. Up the slope in front, set back behind its lawn, is a long, pale facade of arches and pilasters with a cornice running along the top. That's the original Majestic, opened in nineteen thirty two. The tall block rising beside it is new, and we'll come to that.

Have a look at the old building first. The architects were the firm Keys and Dowdz well, and they gave it a split personality: classical arches and columns for dignity, and the cleaner lines of Art Deco for modernity. It's the look of a colony that wanted to seem both ancient and up to date. When it opened it had fifty one rooms, modern plumbing that was something of a boast at the time, and a roof garden with a dance floor that could seat three hundred and fifty. Arriving by train, you simply crossed the road.

Its history is a fair summary of the country's. During the war the Japanese used it as a transit camp. In nineteen fifty one, the Independence of Malaya Party held its very first meeting here. By the seventies, the smart crowd had drifted to new hotels in Boo kit Bin tahng, and in nineteen eighty three the government gazetted the building as a historic landmark and closed the hotel. From nineteen eighty four it became the National Art Gallery, which is how a hotel ended up full of paintings.

And here's the thing. The building was saved not by sentiment but by a trade. In the nineties the developer Why Tee El was given the hotel in exchange for building the nation a new art gallery. It restored the old wing, added the fifteen storey tower beside it, and reopened the Majestic in December twenty twelve.

Before you leave, turn round and look at the railway station across the road. The two have faced each other since nineteen thirty two, one for arriving, one for staying. The footbridge over the Klahng River will take you on to Central Market in about ten minutes.



Hotel Stripes

When this hotel opened in February twenty seventeen, it handed out red bricks to its neighbours.

Jah lahn Kah moon ting, outside the hotel's front door. Look along the street in either direction and you'll see low rows of shophouses with covered walkways under their upper floors, the five foot ways that let you walk the length of a Malaysian street without getting rained on. Hotel Stripes is the tall newcomer among them.

This is one of the older corners of Kuala Lumpur. In the colonial era it was a tin mining settlement, and in the late nineteenth century the British administration had its wooden and thatched buildings replaced with brick and tile, because thatch and a growing town do not mix. What survives now is mostly mid century shophouses and family businesses that have been here for generations, Yoot Kee, the Hye nah neez coffee shop, and Ah Loke the tailor among them.

The site has a private story, too. The very first office of Why Tee El, the company that built the hotel, stood on this plot. So when it came to opening, the company threw a street party and gave nine of its neighbours, local business owners on Jah lahn Kah moon ting, a red brick each, as a token of thanks.

I rather like that gesture, because it says what the building is trying to do. Inside, the restaurant has red brick arcades, dark wood floors and copper chandeliers shaped like sputniks. Up on the roof there's a pool and a bar with a view across to the Kay El Tower. It's a contemporary hotel that has chosen, quite deliberately, to look as if it grew up on this street rather than landing on it.

From the front door, Yoot Kee is round the corner, and it's the obvious next stop, a Hye nah neez breakfast that has been served since nineteen twenty eight. If you'd rather keep walking, the shophouse rows of Jah lahn Kah moon ting reward a slow stroll; look up at the upper floor windows, where the street's real age shows.



The Roo mah Hotel and Residences

There's a birdcage at the front door of this hotel, and it's there because of the canaries.

You're on Jah lahn Kee ah Peng, in the leafy stretch of the Golden Triangle just south of the Petronas Towers. The Roo mah is the dark, quietly detailed building in front of you. Step up to the entrance and look into the foyer: you should see a large round cage of terracotta, with a bronze fountain inside it.

Roo mah comes from roo mah, the Malay word for home. The interiors were led by Andy Hall, a British designer based in Shanghai, and he began not with a style but with research into the two industries that built this city: tin mining and rubber.

The cage is the tin. In mines, miners once carried canaries in small metal cages, because the birds sensed dangerous gas before people did. Here that humble object has been blown up in terracotta and turned into a welcome. Inside, a double staircase appears to drill up through a ceiling tiled in copper, another nod to what came out of the ground. The rubber plantations, meanwhile, are in the bedrooms, which borrow the look of colonial planters' houses.

Look closely at the walls and you'll find keh lah rye, a traditional hand woven panel, and the checked cotton cloth called kine peh lee kaht worked into the furnishings. These are everyday Malaysian materials, the kind you'd find in a grandmother's house rather than a palace.

And that's the interesting bit. Luxury hotels usually tell you how far you've travelled. This one keeps trying to tell you where you've arrived, and the story it chooses is not the glamorous one. Kuala Lumpur grew out of muddy tin fields, and the most expensive room in the house is dressed in their memory.

When you leave, walk a few minutes south east to Jah lahn Stonor and find the Bah dahn Wah ree sahn heritage centre. In its garden stands Roo mah Peng hoo loo Ah boo Seh mahn, an old timber house from Keh dah, the real thing, raised on stilts, that a hotel like this one is quietly quoting.



Klo ee Hotel

The hotel in front of you opened in March twenty twenty, which, you may recall, was not a vintage month for hotels.

You're on Jah lahn Boo kit Bin tahng at number two hundred and twenty seven, the quieter eastern end of the street, away from its busiest stretch. Klo ee is the pair of new towers in front of you. Look at what they're made of: bare concrete for the frame, with clay brick, timber and metal laid against it. Nothing is painted to look like something else.

The name runs Kuala Lumpur's initials into Klo ee, from the Greek word for a young green shoot, and that little sprout appears in the hotel's logo. It was dreamed up by Eng Ping Hoh, a film director and producer, and designed from the ground up by the local practice Domaine Architects. It's small for a city hotel, eighty five rooms, and that's the point.

Here's what I find most interesting. Most hotels hire one designer and let them furnish every corner. Klo ee went the other way and filled itself with the work of the city's own makers: furniture, ceramics and artworks by local designers and craftspeople, alongside mid century Scandinavian pieces. Five of the suites, called the Lofts, were conceived with local tastemakers, each around a creative pursuit. One, a room for cooks and eaters, was put together by the chef and broadcaster Sarah Benjamin.

So the building works as a kind of catalogue. Stay a night and you've been introduced to a generation of Malaysian designers without visiting a single gallery.

From here, walk west along Jah lahn Boo kit Bin tahng and the street gets steadily louder, brighter and more crowded until you reach the shopping malls and the hawker stalls beyond. It's worth doing once, if only to see how quickly the city changes its mind about what it wants to be.



Day wah kahn

The most decorated restaurant in Malaysia began life as a university project.

Look up. You should be at the foot of Naza Tower on Per see ah rahn Kay El See See, a few minutes' walk from the Petronas Towers. Day wah kahn is on the forty eighth floor, so from the pavement there are forty seven storeys of glass between you and dinner.

It opened in twenty fifteen inside Kay Dee You University College in Shah Ah lahm, as a teaching kitchen with ambitions. The chef, Darren Tay oh, moved it to this tower a few years later, and the stars followed. It now holds two Michelin stars, and it was the first restaurant in Malaysia to be given a Michelin Green Star, the award for sustainability.

The name is the clue to the cooking. It runs two Malay words together: day wah, meaning god, and mah kahn, to eat. And what Tay oh's kitchen eats is almost entirely Malaysian. Not dishes, so much as ingredients, things gathered from farms, jungles, hills and the sea. Kelulut honey, made by tiny stingless bees. A wild citrus from Joh hor called lee mow hahn too, the ghost lime. Boo ah keh loo ak, the black seed that is poisonous until it has been properly prepared.

The gift detail is a wild yam called keh mee lee. The team spent about seven years learning to cook it, and in the end borrowed a technique from Latin America, treating it with alkali the way corn is treated for tortillas, to turn it into a kind of dough.

That's the argument this restaurant makes. Malaysian food is usually celebrated for its famous dishes. Day wah kahn suggests the larder is the real treasure, and that most of it has never been on a menu at all.

If you've eaten here, or are simply passing, walk a few minutes west to Kay El See See Park and look back at this tower from the lake. Then try to work out which window is the forty eighth floor, it's harder than it looks.



Yoot Kee

The menu in here has barely changed since nineteen twenty eight.

You're at number one, Jah lahn Kah moon ting, outside a three storey building that used to be a house. If it's a weekend morning, the queue on the pavement will tell you you're in the right place. Inside, the walls are covered with faded newspaper and magazine clippings, and the room has the plain, bright look of the old Malaysian coffee shops, the koh pee tee ahms, of a generation or two ago.

Yoot Kee was opened in January nineteen twenty eight by a cook from Hye nahn Island, in southern China, who had worked as a private chef for the household of Choo Kee ah Peng, one of the leading Chinese figures in colonial Kuala Lumpur. After he died in nineteen forty seven, his three wives kept the business going. His grandson, Mervyn Lee, runs it now and says he has seen fifth generation customers.

For eighty six years it traded from a building on Jah lahn Dahng Wahng ee, round the corner. In twenty fourteen it moved here, and the regulars simply followed.

Now, the food. The famous dish is roh tee bah bee: bread stuffed with shredded pork, onion and crab, then fried, and eaten with Worcestershire sauce, which comes to the table as a matter of course. There's a Hye nah neez chicken chop with gravy, and kah yah, the coconut jam, made the slow way in a double boiler and spread on toast.

And here's the thing about that bottle of Worcestershire. Hye nah neez cooks often worked in British kitchens, and they took the colonial menu home and made it their own. A chicken chop with gravy is not an English dish and not a Chinese one. It's a Kuala Lumpur dish, and this is one of the last places that still cooks it as if nothing had happened since.

Before you go, order a slice of the marble cake to take with you; it's baked in house and it's the one the regulars buy. Then walk the few minutes round the corner to Jah lahn Dahng Wahng ee and picture the old shop, where all this started.



Res toh rahn Sek Yoo en

In the kitchen behind this facade, they still cook over wood fires.

Jah lahn Poo doo, number three hundred and thirteen. Look for the standalone building with steps leading up to its entrance, a little apart from the shophouse row. Next door, at three hundred and fifteen, is its younger sibling: an extension added in the seventies, said to be one of the first air conditioned restaurants in the city.

The story starts on the street. In the early nineteen forties, three brothers of the Fahng family sold won ton noodles from a hawker cart. When the war came they fled, and when it ended they came back, which not every hawker did. In nineteen forty eight they opened a proper restaurant, and later in the fifties put up the building you're looking at.

Go in, and look up first. The ceilings are high on purpose, to let the heat rise in a room that was built before air conditioning. Then look around: the old fans, tables and chairs, and photographs and newspaper cuttings of the restaurant's own past on the walls. In the sixties this was a fashionable place for wedding banquets.

The gift detail is in the kitchen. Sek Yoo en still cooks on wood fired stoves and a wood fired steamer, which almost nobody in the city bothers with any more, because wood is slow, dirty and hard to control. It's held to be the key to the traditional flavours the restaurant has always served.

Try the pee pah duck: split open and pressed flat until it looks like the Chinese lute it's named after, then roasted and served with lemon sauce.

What you're tasting is not nostalgia, exactly. It's what Cantonese restaurant cooking in this city tasted like before electricity made everything faster.

Before you leave, step back to the far side of the pavement and look at how the building stands apart from its neighbours, steps and all. Then come back for dinner if you can, when the place is at its busiest.



Bee jahn Bar and Restaurant

Twenty odd years ago, you could struggle to find a proper Malay restaurant in the middle of Kuala Lumpur.

Number three, Jah lahn Ceylon. You're on a short, sloping street between Boo kit Bin tahng and the city centre, and Bee jahn is the low colonial era building in front of you, with its terrace and bar opening onto the street.

Bee jahn is the Malay word for sesame, and the restaurant was the idea of three friends, Yoo en See Toh, Lissa Yoh and Way Cheng Yoh, who began researching it in two thousand and two. Their problem was simple. Malay food was everywhere in Malaysia, in homes, at stalls and at weddings, but hardly anyone served it in a restaurant setting, with the care you'd expect for French or Japanese cooking. In two thousand and four, Tourism Malaysia named it the country's best Malay restaurant.

The cooking leans on recipes handed down in the owners' own families, then sharpened with a few restaurant habits, cuts of beef, such as ribs, that you wouldn't normally find in a Malay dish, and plating that takes its time. Look out for the ren dahng, the slow cooked spiced beef, and the dishes cooked in mah sak leh mak, a rich turmeric and coconut gravy.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that this restaurant helped change what people thought Malay food was for. Not just something you ate at home, but something you could take a guest to. Plenty of modern Malay restaurants have followed since.

From here, walk up the hill to Chahng kaht Boo kit Bin tahng, a short street of old shophouses turned into bars and restaurants, where the city goes to have dinner and keep going.



Nah see Leh mak Tang lin

If you arrive after ten o'clock, the chicken may already be gone.

Jah lahn Chen deh rah sah ree, on the edge of the government quarter, a short walk from the National Mosque. You're looking for the Tang lin food centre, a covered hawker hall, and inside it, stall number six. If there's a queue, you've found it.

The stall goes back to nineteen forty eight, when a cook called Soor yah tee Jah wee roo nah began selling nah see leh mak, rice cooked in coconut milk, from a small wooden shack on this very spot, long before the hawker hall was built round it. Her son, Zye nahl Ah bee din Hah sahn, took over and made the sahm bahl himself every morning. Her granddaughter runs it now. Three generations, one stall.

Nah see leh mak is the closest thing Malaysia has to a national breakfast, and the plate is built the same way everywhere: coconut rice, crisp fried anchovies, peanuts, cucumber and a spoonful of chilli sahm bahl. The gift detail is that sahm bahl. Suryati cooked hers slowly for three to four hours, and the family still does. It comes out dark red and caramel sweet, with a heat that arrives a moment later.

Then come the extras, which is where people get competitive: fried chicken, cockles in sahm bahl, squid, beef in a dark sauce. They go fast. The stall opens at seven in the morning and is usually finished by around noon.

Here's what I love about this place. It sits a short walk from the police headquarters at Boo kit Ah mahn and from the National Mosque, so on any morning the queue is a mix of office workers, officers in uniform and families after prayers, all waiting at the same counter. It's one of the more democratic institutions in the city.

When you've eaten, walk down the hill to Mahsjid Neh gah rah, the National Mosque, and look up at its folded concrete roof. If you've time, the Per dah nah Botanical Garden is just beyond, and a good place to walk off breakfast.



Pee Ess one fifty

The way in is through a toy shop.

Number one hundred and fifty, Jah lahn Peh tah ling, at the southern end of Chinatown. That's where the name comes from: Peh tah ling Street, one fifty. Look for a small, brightly lit shopfront full of old toys and retro figures. The bar's name is on a modest sign on the wall, and the entrance is a door at the back of the shop.

The building is a pre war shophouse with a past. In the nineteen eighties it was a brothel, and later a hostel for migrant workers, before it was left empty. When the bar moved in, the owners kept as much of the decay as they could: plaster peeling in its own good time, bare brick, tree roots pushing through the walls.

Once you're through, the bar unfolds in three rooms, and each one is a different period. First, the Opium Den, small and close, with beaded curtains. Then an open air courtyard. Then the main bar, with a red neon sign that reads Chee Chung Kye, the old Cantonese name for Peh tah ling Street.

The menu follows the same idea. It's arranged by era, vintage, classic, tiki, disco and contemporary, so you can drink your way from the nineteenth century to the present, and the flavours are local: pahn dahn, lychee, chilli.

What's interesting is the timing. Pee Ess one fifty opened in twenty sixteen, when this end of Chinatown was mostly closed shutters after dark, and in its first year it was the only bar from Malaysia on the list of Asia's Fifty Best Bars. A good deal of the neighbourhood's revival since has followed in its footsteps.

The upstairs cafe, Merchant's Lane, ran in the same building for nearly ten years and closed at the start of twenty twenty six. From here, Kwhy Chye Hong is a couple of minutes' walk away, off Loh rong Pahng goong: a little restored back lane with murals of nineteen sixties street life, and the best place in Chinatown to see what these shophouses looked like from behind.



Vee See Ar

According to the people who run it, this little house was once home to prison warders.

Jah lahn Galloway, on the slope between Chinatown and Boo kit Bin tahng. Vee See Ar is the two storey house in front of you, painted black. You'll know it by the people at the outside tables, almost all of them with a coffee.

The building dates from nineteen twenty seven. It stands near the site of the old Poo doo Jail, the great walled prison that has since been demolished, and the cafe's own story is that this street was where many of the warders lived. That's their account rather than a historian's, so take it in the spirit offered. What isn't in doubt is that the house had been through a good deal before twenty thirteen, when Andrew Lee and his team moved in and turned it into a cafe.

Go inside and look at what they kept. The space is high, light and plain, with the old proportions left alone. Much of its original character, the bones of a pre war house, has simply been allowed to stay, while the coffee bar and kitchen were slotted in around it.

The coffee itself is the point. Vee See Ar belongs to the generation of cafes that gave Kuala Lumpur a specialty coffee scene, at a time when the default order in this city was still a sweet, strong koh pee from a coffee shop.

What's quietly radical here is the choice of building. A decade or so ago, an old house like this was more likely to be knocked down than restored. Vee See Ar made the argument that a pre war house, kept largely as it was, is exactly what people want to sit in, and these days the city is full of cafes making the same argument.

If you're in the mood for something stronger later, Pah hit, a gin bar in a nineteen twenties shophouse, is barely a minute away on Jah lahn Sin Chew Kee. Take a stroll round the back streets first, though; this quiet pocket between two busy districts is easy to miss.



Lokal Coffee Company

Only a few years before this cafe opened, much of this street was abandoned shophouses with rotting shutters.

You're on Jah lahn Toon Aitch Ess Lee, number thirty, in the oldest part of the city, a short walk from Meh dahn Pah sar, the old market square. Lokal is the cafe at street level. Look up at the facades along this row: these are colonial era shophouses, two storeys or three, with tall shuttered windows on the upper floors.

Lokal opened in twenty twelve, founded by the husband and wife team of Eng Ping Hoh, a filmmaker, and Cheryl Sah mahd, an actress. It shares its building with a hostel called Back Home, which restored four of these old shophouses into rooms, dormitories and a courtyard. The cafe is the hostel's kitchen, but it was always meant as a neighbourhood place too, somewhere for the office workers round here to sit down.

Go in and look at the walls and ceiling. The original brickwork has been stripped back and whitewashed rather than plastered over, and the old timber beams overhead have been given the same treatment, with a new timber floor below. It's restoration done lightly: clean up what's there, add as little as possible.

What I find interesting is the pairing. A cafe gives a heritage building a reason to have its doors open all day, and a hostel brings in people who would never otherwise sleep in the oldest part of town. Together they did more for this street than a preservation order ever could. The same founder went on to create Klo ee, the design hotel on Boo kit Bin tahng.

When you leave, walk the short distance to Meh dahn Pah sar, the square where Kuala Lumpur's first market stood. Look for the little Art Deco clock tower in the middle, then carry on down Jah lahn Toon Aitch Ess Lee to the Sin See See Yah temple, the oldest Taoist temple in the city.



Hoh Cow Hye nahm Kopitiam

For more than sixty years this coffee shop lived in a back lane. Then it was evicted, and moved about a minute's walk away.

Jah lahn Bah lye Poh liss, in Chinatown, on the main street rather than the lane behind it. Hoh Cow is in the shophouse in front of you; on most mornings, the queue is the giveaway. The lane behind is Loh rong Pahng goong, better known as Kwhy Chye Hong, and that is where this story starts.

In nineteen fifty six a Hye nah neez man called Hoh Kee Soon opened a small coffee shop there, selling tea, soft boiled eggs and toast. Hye nah neez families ran a great many of Malaysia's old coffee shops, the koh pee tee ahms, and the formula hardly varied: strong coffee, eggs barely cooked, and toast spread with kah yah, the sweet coconut jam. His son, Hoh Cow, gave the shop its name.

In twenty eighteen the family received an eviction notice. Rather than close, they moved round the corner, to the larger shophouse you're looking at now. Hoh Cow's daughter, Hoh Sook Chahn, runs it today with her own children, which makes this the third and fourth generations.

Go in and order the classics. The coffee is koh pee, roasted dark and served with condensed milk unless you say otherwise. The eggs come runny, in a little bowl, and the trick is a dash of soy sauce and white pepper. The toast is thick, with kah yah and a slab of butter.

Here's what I find touching about it. The move should have been the end. Instead the new shop became a destination in its own right, and the old lane it left behind has since been restored and opened to the public. Both sides of the story survived.

When you've finished, step round the back to Kwhy Chye Hong itself. Look for the murals of nineteen sixties street life on the walls, and imagine a coffee shop tucked into the lane, where this one began.



Pah hit

In colonial Malaya, the sundowner of choice was gin with bitters, and the word for bitter was pah hit.

Jah lahn Sin Chew Kee is a short, quiet street of old shophouses, tucked between Chinatown and Boo kit Bin tahng. Pah hit is at number three, in a shophouse from the nineteen twenties. If you're facing it, you're in the right place; if you're lost, you're probably one street over, which is easily done round here.

The name is the joke and the history at once. Planters and officials in colonial Malaya would call for a gin pah hit, gin with a dash of bitters, as the sun went down. The phrase outlived the empire that coined it, and a Malaysian bar has now borrowed it back.

The bar was set up by See Kay Koh, who also runs Coley in Bahng sar, one of the city's better known cocktail bars. The back bar is lined with gins, dozens of them, from all over the world. The house speciality is the gin and tonic, and here it's done with fruit infused into the gin by slow, gentle cooking. Look out for local flavours: jackfruit, pomelo, and ah sahm boy, the salty preserved plum that Malaysians have been dropping into drinks for generations.

What I like about Pah hit is that it takes the most colonial drink there is and makes it unmistakably local. Gin is the British part. The jackfruit and the preserved plum are not. It's a small, cheerful act of reclamation, and it happens to taste very good.

When you leave, Vee See Ar, the cafe in a nineteen twenty seven house, is a minute away on Jah lahn Galloway, if you need a coffee to balance things out. And if you walk the length of Jah lahn Sin Chew Kee, look up at the upper floors as you go; the shopfronts have changed many times, but the upper storeys are where these buildings keep their age.



Bar Trig oh nah

This bar is named after a bee with no sting.

You're at the Four Seasons Hotel on Per see ah rahn Petronas, right beside the Petronas Towers. Bar Trig oh nah is inside the hotel. If you're standing in the forecourt, the towers will be filling most of the sky above you.

Trig oh nah is the name of a family of tiny stingless bees found across Malaysia. They build their hives in hollow places, including coconut trees, and their honey, known in Malay as mah doo keh loo loot, is thinner and sharper than ordinary honey, with a sourness that surprises people. It sits at the heart of the bar's drinks.

The bar has been a regular on the list of Asia's Fifty Best Bars, appearing for six years running by twenty twenty four, and in twenty twenty two it won the award for the best cocktail menu on that list. It has also picked up awards for sustainability, more than once.

That's the real story here. The team works on what they call a root to shoot approach, using as much of each ingredient as they can rather than throwing away the parts that don't look pretty. Their motto is reuse, recycle, repurpose. They have gone further than most: guests have been able to adopt a beehive on a keh loo loot farm in Neh gree Sem bee lahn, and part of what you pay for a drink goes towards restoring habitats for local bees.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how unusual this is. Hotel bars exist to make guests feel they could be anywhere comfortable in the world. Bar Trig oh nah does the opposite: it builds its menu from Malaysian farms and Malaysian forests, and quietly argues that the most luxurious thing in a glass might be something grown down the road.

When you leave, step out into Kay El See See Park, the gardens at the foot of the towers. After dark the lake has a fountain show, set to music, and the towers look best from the far side of the water.



Heli Lounge Bar

By day, helicopters land on this roof. By night, you can order a drink on it.

Jah lahn Sool tahn Iss my eel, outside Meh nah rah Kay Aitch, a little way from the Rah jah Choo lahn monorail station. Look up at the tower; the flat top is a helipad. To reach it, take the lift to the thirty fourth floor, pay at the bar inside, the entry price includes drinks, and then climb one last staircase out into the open air.

Up there, there's no glass between you and the city. The platform is a working helipad, which means it was built to keep the approach clear, not to frame a view, and the effect is startling. Kuala Lumpur spreads out on every side, and the only thing above you is the sky.

Look north first, and you'll find the Petronas Towers, close enough that you can see the bridge between them. Then turn to the west and look for the Kay El Tower on its hill, with its pod near the top. From up here, you can see that the tower stands on high ground, Boo kit Nah nahs, Pineapple Hill, which is why it can look taller than the Petronas Towers from certain angles, even though it isn't.

What I love about this place is that it's honest. Most rooftop bars in this city are glass boxes, air conditioned and sealed off. This is the roof of an office block, still doing its day job, with a bar wheeled on at sunset. It's warm, it can be windy, and it shows you the real skyline without any reflections in the way.

Practicalities matter here. The bar opens only in the evening, closes on Sundays and when the weather turns, and it's worth checking before you set off. If you're up there at dusk, wait for the moment the city lights come on below you, and then look back towards Boo kit Nah nahs, where the forest is still dark.



Bah ngoo nahn Jong shahn

There were offers to knock this building down, and one to turn it into a karaoke bar of a less respectable kind.

Jah lahn Roh tahn, in Kahm poong Ah tahp, a quiet district just south of Chinatown. The Jong shahn Building is the row in front of you: three four storey shophouses from the nineteen fifties, joined together, with a plain, mid century front. You should be able to see small shop signs at street level and windows stacked above.

The name comes from the Seh lahng or Jong shahn Association, a Chinese clan association that once had its rooms here. Over the decades, the building housed families, small merchants and a frozen food business, and later became a hostel for foreign workers. It belongs to the family of Rob Tan, whose grandparents bought up the units one by one.

When the family considered selling, the offers were not encouraging: buyers wanted to demolish it, or to convert it into a karaoke bar with rooms upstairs. Instead, Rob Tan and his wife, Liza Hoh, chose to restore it. A grant from the heritage organisation Think City covered about a fifth of the cost, and in twenty seventeen the building reopened as a home for independent creative businesses.

Go in and wander. When it opened, the tenants included a record shop, Tahn dahng Records. There's the Malaysia Design Archive, which collects the country's graphic design history, posters, packaging and the rest. There's a library, studios, a bakery and small galleries. Around two dozen small businesses share the space, though the mix changes over time.

What's worth knowing is how the place is run. The owners keep the rents low and choose tenants who fit, rather than the highest bidder. That's the whole trick: a building can only stay interesting if the people who make it interesting can afford to stay.

When you leave, wander the streets of Kahm poong Ah tahp, one of the city's less polished corners. Then look up and north east; the vast spire of Mer day kah one one eight is close enough to dominate the view, and Stadium Mer day kah, where independence was declared, lies at its foot.



Rex Kay El

Inside this building, the bookshelves step down towards where the cinema screen used to be.

Jah lahn Sool tahn, on the edge of Chinatown. Rex Kay El is the old cinema building in front of you; its name has kept the Rex of the cinema it used to be. Stand back on the far pavement and look at the frontage. You're looking at a survivor of several fires.

Then look at the shape of the whole. It's often said to recall a drum, the kind traditionally beaten to call pilgrims together for the journey.

And here's the thing. Most office towers in the eighties were trying to look like they belonged in New York. This one was trying to look like it belonged to its purpose. You can argue about whether symbolism makes good architecture, but there's no mistaking what this building is for.

Before you go, walk round to the other side for a clearer view of the columns against the tower. And keep this building in mind: Hee jahs Kahs too ree also designed the keh riss shaped Meh nah rah Toon Peh rak, the old Maybank tower. Seeing both is the quickest lesson in his thinking.



Meh nah rah Toon Peh rak formerly Meh nah rah Maybank

This tower is a dagger, still in its sheath.

You're on Jah lahn Toon Peh rak, at the foot of the tall white tower that until July twenty twenty six was called Meh nah rah Maybank. It's now Meh nah rah Toon Peh rak. Look up and find the shape: two square blocks, locked together at one corner, rising to a sharp, angled top.

It was designed by Hee jahs Kahs too ree Associates and built between nineteen eighty four and nineteen eighty eight for Maybank, one of the country's largest banks. It's around two hundred and forty four metres tall, with fifty floors, and for a time it was among the tallest buildings in the city.

The gift detail is the form. It's based on the keh riss, the wavy bladed Malay dagger, shown pointing upwards and partly drawn from its sheath. Kom pleks Dye ah boo mee

Before the Petronas Towers, Petronas lived here.

Look at the white tower beside the river, between Central Market and the old railway station. It's wrapped in a lattice of pointed arches, layer upon layer, so the whole building looks as if it's wearing a carved screen.

Have a look at the arches. In Islamic architecture, a screen of patterned openings lets air and light through while keeping out the sun's glare. Here that old idea is applied to a modern office tower. It's ornament, but it's also shade.

Bah ngoo nahn Par lee men Malaysia Parliament House

The tower of Malaysia's parliament is wrapped in a concrete grille, and that grille is doing two jobs at once.

It was designed by William Ivor Shipley, a British architect in the Public Works Department, and opened on the second of November, nineteen sixty three, just weeks after Malaysia was formed. That double job, climate and structure, is the gift detail. In the tropics, a sealed glass box overheats. A building that shades itself is simply sensible. Here the shading becomes the whole character of the building.

What's worth knowing is what this building represents. It went up in the years between independence and the formation of Malaysia, when the country's new institutions needed new buildings. Stadium Mer day kah

On the thirty first of August, nineteen fifty seven, a man stood in this stadium and shouted the same word seven times.

You're at Stadium Mer day kah, in the old city, on the hill south of Chinatown. In front of you is a low, open stadium, with cantilevered roofs over its stands and tall floodlight towers at its corners. And right next to it, filling the sky, is Mer day kah one one eight.

The stadium was built in less than a year, between September nineteen fifty six and August nineteen fifty seven, for one purpose: the declaration of independence of the Federation of Malaya. It was designed by Stanley Jooks, director of the Public Works Department, and when it was finished it held world records for its prestressed floodlight towers and its cantilevered shell roofs.

Then, on the morning of the thirty first of August, nineteen fifty seven, in front of more than twenty thousand people, the first prime minister, Toon koo Ab dool Rah mahn, read the proclamation and shouted Mer day kah, freedom, seven times.

The stadium had a lively life afterwards: the proclamation of Malaysia in nineteen sixty three, Muhammad Ali boxing Joe Bugner in nineteen seventy five, and Michael Jackson in nineteen ninety six. But by the nineties it was threatened with demolition. It survived thanks to a public outcry and the Asian financial crisis, which killed off the redevelopment. It was then restored to its nineteen fifty seven appearance, work that won a Yoo nes koh heritage conservation award in two thousand and eight and was completed the following year.

And here's the thing about the tower beside it. Mer day kah one one eight, now officially Meh nah rah Mer day kah Maybank, is the second tallest building in the world, and it was built on the stadium's doorstep. You can read that as a celebration of independence, or as the modern city crowding its own birthplace. Standing here, it's hard not to feel both.

Before you leave, if the gates are open, find a spot in the stands facing the field, and picture the crowd in nineteen fifty seven. Then look up at the tower. Few places put a country's past and present quite so close together.



Kay El Forest Eco Park

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how unusual this is. Most cities keep their parks, but few keep a piece of the original forest they were built in. This is what Kuala Lumpur was before it was Kuala Lumpur, and it's still here, in the middle of the skyline.

You're in Kay El See See Park, at the foot of the Petronas Towers. Find a spot near the lake, where you can see both the water and the towers above you.
