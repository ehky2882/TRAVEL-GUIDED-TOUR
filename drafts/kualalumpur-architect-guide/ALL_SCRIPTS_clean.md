# Kuala Lumpur — an architect's guide · all 42 scripts (clean)

## 01 · The Majestic Hotel

*STAY · The Majestic Hotel Kuala Lumpur, Autograph Collection · 3.138563, 101.692064*

For fourteen years, the national art collection of Malaysia hung in a hotel.

Stand on the pavement of Jalan Sultan Hishamuddin with the white domes of the old railway station behind you. Up the slope in front, set back behind its lawn, is a long, pale facade of arches and pilasters with a cornice running along the top. That's the original Majestic, opened in 1932. The tall block rising beside it is new, and we'll come to that.

Have a look at the old building first. The architects were the firm Keys and Dowdeswell, and they gave it a split personality: classical arches and columns for dignity, and the cleaner lines of Art Deco for modernity. It's the look of a colony that wanted to seem both ancient and up to date. When it opened it had fifty-one rooms, modern plumbing that was something of a boast at the time, and a roof garden with a dance floor that could seat three hundred and fifty. Arriving by train, you simply crossed the road.

Its history is a fair summary of the country's. During the war the Japanese used it as a transit camp. In 1951, the Independence of Malaya Party held its very first meeting here. By the seventies, the smart crowd had drifted to new hotels in Bukit Bintang, and in 1983 the government gazetted the building as a historic landmark and closed the hotel. From 1984 it became the National Art Gallery, which is how a hotel ended up full of paintings.

And here's the thing. The building was saved not by sentiment but by a trade. In the nineties the developer YTL was given the hotel in exchange for building the nation a new art gallery. It restored the old wing, added the fifteen-storey tower beside it, and reopened the Majestic in December 2012.

[beat]

Before you leave, turn round and look at the railway station across the road. The two have faced each other since 1932 — one for arriving, one for staying. The footbridge over the Klang River will take you on to Central Market in about ten minutes.

## 02 · Hotel Stripes

*STAY · Hotel Stripes Kuala Lumpur, Autograph Collection · 3.157242, 101.700546*

When this hotel opened in February 2017, it handed out red bricks to its neighbours.

Jalan Kamunting, outside the hotel's front door. Look along the street in either direction and you'll see low rows of shophouses with covered walkways under their upper floors — the five-foot ways that let you walk the length of a Malaysian street without getting rained on. Hotel Stripes is the tall newcomer among them.

This is one of the older corners of Kuala Lumpur. In the colonial era it was a tin-mining settlement, and in the late nineteenth century the British administration had its wooden and thatched buildings replaced with brick and tile, because thatch and a growing town do not mix. What survives now is mostly mid-century shophouses and family businesses that have been here for generations — Yut Kee, the Hainanese coffee shop, and Ah Loke the tailor among them.

The site has a private story, too. The very first office of YTL, the company that built the hotel, stood on this plot. So when it came to opening, the company threw a street party and gave nine of its neighbours — local business owners on Jalan Kamunting — a red brick each, as a token of thanks.

I rather like that gesture, because it says what the building is trying to do. Inside, the restaurant has red-brick arcades, dark wood floors and copper chandeliers shaped like sputniks. Up on the roof there's a pool and a bar with a view across to the KL Tower. It's a contemporary hotel that has chosen, quite deliberately, to look as if it grew up on this street rather than landing on it.

[beat]

From the front door, Yut Kee is round the corner, and it's the obvious next stop — a Hainanese breakfast that has been served since 1928. If you'd rather keep walking, the shophouse rows of Jalan Kamunting reward a slow stroll; look up at the upper-floor windows, where the street's real age shows.

## 03 · The RuMa Hotel and Residences

*STAY · The RuMa Hotel and Residences · 3.152416, 101.714374*

There's a birdcage at the front door of this hotel, and it's there because of the canaries.

You're on Jalan Kia Peng, in the leafy stretch of the Golden Triangle just south of the Petronas Towers. The RuMa is the dark, quietly detailed building in front of you. Step up to the entrance and look into the foyer: you should see a large round cage of terracotta, with a bronze fountain inside it.

RuMa comes from rumah, the Malay word for home. The interiors were led by Andy Hall, a British designer based in Shanghai, and he began not with a style but with research into the two industries that built this city: tin mining and rubber.

The cage is the tin. In mines, miners once carried canaries in small metal cages, because the birds sensed dangerous gas before people did. Here that humble object has been blown up in terracotta and turned into a welcome. Inside, a double staircase appears to drill up through a ceiling tiled in copper, another nod to what came out of the ground. The rubber plantations, meanwhile, are in the bedrooms, which borrow the look of colonial planters' houses.

Look closely at the walls and you'll find kelarai, a traditional hand-woven panel, and the checked cotton cloth called kain pelikat worked into the furnishings. These are everyday Malaysian materials, the kind you'd find in a grandmother's house rather than a palace.

And that's the interesting bit. Luxury hotels usually tell you how far you've travelled. This one keeps trying to tell you where you've arrived, and the story it chooses is not the glamorous one. Kuala Lumpur grew out of muddy tin fields, and the most expensive room in the house is dressed in their memory.

[beat]

When you leave, walk a few minutes south-east to Jalan Stonor and find the Badan Warisan heritage centre. In its garden stands Rumah Penghulu Abu Seman, an old timber house from Kedah — the real thing, raised on stilts, that a hotel like this one is quietly quoting.

## 04 · KLoé Hotel

*STAY · KLoé Hotel · 3.146949, 101.717741*

The hotel in front of you opened in March 2020, which, you may recall, was not a vintage month for hotels.

You're on Jalan Bukit Bintang at number two hundred and twenty-seven, the quieter eastern end of the street, away from its busiest stretch. KLoé is the pair of new towers in front of you. Look at what they're made of: bare concrete for the frame, with clay brick, timber and metal laid against it. Nothing is painted to look like something else.

The name runs Kuala Lumpur's initials into Chloe, from the Greek word for a young green shoot, and that little sprout appears in the hotel's logo. It was dreamed up by Ng Ping Ho, a film director and producer, and designed from the ground up by the local practice Domaine Architects. It's small for a city hotel — eighty-five rooms — and that's the point.

Here's what I find most interesting. Most hotels hire one designer and let them furnish every corner. KLoé went the other way and filled itself with the work of the city's own makers: furniture, ceramics and artworks by local designers and craftspeople, alongside mid-century Scandinavian pieces. Five of the suites, called the Lofts, were conceived with local tastemakers, each around a creative pursuit. One, a room for cooks and eaters, was put together by the chef and broadcaster Sarah Benjamin.

So the building works as a kind of catalogue. Stay a night and you've been introduced to a generation of Malaysian designers without visiting a single gallery.

[beat]

From here, walk west along Jalan Bukit Bintang and the street gets steadily louder, brighter and more crowded until you reach the shopping malls and the hawker stalls beyond. It's worth doing once, if only to see how quickly the city changes its mind about what it wants to be.

## 05 · Dewakan

*DINE · Dewakan · 3.154719, 101.718505*

The most decorated restaurant in Malaysia began life as a university project.

Look up. You should be at the foot of Naza Tower on Persiaran KLCC, a few minutes' walk from the Petronas Towers. Dewakan is on the forty-eighth floor, so from the pavement there are forty-seven storeys of glass between you and dinner.

It opened in 2015 inside KDU University College in Shah Alam, as a teaching kitchen with ambitions. The chef, Darren Teoh, moved it to this tower a few years later, and the stars followed. It now holds two Michelin stars, and it was the first restaurant in Malaysia to be given a Michelin Green Star, the award for sustainability.

The name is the clue to the cooking. It runs two Malay words together: dewa, meaning god, and makan, to eat. And what Teoh's kitchen eats is almost entirely Malaysian. Not dishes, so much as ingredients — things gathered from farms, jungles, hills and the sea. Kelulut honey, made by tiny stingless bees. A wild citrus from Johor called limau hantu, the ghost lime. Buah keluak, the black seed that is poisonous until it has been properly prepared.

The gift detail is a wild yam called kemili. The team spent about seven years learning to cook it, and in the end borrowed a technique from Latin America, treating it with alkali the way corn is treated for tortillas, to turn it into a kind of dough.

[beat]

That's the argument this restaurant makes. Malaysian food is usually celebrated for its famous dishes. Dewakan suggests the larder is the real treasure, and that most of it has never been on a menu at all.

If you've eaten here, or are simply passing, walk a few minutes west to KLCC Park and look back at this tower from the lake. Then try to work out which window is the forty-eighth floor — it's harder than it looks.

## 06 · Yut Kee

*DINE · Yut Kee · 3.156483, 101.700302*

The menu in here has barely changed since 1928.

You're at number one, Jalan Kamunting, outside a three-storey building that used to be a house. If it's a weekend morning, the queue on the pavement will tell you you're in the right place. Inside, the walls are covered with faded newspaper and magazine clippings, and the room has the plain, bright look of the old Malaysian coffee shops — the kopitiams — of a generation or two ago.

Yut Kee was opened in January 1928 by a cook from Hainan Island, in southern China, who had worked as a private chef for the household of Choo Kia Peng, one of the leading Chinese figures in colonial Kuala Lumpur. After he died in 1947, his three wives kept the business going. His grandson, Mervyn Lee, runs it now and says he has seen fifth-generation customers.

For eighty-six years it traded from a building on Jalan Dang Wangi, round the corner. In 2014 it moved here, and the regulars simply followed.

Now, the food. The famous dish is roti babi: bread stuffed with shredded pork, onion and crab, then fried, and eaten with Worcestershire sauce, which comes to the table as a matter of course. There's a Hainanese chicken chop with gravy, and kaya, the coconut jam, made the slow way in a double boiler and spread on toast.

And here's the thing about that bottle of Worcestershire. Hainanese cooks often worked in British kitchens, and they took the colonial menu home and made it their own. A chicken chop with gravy is not an English dish and not a Chinese one. It's a Kuala Lumpur dish, and this is one of the last places that still cooks it as if nothing had happened since.

[beat]

Before you go, order a slice of the marble cake to take with you; it's baked in-house and it's the one the regulars buy. Then walk the few minutes round the corner to Jalan Dang Wangi and picture the old shop, where all this started.

## 07 · Restoran Sek Yuen

*DINE · Restoran Sek Yuen · 3.135594, 101.712161*

In the kitchen behind this facade, they still cook over wood fires.

Jalan Pudu, number three hundred and thirteen. Look for the standalone building with steps leading up to its entrance, a little apart from the shophouse row. Next door, at three hundred and fifteen, is its younger sibling: an extension added in the seventies, said to be one of the first air-conditioned restaurants in the city.

The story starts on the street. In the early 1940s, three brothers of the Phang family sold wantan noodles from a hawker cart. When the war came they fled, and when it ended they came back, which not every hawker did. In 1948 they opened a proper restaurant, and later in the fifties put up the building you're looking at.

Go in, and look up first. The ceilings are high on purpose, to let the heat rise in a room that was built before air conditioning. Then look around: the old fans, tables and chairs, and photographs and newspaper cuttings of the restaurant's own past on the walls. In the sixties this was a fashionable place for wedding banquets.

The gift detail is in the kitchen. Sek Yuen still cooks on wood-fired stoves and a wood-fired steamer, which almost nobody in the city bothers with any more, because wood is slow, dirty and hard to control. It's held to be the key to the traditional flavours the restaurant has always served.

Try the pipa duck: split open and pressed flat until it looks like the Chinese lute it's named after, then roasted and served with lemon sauce.

[beat]

What you're tasting is not nostalgia, exactly. It's what Cantonese restaurant cooking in this city tasted like before electricity made everything faster.

Before you leave, step back to the far side of the pavement and look at how the building stands apart from its neighbours, steps and all. Then come back for dinner if you can, when the place is at its busiest.

## 08 · Bijan Bar and Restaurant

*DINE · Bijan Bar and Restaurant · 3.148392, 101.705971*

Twenty-odd years ago, you could struggle to find a proper Malay restaurant in the middle of Kuala Lumpur.

Number three, Jalan Ceylon. You're on a short, sloping street between Bukit Bintang and the city centre, and Bijan is the low colonial-era building in front of you, with its terrace and bar opening onto the street.

Bijan is the Malay word for sesame, and the restaurant was the idea of three friends — Yuen Sze To, Lissa Yeoh and Way Cheng Yeoh — who began researching it in 2002. Their problem was simple. Malay food was everywhere in Malaysia, in homes, at stalls and at weddings, but hardly anyone served it in a restaurant setting, with the care you'd expect for French or Japanese cooking. In 2004, Tourism Malaysia named it the country's best Malay restaurant.

The cooking leans on recipes handed down in the owners' own families, then sharpened with a few restaurant habits — cuts of beef, such as ribs, that you wouldn't normally find in a Malay dish, and plating that takes its time. Look out for the rendang, the slow-cooked spiced beef, and the dishes cooked in masak lemak, a rich turmeric and coconut gravy.

[beat]

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that this restaurant helped change what people thought Malay food was for. Not just something you ate at home, but something you could take a guest to. Plenty of modern Malay restaurants have followed since.

From here, walk up the hill to Changkat Bukit Bintang, a short street of old shophouses turned into bars and restaurants, where the city goes to have dinner and keep going.

## 09 · Nasi Lemak Tanglin

*DINE · Nasi Lemak Tanglin · 3.144078, 101.690677*

If you arrive after ten o'clock, the chicken may already be gone.

Jalan Cenderasari, on the edge of the government quarter, a short walk from the National Mosque. You're looking for the Tanglin food centre, a covered hawker hall, and inside it, stall number six. If there's a queue, you've found it.

The stall goes back to 1948, when a cook called Suryati Jawirunnah began selling nasi lemak — rice cooked in coconut milk — from a small wooden shack on this very spot, long before the hawker hall was built round it. Her son, Zainal Abidin Hassan, took over and made the sambal himself every morning. Her granddaughter runs it now. Three generations, one stall.

Nasi lemak is the closest thing Malaysia has to a national breakfast, and the plate is built the same way everywhere: coconut rice, crisp fried anchovies, peanuts, cucumber and a spoonful of chilli sambal. The gift detail is that sambal. Suryati cooked hers slowly for three to four hours, and the family still does. It comes out dark red and caramel-sweet, with a heat that arrives a moment later.

Then come the extras, which is where people get competitive: fried chicken, cockles in sambal, squid, beef in a dark sauce. They go fast. The stall opens at seven in the morning and is usually finished by around noon.

[beat]

Here's what I love about this place. It sits a short walk from the police headquarters at Bukit Aman and from the National Mosque, so on any morning the queue is a mix of office workers, officers in uniform and families after prayers, all waiting at the same counter. It's one of the more democratic institutions in the city.

When you've eaten, walk down the hill to Masjid Negara, the National Mosque, and look up at its folded concrete roof. If you've time, the Perdana Botanical Garden is just beyond, and a good place to walk off breakfast.

## 10 · PS150

*DRINK · PS150 · 3.141045, 101.698096*

The way in is through a toy shop.

Number one hundred and fifty, Jalan Petaling, at the southern end of Chinatown. That's where the name comes from: Petaling Street, one-fifty. Look for a small, brightly lit shopfront full of old toys and retro figures. The bar's name is on a modest sign on the wall, and the entrance is a door at the back of the shop.

The building is a pre-war shophouse with a past. In the 1980s it was a brothel, and later a hostel for migrant workers, before it was left empty. When the bar moved in, the owners kept as much of the decay as they could: plaster peeling in its own good time, bare brick, tree roots pushing through the walls.

Once you're through, the bar unfolds in three rooms, and each one is a different period. First, the Opium Den, small and close, with beaded curtains. Then an open-air courtyard. Then the main bar, with a red neon sign that reads Chi Cheung Kai, the old Cantonese name for Petaling Street.

The menu follows the same idea. It's arranged by era — vintage, classic, tiki, disco and contemporary — so you can drink your way from the nineteenth century to the present, and the flavours are local: pandan, lychee, chilli.

[beat]

What's interesting is the timing. PS150 opened in 2016, when this end of Chinatown was mostly closed shutters after dark, and in its first year it was the only bar from Malaysia on the list of Asia's Fifty Best Bars. A good deal of the neighbourhood's revival since has followed in its footsteps.

The upstairs café, Merchant's Lane, ran in the same building for nearly ten years and closed at the start of 2026. From here, Kwai Chai Hong is a couple of minutes' walk away, off Lorong Panggung: a little restored back lane with murals of 1960s street life, and the best place in Chinatown to see what these shophouses looked like from behind.

## 11 · VCR

*DRINK · VCR · 3.143253, 101.705448*

According to the people who run it, this little house was once home to prison warders.

Jalan Galloway, on the slope between Chinatown and Bukit Bintang. VCR is the two-storey house in front of you, painted black. You'll know it by the people at the outside tables, almost all of them with a coffee.

The building dates from 1927. It stands near the site of the old Pudu Jail, the great walled prison that has since been demolished, and the café's own story is that this street was where many of the warders lived. That's their account rather than a historian's, so take it in the spirit offered. What isn't in doubt is that the house had been through a good deal before 2013, when Andrew Lee and his team moved in and turned it into a café.

Go inside and look at what they kept. The space is high, light and plain, with the old proportions left alone. Much of its original character — the bones of a pre-war house — has simply been allowed to stay, while the coffee bar and kitchen were slotted in around it.

The coffee itself is the point. VCR belongs to the generation of cafés that gave Kuala Lumpur a specialty coffee scene, at a time when the default order in this city was still a sweet, strong kopi from a coffee shop.

[beat]

What's quietly radical here is the choice of building. A decade or so ago, an old house like this was more likely to be knocked down than restored. VCR made the argument that a pre-war house, kept largely as it was, is exactly what people want to sit in — and these days the city is full of cafés making the same argument.

If you're in the mood for something stronger later, Pahit, a gin bar in a 1920s shophouse, is barely a minute away on Jalan Sin Chew Kee. Take a stroll round the back streets first, though; this quiet pocket between two busy districts is easy to miss.

## 12 · LOKL Coffee Co

*DRINK · LOKL Coffee Co · 3.148788, 101.697994*

Only a few years before this café opened, much of this street was abandoned shophouses with rotting shutters.

You're on Jalan Tun H.S. Lee, number thirty, in the oldest part of the city, a short walk from Medan Pasar, the old market square. LOKL is the café at street level. Look up at the facades along this row: these are colonial-era shophouses, two storeys or three, with tall shuttered windows on the upper floors.

LOKL opened in 2012, founded by the husband-and-wife team of Ng Ping Ho, a filmmaker, and Cheryl Samad, an actress. It shares its building with a hostel called BackHome, which restored four of these old shophouses into rooms, dormitories and a courtyard. The café is the hostel's kitchen, but it was always meant as a neighbourhood place too, somewhere for the office workers round here to sit down.

Go in and look at the walls and ceiling. The original brickwork has been stripped back and whitewashed rather than plastered over, and the old timber beams overhead have been given the same treatment, with a new timber floor below. It's restoration done lightly: clean up what's there, add as little as possible.

[beat]

What I find interesting is the pairing. A café gives a heritage building a reason to have its doors open all day, and a hostel brings in people who would never otherwise sleep in the oldest part of town. Together they did more for this street than a preservation order ever could. The same founder went on to create KLoé, the design hotel on Bukit Bintang.

When you leave, walk the short distance to Medan Pasar, the square where Kuala Lumpur's first market stood. Look for the little Art Deco clock tower in the middle — then carry on down Jalan Tun H.S. Lee to the Sin Sze Si Ya temple, the oldest Taoist temple in the city.

## 13 · Ho Kow Hainam Kopitiam

*DRINK · Ho Kow Hainam Kopitiam · 3.141349, 101.697680*

For more than sixty years this coffee shop lived in a back lane. Then it was evicted, and moved about a minute's walk away.

Jalan Balai Polis, in Chinatown, on the main street rather than the lane behind it. Ho Kow is in the shophouse in front of you; on most mornings, the queue is the giveaway. The lane behind is Lorong Panggung, better known as Kwai Chai Hong, and that is where this story starts.

In 1956 a Hainanese man called Ho Kee Soon opened a small coffee shop there, selling tea, soft-boiled eggs and toast. Hainanese families ran a great many of Malaysia's old coffee shops, the kopitiams, and the formula hardly varied: strong coffee, eggs barely cooked, and toast spread with kaya, the sweet coconut jam. His son, Ho Kow, gave the shop its name.

In 2018 the family received an eviction notice. Rather than close, they moved round the corner, to the larger shophouse you're looking at now. Ho Kow's daughter, Ho Sook Chan, runs it today with her own children, which makes this the third and fourth generations.

Go in and order the classics. The coffee is kopi, roasted dark and served with condensed milk unless you say otherwise. The eggs come runny, in a little bowl, and the trick is a dash of soy sauce and white pepper. The toast is thick, with kaya and a slab of butter.

[beat]

Here's what I find touching about it. The move should have been the end. Instead the new shop became a destination in its own right, and the old lane it left behind has since been restored and opened to the public. Both sides of the story survived.

When you've finished, step round the back to Kwai Chai Hong itself. Look for the murals of 1960s street life on the walls — and imagine a coffee shop tucked into the lane, where this one began.

## 14 · Pahit

*DRINK · Pahit · 3.143408, 101.705019*

In colonial Malaya, the sundowner of choice was gin with bitters, and the word for bitter was pahit.

Jalan Sin Chew Kee is a short, quiet street of old shophouses, tucked between Chinatown and Bukit Bintang. Pahit is at number three, in a shophouse from the 1920s. If you're facing it, you're in the right place; if you're lost, you're probably one street over, which is easily done round here.

The name is the joke and the history at once. Planters and officials in colonial Malaya would call for a gin pahit — gin with a dash of bitters — as the sun went down. The phrase outlived the empire that coined it, and a Malaysian bar has now borrowed it back.

The bar was set up by CK Kho, who also runs Coley in Bangsar, one of the city's better-known cocktail bars. The back bar is lined with gins, dozens of them, from all over the world. The house speciality is the gin and tonic, and here it's done with fruit infused into the gin by slow, gentle cooking. Look out for local flavours: jackfruit, pomelo, and assam boi, the salty preserved plum that Malaysians have been dropping into drinks for generations.

[beat]

What I like about Pahit is that it takes the most colonial drink there is and makes it unmistakably local. Gin is the British part. The jackfruit and the preserved plum are not. It's a small, cheerful act of reclamation, and it happens to taste very good.

When you leave, VCR, the café in a 1927 house, is a minute away on Jalan Galloway, if you need a coffee to balance things out. And if you walk the length of Jalan Sin Chew Kee, look up at the upper floors as you go; the shopfronts have changed many times, but the upper storeys are where these buildings keep their age.

## 15 · Bar Trigona

*DRINK · Bar Trigona · 3.158093, 101.713682*

This bar is named after a bee with no sting.

You're at the Four Seasons Hotel on Persiaran Petronas, right beside the Petronas Towers. Bar Trigona is inside the hotel. If you're standing in the forecourt, the towers will be filling most of the sky above you.

Trigona is the name of a family of tiny stingless bees found across Malaysia. They build their hives in hollow places, including coconut trees, and their honey, known in Malay as madu kelulut, is thinner and sharper than ordinary honey, with a sourness that surprises people. It sits at the heart of the bar's drinks.

The bar has been a regular on the list of Asia's Fifty Best Bars, appearing for six years running by 2024, and in 2022 it won the award for the best cocktail menu on that list. It has also picked up awards for sustainability, more than once.

That's the real story here. The team works on what they call a root-to-shoot approach, using as much of each ingredient as they can rather than throwing away the parts that don't look pretty. Their motto is reuse, recycle, repurpose. They have gone further than most: guests have been able to adopt a beehive on a kelulut farm in Negeri Sembilan, and part of what you pay for a drink goes towards restoring habitats for local bees.

[beat]

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how unusual this is. Hotel bars exist to make guests feel they could be anywhere comfortable in the world. Bar Trigona does the opposite: it builds its menu from Malaysian farms and Malaysian forests, and quietly argues that the most luxurious thing in a glass might be something grown down the road.

When you leave, step out into KLCC Park, the gardens at the foot of the towers. After dark the lake has a fountain show, set to music, and the towers look best from the far side of the water.

## 16 · Heli Lounge Bar

*DRINK · Heli Lounge Bar · 3.151186, 101.709846*

By day, helicopters land on this roof. By night, you can order a drink on it.

Jalan Sultan Ismail, outside Menara KH, a little way from the Raja Chulan monorail station. Look up at the tower; the flat top is a helipad. To reach it, take the lift to the thirty-fourth floor, pay at the bar inside — the entry price includes drinks — and then climb one last staircase out into the open air.

Up there, there's no glass between you and the city. The platform is a working helipad, which means it was built to keep the approach clear, not to frame a view, and the effect is startling. Kuala Lumpur spreads out on every side, and the only thing above you is the sky.

Look north first, and you'll find the Petronas Towers, close enough that you can see the bridge between them. Then turn to the west and look for the KL Tower on its hill, with its pod near the top. From up here, you can see that the tower stands on high ground — Bukit Nanas, Pineapple Hill — which is why it can look taller than the Petronas Towers from certain angles, even though it isn't.

[beat]

What I love about this place is that it's honest. Most rooftop bars in this city are glass boxes, air-conditioned and sealed off. This is the roof of an office block, still doing its day job, with a bar wheeled on at sunset. It's warm, it can be windy, and it shows you the real skyline without any reflections in the way.

Practicalities matter here. The bar opens only in the evening, closes on Sundays and when the weather turns, and it's worth checking before you set off. If you're up there at dusk, wait for the moment the city lights come on below you — and then look back towards Bukit Nanas, where the forest is still dark.

## 17 · Bangunan Zhongshan

*SHOP · Bangunan Zhongshan · 3.137892, 101.697139*

There were offers to knock this building down, and one to turn it into a karaoke bar of a less respectable kind.

Jalan Rotan, in Kampung Attap, a quiet district just south of Chinatown. The Zhongshan Building is the row in front of you: three four-storey shophouses from the 1950s, joined together, with a plain, mid-century front. You should be able to see small shop signs at street level and windows stacked above.

The name comes from the Selangor Zhongshan Association, a Chinese clan association that once had its rooms here. Over the decades, the building housed families, small merchants and a frozen-food business, and later became a hostel for foreign workers. It belongs to the family of Rob Tan, whose grandparents bought up the units one by one.

When the family considered selling, the offers were not encouraging: buyers wanted to demolish it, or to convert it into a karaoke bar with rooms upstairs. Instead, Rob Tan and his wife, Liza Ho, chose to restore it. A grant from the heritage organisation Think City covered about a fifth of the cost, and in 2017 the building reopened as a home for independent creative businesses.

Go in and wander. When it opened, the tenants included a record shop, Tandang Records. There's the Malaysia Design Archive, which collects the country's graphic design history — posters, packaging and the rest. There's a library, studios, a bakery and small galleries. Around two dozen small businesses share the space, though the mix changes over time.

[beat]

[beat]

What's worth knowing is that this building belongs to a particular moment, in the eighties and nineties, when Malaysian architects were trying hard to find a national style. Some looked to Islamic geometry, as at Dayabumi. Some looked to traditional houses. This library looked to dress, and to the pride of formal occasions. Whether you find it charming or a little literal, it's a building that knows exactly what it wants to say.

Before you leave, walk round the side of the building to see the roof from another angle. The points look quite different from the side, and the folded shape is much easier to read.

## 35 · Menara Tabung Haji

*OBSERVE · Menara Tabung Haji · 3.158307, 101.719851*

This tower is a savings bank for one purpose only: getting to Mecca.

You're on Jalan Tun Razak, a little east of the Petronas Towers. Find the rounded tower with five tall white columns standing around it and rising up its sides. It looks less like an office building than an enormous drum.

Tabung Haji is the pilgrims' fund. It was set up so that Malaysian Muslims could save, a little at a time, towards the cost of the hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca. Its headquarters was designed by Hijjas Kasturi, one of the most important Malaysian architects of his generation, and completed in the mid-eighties.

Hijjas later said this was one of the hardest buildings he ever designed, because the client wanted the form itself to express what the institution was for. So look at the five columns. They stand for the five pillars of Islam, the five duties of every Muslim, of which the hajj is one.

Then look at the shape of the whole. It's often said to recall a drum, the kind traditionally beaten to call pilgrims together for the journey.

[beat]

And here's the thing. Most office towers in the eighties were trying to look like they belonged in New York. This one was trying to look like it belonged to its purpose. You can argue about whether symbolism makes good architecture, but there's no mistaking what this building is for.

Before you go, walk round to the other side for a clearer view of the columns against the tower. And keep this building in mind: Hijjas Kasturi also designed the keris-shaped Menara Tun Perak, the old Maybank tower. Seeing both is the quickest lesson in his thinking.

## 36 · Menara Tun Perak (formerly Menara Maybank)

*OBSERVE · Menara Tun Perak · 3.147451, 101.699417*

This tower is a dagger, still in its sheath.

You're on Jalan Tun Perak, at the foot of the tall white tower that until July 2026 was called Menara Maybank. It's now Menara Tun Perak. Look up and find the shape: two square blocks, locked together at one corner, rising to a sharp, angled top.

It was designed by Hijjas Kasturi Associates and built between 1984 and 1988 for Maybank, one of the country's largest banks. It's around two hundred and forty-four metres tall, with fifty floors, and for a time it was among the tallest buildings in the city.

The gift detail is the form. It's based on the keris, the wavy-bladed Malay dagger, shown pointing upwards and partly drawn from its sheath. The keris is a sacred object in Malay culture, a symbol of power, protection and status. So the tower presents the bank as something strong and dignified, and very much of this place.

Now, have a look at the name. Tun Perak was the Bendahara, or chief minister, of the Malacca Sultanate in the fifteenth century — one of the great statesmen of Malay history. When Maybank moved its headquarters into Merdeka 118, which has been renamed Menara Merdeka Maybank, this building took his name.

[beat]

What I find interesting is how this tower sits next to its successor. Look south, and you'll see Merdeka 118 on the skyline, the second-tallest building in the world. Forty years separate them. The old tower used a Malay symbol to look modern; the new one is simply very, very tall.

Before you go, remember that the tower also houses the Maybank Numismatic Museum, which tells the story of money in Malaysia. And then walk round to the side to see how the two blocks interlock — the trick is easiest to read from the corner.

## 37 · Kompleks Dayabumi

*OBSERVE · Kompleks Dayabumi · 3.144377, 101.694008*

Before the Petronas Towers, Petronas lived here.

Look at the white tower beside the river, between Central Market and the old railway station. It's wrapped in a lattice of pointed arches, layer upon layer, so the whole building looks as if it's wearing a carved screen.

This is Dayabumi, completed in 1984 and opened that May by the prime minister, Mahathir Mohamad. It was designed by a joint venture of two local practices, Arkitek MAA and BEP Architects, and it's about a hundred and fifty-seven metres tall, with thirty-five floors. It was originally planned to be sixty storeys.

Have a look at the arches. In Islamic architecture, a screen of patterned openings lets air and light through while keeping out the sun's glare. Here that old idea is applied to a modern office tower. It's ornament, but it's also shade.

The gift detail is who moved in. Petronas, the national oil company, made this its headquarters in 1984, years before its famous towers went up across the city. And at the foot of the complex sits the General Post Office.

The site has history, too. For much of the twentieth century, this land held the railway's workshops and depots. When the complex was built, Central Market, right next door, came close to being demolished. It survived.

[beat]

Here's why it matters. Dayabumi was one of the earliest serious attempts to make a modern tower look Malaysian, and specifically Islamic, rather than borrowing straight from America. Plenty of what came after — the Petronas Towers included — owes something to that idea.

Before you go, cross to the footbridge over the river and look back. With the Masjid Jamek upstream and the old station behind you, you can see a hundred years of the city's attempts at an eastern architecture all at once.

## 38 · Bangunan Parlimen Malaysia (Parliament House)

*OBSERVE · Bangunan Parlimen Malaysia · 3.150054, 101.679094*

The tower of Malaysia's parliament is wrapped in a concrete grille, and that grille is doing two jobs at once.

You're on the approach to Parliament House, on a hill in the Lake Gardens, near the National Monument. You may not be able to go in, but you don't need to. Find the tall white tower, and in front of it, the long, low block where the two chambers meet.

It was designed by William Ivor Shipley, a British architect in the Public Works Department, and opened on 2 November 1963, just weeks after Malaysia was formed. The tower is seventeen storeys and seventy-seven metres high, and it holds the offices of members of parliament. The low block beneath holds the two houses: the Dewan Rakyat and the Dewan Negara.

Have a look at the tower's skin. It's covered in precast panels, sunbreakers, which were lifted into place in 1963. The facade is in two layers, like a grille, and the effect is to keep direct sunlight off the windows while letting air move through. It's also meant to give the structure strength.

That double job — climate and structure — is the gift detail. In the tropics, a sealed glass box overheats. A building that shades itself is simply sensible. Here the shading becomes the whole character of the building.

[beat]

What's worth knowing is what this building represents. It went up in the years between independence and the formation of Malaysia, when the country's new institutions needed new buildings. Its architects chose modernism, not a borrowed eastern style, and made it work for the climate. It's a building of confident, practical optimism.

Before you go, walk down the hill to the National Monument, a few minutes away, and then look back. The tower stands over the trees, and from there you can see how its pattern of sunbreakers reads from a distance — like a screen pulled across the sky.

## 39 · Stadium Merdeka

*OBSERVE · Stadium Merdeka · 3.139270, 101.700772*

On 31 August 1957, a man stood in this stadium and shouted the same word seven times.

You're at Stadium Merdeka, in the old city, on the hill south of Chinatown. In front of you is a low, open stadium, with cantilevered roofs over its stands and tall floodlight towers at its corners. And right next to it, filling the sky, is Merdeka 118.

The stadium was built in less than a year, between September 1956 and August 1957, for one purpose: the declaration of independence of the Federation of Malaya. It was designed by Stanley Jewkes, director of the Public Works Department, and when it was finished it held world records for its prestressed floodlight towers and its cantilevered shell roofs.

Then, on the morning of 31 August 1957, in front of more than twenty thousand people, the first prime minister, Tunku Abdul Rahman, read the proclamation and shouted Merdeka — freedom — seven times.

The stadium had a lively life afterwards: the proclamation of Malaysia in 1963, Muhammad Ali boxing Joe Bugner in 1975, and Michael Jackson in 1996. But by the nineties it was threatened with demolition. It survived thanks to a public outcry and the Asian financial crisis, which killed off the redevelopment. It was then restored to its 1957 appearance, work that won a UNESCO heritage conservation award in 2008 and was completed the following year.

[beat]

And here's the thing about the tower beside it. Merdeka 118 — now officially Menara Merdeka Maybank — is the second-tallest building in the world, and it was built on the stadium's doorstep. You can read that as a celebration of independence, or as the modern city crowding its own birthplace. Standing here, it's hard not to feel both.

Before you leave, if the gates are open, find a spot in the stands facing the field, and picture the crowd in 1957. Then look up at the tower. Few places put a country's past and present quite so close together.

## 40 · KL Forest Eco Park

*OBSERVE · Taman Eko Rimba Kuala Lumpur · 3.152931, 101.702692*

This patch of rainforest has been protected since 1906, and it's right in the middle of the city.

You're at the KL Forest Eco Park, on Bukit Nanas, the hill below the KL Tower. There are two ways in, one from Jalan Raja Chulan and one from the KL Tower grounds. Either way, within a minute of leaving the road, you'll be under tall trees, with the traffic noise fading behind you.

Bukit Nanas means Pineapple Hill. In 1906 the colonial government gazetted seventeen and a half hectares of forest here as a reserve, which makes it one of the oldest permanent forest reserves in the country. Development has nibbled away at it since, including the KL Tower, and about nine hectares remain. But what remains is the real thing: lowland tropical rainforest, the kind that covered this whole valley before the tin miners came.

The gift is the canopy walk. A series of suspended walkways takes you up among the treetops, high above the forest floor. Up there, look through the leaves and you'll see office towers and the KL Tower itself, peering in from all sides.

Then look at the trees. Some of them are enormous, with buttress roots spreading out like walls at their base. That's what a tropical tree does when the soil is thin: it braces itself sideways.

[beat]

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how unusual this is. Most cities keep their parks, but few keep a piece of the original forest they were built in. This is what Kuala Lumpur was before it was Kuala Lumpur — and it's still here, in the middle of the skyline.

Before you leave, take one of the trails down to the bottom of the hill, rather than going back the way you came. Look for the moment the forest stops, and the city starts again; it's almost a straight line.

## 41 · Saloma Link

*OBSERVE · Saloma Link · 3.160956, 101.708976*

This footbridge was designed after a wedding decoration.

You're on Saloma Link, the pedestrian bridge between Kampung Baru and the KLCC area. Walk to the middle of the span and stop. Behind you is Kampung Baru, the old Malay village with its low houses and gardens. Ahead are the Petronas Towers and the glass towers around them.

The bridge opened in February 2020. Its main span is about sixty-nine metres, crossing the Klang River and an elevated highway, and the walkway runs some three hundred and seventy metres end to end. It was designed by VERITAS Design Group for the Kampung Baru Development Corporation.

The gift detail is its shape. The curved, woven lattice that wraps the walkway was inspired by the sirih junjung, an arrangement of betel leaves that plays a special part in Malay weddings. So this bridge, between an old Malay village and the modern city, is dressed as a wedding gift. After dark, the lattice lights up.

The name comes from the street where the bridge lands: Jalan Saloma, named after Saloma, one of the great Malay singers and screen stars of the fifties and sixties.

[beat]

What's worth thinking about, standing here, is the view on either side. Kampung Baru has been a Malay settlement since the turn of the twentieth century, and it has resisted redevelopment for decades. The towers have been pressing at its edges for just as long. This bridge doesn't settle that argument. It simply lets you walk from one side to the other in a few minutes, which makes the contrast impossible to miss.

Before you leave, walk to the Kampung Baru end and turn into its streets. Look for the timber houses with their steep roofs, and the food stalls in between. Then turn round and look back at the towers; it's one of the best views in the city.

## 42 · KLCC Park

*OBSERVE · KLCC Park · 3.155580, 101.714780*

The man who designed this park died before it was planted.

You're in KLCC Park, at the foot of the Petronas Towers. Find a spot near the lake, where you can see both the water and the towers above you.

The park was designed by Roberto Burle Marx, the great Brazilian landscape architect. It was his last work; he died in 1994, and the park was created in the mid-nineties. It covers about fifty acres, and its planting is deliberately local: some nineteen hundred indigenous trees, sixty-six kinds of palm, and twenty-three mature trees moved here from the old Selangor Turf Club, the racecourse that once stood on this site.

Have a look at the paths and the water. Burle Marx loved flowing curves, and you'll find few straight lines here. The lake is called Lake Symphony, and after dark the fountains put on a show set to music. There's also a jogging track and a children's playground, so the park is in constant use.

Now, look up at the towers. They were designed by the Argentine-American architect César Pelli, and their floor plan is based on the Rub el Hizb, the eight-pointed star made of two overlapping squares, a symbol from Islamic art. Pelli added rounded sections between the points to give more space, which is why the towers look both faceted and soft. At the forty-first and forty-second floors, a double-decker bridge links the two, about a hundred and seventy metres above the ground.

[beat]

What's worth knowing is how the two parts depend on each other. The towers are all about height and symbolism. The park is about shade, water and somewhere to sit. Without the park, the towers would be a spectacle. With it, they're part of the city.

Before you leave, walk round to the far side of the lake, away from the shopping centre. Find the spot where the towers are reflected in the water, and wait for the light to change. It's the best view of them in Kuala Lumpur.
