Kuala Lumpur means muddy confluence, and this is the confluence.

You're at Mahsjid Jah mek, on Jah lahn Toon Peh rak, on the point of land where two rivers meet: the Gom bak, coming in from the north, and the Klahng. In front of you is a mosque of red brick with white stripes, with onion domes and slender minarets, sitting in a grove of palms right at the water's edge.

This is the oldest mosque in Kuala Lumpur. It was opened in December nineteen oh nine by the Sultan of Seh lahng or, and it was designed by Ay Bee Hubback, the same British architect who built the railway station a year later. It's in that same Indo Saracenic style, with Mughal domes and the red and white brickwork called blood and bandages.

Have a look at the minarets. The stripes are layers of red brick and white plaster, which is a cheap, clever way to make a building look elaborate. And look at the setting: it was built on an old Malay burial ground, close to where the tin miners first landed in the eighteen fifties.

The gift detail is less happy. In nineteen ninety three, during a heavy downpour, one of the domes collapsed. It has since been rebuilt, but it's a reminder that tropical rain can do serious damage, even to the city's oldest buildings.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that this is where the city's story starts. Tin miners came up the Klahng River, landed at the confluence, and the muddy clearing they made became the capital. Everything else in Kuala Lumpur radiates out from this spot.

Before you leave, walk to the riverside walkway and look across the water at the mosque from the opposite bank. That's the classic view, and after dark, the riverbanks here are lit up in blue.
