Somewhere in this building is the largest pewter tankard in the world. It holds nearly two thousand eight hundred litres.

You're at the Royal Selangor Visitor Centre in Setapak, a few kilometres north of the city centre. The building in front of you is the company's headquarters and its working factory. Inside there's a museum, a shop and a factory floor where you can watch pewter being made.

The firm was founded in 1885 by Yong Koon, a pewtersmith from China, in a small shop called Ngeok Foh, meaning Jade Peace. Pewter is mostly tin, and this part of Malaya had plenty of tin. His first customers wanted ceremonial pieces: incense burners, candle holders and joss-stick holders for Chinese altars. He polished them with a wild leaf that has a natural rough texture, known as the stone leaf.

Then the British arrived, and they wanted tankards, ashtrays and tea sets. The firm adapted, and became known as Royal Selangor Pewter, with the endorsement of the Sultan of Selangor. In 1992 it dropped the word pewter from its name, because it was making so much else.

Now the gift detail. To mark the company's hundredth birthday in 1985, it made a pewter tankard nearly two metres tall, weighing over a tonne and a half. It's in the Guinness record books as the largest pewter tankard in the world. Look for it during your visit.

The factory tour is free, and you'll see craftspeople casting, polishing and finishing by hand. If you'd like to have a go yourself, the School of Hard Knocks will let you hammer your own pewter bowl with traditional tools.

What's quietly remarkable here is the continuity. This is a tin story, and Kuala Lumpur is a tin city — it grew up around the mines. Royal Selangor is one of the few places where you can still hold the result in your hands.

Before you leave, have a look in the museum at the earliest pieces, the ones made for altars. Then compare them with the tankards in the shop. You'll see a whole colonial history in a single metal.
