Inside this building, the bookshelves step down towards where the cinema screen used to be.

Jah lahn Sool tahn, on the edge of Chinatown. Rex Kay El is the old cinema building in front of you; its name has kept the Rex of the cinema it used to be. Stand back on the far pavement and look at the frontage. You're looking at a survivor of several fires.

The Rex Theatre first opened in nineteen forty seven, designed by the architect James Robert Veh tah vah nahm in the Streamline Moderne style, that sleek, rounded, speedboat look of the late thirties and forties. In nineteen seventy two it burned down, during a burglary that went wrong. It was rebuilt in nineteen seventy six as a single screen cinema with eleven hundred seats.

Then the multiplexes came, and the Rex closed in the early twenty zeros. It became a backpackers' hostel, and in two thousand and seven another fire gutted it.

In twenty nineteen it was reborn as Rex Kay El, a cultural centre for Malaysian creative businesses, the work of architects Shin Chahng and Shin Tsung. They chose to work with the old cinema's structure rather than erase it, so the building still reads as what it was.

The gift detail is upstairs. The bookshop, run by Book Excess, occupies the old cinema hall, and its shelves are arranged across the stepped floor where the seats used to be. So you browse downhill, towards where the screen would have been, as if the books were the audience.

And here's the thing. A city like this usually does one of two things with a burnt out cinema: knocks it down, or restores it so perfectly you'd never guess what happened. Rex Kay El chose a third way, keep the damage, fill the place with people, and let the building tell its own story.

When you leave, walk a couple of minutes south west and you'll reach Jah lahn Peh tah ling, the old heart of Chinatown, with its covered market. Look for the green roof over the street.
