This is a railway station dressed as a Mughal palace.

Jah lahn Sool tahn Hee shah moo din, on the pavement opposite the Majestic Hotel. Turn to face the station: a long, white building with arcades along the front, and on top, a skyline of domes, pointed arches and slim towers. Those little domed pavilions on stilts at the corners are called chut trees, a form borrowed from India.

The architect was Arthur Benison Hubback, a British architect in the colonial Public Works Department, and the station was opened on the first of August, nineteen ten. It replaced a much smaller station of eighteen eighty six on the same site.

Have a look at the roofline. There's a lot going on: domes, pointed arches, slim towers. The style is sometimes called Indo Saracenic, a British invention that mixed Islamic and Indian forms and applied them to colonial buildings across Asia. It wasn't Malay at all. It was the empire's idea of what an eastern building should look like.

And here's the gift. There were originally six chut trees. When the station was refurbished in nineteen eighty six, two more were added. So even the decoration kept growing.

The station was more than a station. For decades, the Station Hotel occupied its upper floors and northern wing. Since two thousand and one the long distance trains have run from Kay El Sentral instead, but local Commuter trains still stop here, so it remains a working station.

What's worth knowing is how much of colonial Kuala Lumpur Hubback designed. The Mahsjid Jah mek, the old Seh lahng or Museum, a good deal of this city's postcard image, they're his. He gave the city its face, and that face came from India.

Before you leave, turn round and look up the slope to the Majestic Hotel, opposite. The two buildings have faced each other since nineteen thirty two. Then, if you've the time, walk north to Dye ah boo mee and see how the nineteen eighties answered Hubback's arches.
