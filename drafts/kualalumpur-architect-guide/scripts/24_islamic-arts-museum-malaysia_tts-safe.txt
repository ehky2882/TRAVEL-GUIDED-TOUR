Islamic Arts Museum Malaysia

The tiles around this entrance were laid by craftsmen from Iran.

Jah lahn Lem bah Per dah nah, at the edge of the Lake Gardens, a short walk from the National Mosque. You should be facing a pale, modern building with a grand arched entrance, and above the roofline, a cluster of domes in turquoise tile.

The museum opened in December nineteen ninety eight, and it holds more than seven thousand objects from across the Islamic world. It's the largest museum of its kind in South East Asia. But before you go in, take a minute with the entrance.

Look at the great arched porch. That shape, a tall vaulted recess set into a flat wall, is called an ee wahn, and it's the gateway form of mosques and palaces from Iran to Central Asia. Here, the tilework was done by Iranian craftsmen, and it's worth getting close enough to see how the pattern is built from small, repeating pieces.

Then look up at the domes. When you're in the galleries, keep looking up; the domes are as rewarding from below as from outside.

Inside, the gift is the Ottoman Room, a complete early nineteenth century room, panelled, painted and gilded, which was restored by the museum's own conservators. Walk in, and you're standing inside someone's reception room from two hundred years ago. There's also a gallery devoted to architecture, which is the right place to start if you've come from the mosque next door.

The interesting bit is the contrast. A few hundred metres away, the National Mosque tries hard not to look Middle Eastern. This museum does the reverse, gathering the arts of the Islamic world and placing Malaysia within that much larger story. The two buildings make a good argument with each other.

When you leave, walk down the hill to the National Mosque and look at the two side by side, the folded concrete umbrella and the turquoise domes, thirty odd years and a whole philosophy apart.
