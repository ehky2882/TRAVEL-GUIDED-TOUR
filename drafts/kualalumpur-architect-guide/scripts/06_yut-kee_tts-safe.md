The menu in here has barely changed since nineteen twenty eight.

You're at number one, Jah lahn Kah moon ting, outside a three storey building that used to be a house. If it's a weekend morning, the queue on the pavement will tell you you're in the right place. Inside, the walls are covered with faded newspaper and magazine clippings, and the room has the plain, bright look of the old Malaysian coffee shops, the koh pee tee ahms, of a generation or two ago.

Yoot Kee was opened in January nineteen twenty eight by a cook from Hye nahn Island, in southern China, who had worked as a private chef for the household of Choo Kee ah Peng, one of the leading Chinese figures in colonial Kuala Lumpur. After he died in nineteen forty seven, his three wives kept the business going. His grandson, Mervyn Lee, runs it now and says he has seen fifth generation customers.

For eighty six years it traded from a building on Jah lahn Dahng Wahng ee, round the corner. In twenty fourteen it moved here, and the regulars simply followed.

Now, the food. The famous dish is roh tee bah bee: bread stuffed with shredded pork, onion and crab, then fried, and eaten with Worcestershire sauce, which comes to the table as a matter of course. There's a Hye nah neez chicken chop with gravy, and kah yah, the coconut jam, made the slow way in a double boiler and spread on toast.

And here's the thing about that bottle of Worcestershire. Hye nah neez cooks often worked in British kitchens, and they took the colonial menu home and made it their own. A chicken chop with gravy is not an English dish and not a Chinese one. It's a Kuala Lumpur dish, and this is one of the last places that still cooks it as if nothing had happened since.

Before you go, order a slice of the marble cake to take with you; it's baked in house and it's the one the regulars buy. Then walk the few minutes round the corner to Jah lahn Dahng Wahng ee and picture the old shop, where all this started.
