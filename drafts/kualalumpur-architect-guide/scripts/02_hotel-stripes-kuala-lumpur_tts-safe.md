When this hotel opened in February twenty seventeen, it handed out red bricks to its neighbours.

Jah lahn Kah moon ting, outside the hotel's front door. Look along the street in either direction and you'll see low rows of shophouses with covered walkways under their upper floors, the five foot ways that let you walk the length of a Malaysian street without getting rained on. Hotel Stripes is the tall newcomer among them.

This is one of the older corners of Kuala Lumpur. In the colonial era it was a tin mining settlement, and in the late nineteenth century the British administration had its wooden and thatched buildings replaced with brick and tile, because thatch and a growing town do not mix. What survives now is mostly mid century shophouses and family businesses that have been here for generations, Yoot Kee, the Hye nah neez coffee shop, and Ah Loke the tailor among them.

The site has a private story, too. The very first office of Why Tee El, the company that built the hotel, stood on this plot. So when it came to opening, the company threw a street party and gave nine of its neighbours, local business owners on Jah lahn Kah moon ting, a red brick each, as a token of thanks.

I rather like that gesture, because it says what the building is trying to do. Inside, the restaurant has red brick arcades, dark wood floors and copper chandeliers shaped like sputniks. Up on the roof there's a pool and a bar with a view across to the Kay El Tower. It's a contemporary hotel that has chosen, quite deliberately, to look as if it grew up on this street rather than landing on it.

From the front door, Yoot Kee is round the corner, and it's the obvious next stop, a Hye nah neez breakfast that has been served since nineteen twenty eight. If you'd rather keep walking, the shophouse rows of Jah lahn Kah moon ting reward a slow stroll; look up at the upper floor windows, where the street's real age shows.
