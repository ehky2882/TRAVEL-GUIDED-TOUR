# Sultan Abdul Samad Building

At midnight on 30 August 1957, as the clock in that tower began to chime, the British flag came down for the last time.

You're standing on Dataran Merdeka, Independence Square, the wide green that used to be the colonial cricket field. Turn to face Jalan Raja. The long building in front of you is red brick with white stripes, with a tall clock tower in the middle and copper domes on top.

It was built between 1894 and 1897 to house the colonial government offices. The architect named on the foundation stone is A. C. Norman, though R. A. J. Bidwell did much of the design. The style is the same Indo-Saracenic you'll find on the railway station: Mughal and Moorish shapes, imported from British India. The red-and-white stripes have earned the nickname blood and bandages.

Look at the clock tower. It's about forty-one metres high, and it was meant to echo Big Ben — London, by way of Delhi. Then look at the domes. They're copper-clad, which is why they glow at sunset.

The numbers are good. Around four million bricks went into this building, and over five thousand pounds of copper.

It housed the government, and later the country's highest courts. After a long restoration completed in 2026, it now holds galleries, exhibition spaces and cafés, so you can finally go inside.

And here's what I find moving about this spot. The building was made to show who was in charge. On the night of independence, it became the backdrop to the moment the British handed power back. The clock that once kept colonial time chimed in the new country.

Before you go, look for the tall flagpole on the square, which marks where the Malayan flag was raised. Then walk along the front of the building to the river behind it — the confluence where Kuala Lumpur began is just there, beside the Masjid Jamek.
