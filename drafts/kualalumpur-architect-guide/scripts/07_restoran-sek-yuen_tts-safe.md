In the kitchen behind this facade, they still cook over wood fires.

Jah lahn Poo doo, number three hundred and thirteen. Look for the standalone building with steps leading up to its entrance, a little apart from the shophouse row. Next door, at three hundred and fifteen, is its younger sibling: an extension added in the seventies, said to be one of the first air conditioned restaurants in the city.

The story starts on the street. In the early nineteen forties, three brothers of the Fahng family sold won ton noodles from a hawker cart. When the war came they fled, and when it ended they came back, which not every hawker did. In nineteen forty eight they opened a proper restaurant, and later in the fifties put up the building you're looking at.

Go in, and look up first. The ceilings are high on purpose, to let the heat rise in a room that was built before air conditioning. Then look around: the old fans, tables and chairs, and photographs and newspaper cuttings of the restaurant's own past on the walls. In the sixties this was a fashionable place for wedding banquets.

The gift detail is in the kitchen. Sek Yoo en still cooks on wood fired stoves and a wood fired steamer, which almost nobody in the city bothers with any more, because wood is slow, dirty and hard to control. It's held to be the key to the traditional flavours the restaurant has always served.

Try the pee pah duck: split open and pressed flat until it looks like the Chinese lute it's named after, then roasted and served with lemon sauce.

What you're tasting is not nostalgia, exactly. It's what Cantonese restaurant cooking in this city tasted like before electricity made everything faster.

Before you leave, step back to the far side of the pavement and look at how the building stands apart from its neighbours, steps and all. Then come back for dinner if you can, when the place is at its busiest.
