The man who built this temple is often called the founder of Kuala Lumpur, and he built it for two dead men he believed were watching over him.

You're on Leh boo Poo doo, in Chinatown, a short walk from Central Market. The temple sits back from the street, so look for a narrow gateway leading off it. Inside you'll find a modest temple of red and gold, thick with incense smoke.

It was founded in eighteen sixty four by Yap Ah Loy, the Kah pee tahn Chee nah, the leader of the Chinese community in early Kuala Lumpur. He was the man who rebuilt the town after fire and war, and who did more than anyone to make a mining camp into a city.

The temple is dedicated to two figures, both of whom Yap believed had guided him through the Seh lahng or Civil War. The first, Sin See Yah, was Seng Meng Lee, a Kah pee tahn from Soong eye Oo jong, killed in eighteen sixty. The second, See See Yah, was Choong Pee ahng Lye, one of Yap's own generals. To Yap, they were protectors, and after their deaths he gave them a home.

At first the shrine was made of wood. Then, in eighteen seventy three, Yap gave the land behind his own house for a proper temple, finished in eighteen seventy five. It was rebuilt after fire and flood in the early eighteen eighties, and repaired and restored several times since.

What I find quietly remarkable is how hidden this is. It's the oldest Taoist temple in the city and a national heritage site, yet you could walk past the entrance and never know. That's the way Chinatown works: the important things are often tucked behind the shopfronts.

Before you leave, walk out and down the lane to Meh dahn Pah sar, the old market square, just to the north. This was the centre of Yap Ah Loy's town. Stand there for a moment, and you're in the middle of the first Kuala Lumpur.
