If this sculpture reminds you of the flag raising on Iwo Jima, there's a good reason.

You're at the National Monument, Too goo Neh gah rah, on Jah lahn Par lee men, in the Lake Gardens. In front of you, across a reflecting pool, is a large bronze sculpture of soldiers on a rocky base, with one of them holding up a flag.

After a visit to the United States in nineteen sixty, Toon koo Ab dool Rah mahn, Malaysia's first prime minister, saw the Marine Corps War Memorial outside Washington and wanted something similar for his own country. He met its sculptor, Felix de Weldon, and commissioned him. The result was unveiled in February nineteen sixty six.

Have a look at the figures. There are seven of them. Five are standing: one holds the flag, one has a rifle, one a machine gun, and two are helping a wounded comrade. The two lying on the ground represent defeated communist insurgents. The monument commemorates those who died in the Second World War and in the Malayan Emergency.

The gift detail is in the stones. The rocks under the soldiers' feet were imported from Karls hahm, in Sweden.

The monument has a dark chapter of its own. On the twenty sixth of August, nineteen seventy five, a bomb planted by the Malayan Communist Party exploded here and badly damaged the sculpture. It was restored and unveiled again in nineteen seventy seven.

What's worth thinking about, standing here, is how strange this monument is. It's a Malaysian memorial made by an Austrian American sculptor, modelled on an American war memorial, to remember a war against an enemy who then tried to blow it up. It tells you a great deal about the Cold War, and about the young nation trying to find its own way through it.

Before you leave, look for the older cenotaph nearby, which was moved here from its original site near the railway station in nineteen sixty four. Then walk on to the Per dah nah Botanical Garden; the path down from here takes you into the shade.
