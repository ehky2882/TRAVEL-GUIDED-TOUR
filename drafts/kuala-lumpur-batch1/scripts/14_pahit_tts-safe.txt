Pah hit

In colonial Malaya, the sundowner of choice was gin with bitters, and the word for bitter was pah hit.

Jah lahn Sin Chew Kee is a short, quiet street of old shophouses, tucked between Chinatown and Boo kit Bin tahng. Pah hit is at number three, in a shophouse from the nineteen twenties. If you're facing it, you're in the right place; if you're lost, you're probably one street over, which is easily done round here.

The name is the joke and the history at once. Planters and officials in colonial Malaya would call for a gin pah hit, gin with a dash of bitters, as the sun went down. The phrase outlived the empire that coined it, and a Malaysian bar has now borrowed it back.

The bar was set up by See Kay Koh, who also runs Coley in Bahng sar, one of the city's better known cocktail bars. The back bar is lined with gins, dozens of them, from all over the world. The house speciality is the gin and tonic, and here it's done with fruit infused into the gin by slow, gentle cooking. Look out for local flavours: jackfruit, pomelo, and ah sahm boy, the salty preserved plum that Malaysians have been dropping into drinks for generations.

What I like about Pah hit is that it takes the most colonial drink there is and makes it unmistakably local. Gin is the British part. The jackfruit and the preserved plum are not. It's a small, cheerful act of reclamation, and it happens to taste very good.

When you leave, Vee See Ar, the cafe in a nineteen twenty seven house, is a minute away on Jah lahn Galloway, if you need a coffee to balance things out. And if you walk the length of Jah lahn Sin Chew Kee, look up at the upper floors as you go; the shopfronts have changed many times, but the upper storeys are where these buildings keep their age.
