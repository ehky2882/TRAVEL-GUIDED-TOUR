Masjid Negara (National Mosque)

Look at the roof, and then imagine it pink.

You're at the National Mosque, Masjid Negara, on Jalan Perdana, at the edge of the Lake Gardens. In front of you is a low, wide building under a great folded roof, with a slender minaret rising on one side and long shaded walkways and pools spread around it. If you're going in, dress modestly; robes are provided at the entrance for visitors who need them.

It opened in August 1965, eight years after independence, and it was designed by a small team from the Public Works Department: Baharuddin Abu Kassim, Hisham Albakri and the British architect Howard Ashley. Their brief was to give a new nation a mosque that looked forward rather than back.

Have a look at the roof first. It's a sixteen-pointed star of folded concrete plates, and it's meant to look like an open umbrella — the object that, in a hot, wet country, means shelter. Now look at the minaret. Its cap is a folded umbrella. One open, one shut. The folding is also what gives the concrete the strength to span the great prayer hall.

When it opened, that roof was pink concrete. In 1987 it was covered in the green and blue tiles you see today.

Then look at everything around the building: open verandahs, deep overhangs, reflecting pools and fountains. There's very little solid wall. Air moves through, and the water cools it.

[beat]

And here's the thing. There are no onion domes here, no imported Arabian or Mughal costume, which is what colonial architects had used for Malaya's grand buildings. Masjid Negara said that a modern Malaysian mosque could be made of concrete, shade and breeze, and be every bit as dignified.

Before you leave, look for the Makam Pahlawan, the Heroes' Mausoleum, in the grounds, where some of the country's leaders are buried. Its roof is a smaller, seven-pointed concrete star — the big roof's younger sibling.
