Nah see Leh mak Tang lin

If you arrive after ten o'clock, the chicken may already be gone.

Jah lahn Chen deh rah sah ree, on the edge of the government quarter, a short walk from the National Mosque. You're looking for the Tang lin food centre, a covered hawker hall, and inside it, stall number six. If there's a queue, you've found it.

The stall goes back to nineteen forty eight, when a cook called Soor yah tee Jah wee roo nah began selling nah see leh mak, rice cooked in coconut milk, from a small wooden shack on this very spot, long before the hawker hall was built round it. Her son, Zye nahl Ah bee din Hah sahn, took over and made the sahm bahl himself every morning. Her granddaughter runs it now. Three generations, one stall.

Nah see leh mak is the closest thing Malaysia has to a national breakfast, and the plate is built the same way everywhere: coconut rice, crisp fried anchovies, peanuts, cucumber and a spoonful of chilli sahm bahl. The gift detail is that sahm bahl. Suryati cooked hers slowly for three to four hours, and the family still does. It comes out dark red and caramel sweet, with a heat that arrives a moment later.

Then come the extras, which is where people get competitive: fried chicken, cockles in sahm bahl, squid, beef in a dark sauce. They go fast. The stall opens at seven in the morning and is usually finished by around noon.

Here's what I love about this place. It sits a short walk from the police headquarters at Boo kit Ah mahn and from the National Mosque, so on any morning the queue is a mix of office workers, officers in uniform and families after prayers, all waiting at the same counter. It's one of the more democratic institutions in the city.

When you've eaten, walk down the hill to Mahsjid Neh gah rah, the National Mosque, and look up at its folded concrete roof. If you've time, the Per dah nah Botanical Garden is just beyond, and a good place to walk off breakfast.
