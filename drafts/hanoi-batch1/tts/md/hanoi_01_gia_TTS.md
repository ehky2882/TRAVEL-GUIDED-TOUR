# Gia

The best table in this restaurant faces a building that's nearly a thousand years old.

You're on van mee oo Street, outside number sixty one. Turn round for a moment. The long wall across the road belongs to the Temple of Literature, Vietnam's first university, founded in ten seventy. Now turn back. The narrow terraced house in front of you is Gia, and in twenty twenty three it became one of the first restaurants in Vietnam to win a Michelin star.

The name is half a word. Gia, as in zah ding, family. The chef, Sam chan, and business partner Long chan spent years working abroad, and the name is what homesickness looks like when you finally come back and do something about it. The same year as the star, Sam chan was given Michelin's Young Chef Award.

Inside, the room borrows from the temple across the street, dark wooden rafters, bold light fixtures, and a stone dog that guests rub for luck, exactly as people do with the stone animals over the road. There's one menu, a dozen or so courses, and it changes with the seasons and with what northern Vietnam happens to be growing.

Here's the detail I like best. One of the opening bites has, in some seasons, been a betel leaf with caviar and areca nut. Betel and areca is how a Vietnamese visit traditionally began, a chew of it was offered to a guest before anyone said anything of consequence. There's an old saying that the betel quid is the start of every conversation. So the most expensive meal on this street opens, quite deliberately, with the oldest form of hello.

That's the trick of the place. It isn't fusion, and it isn't nostalgia. It's a kitchen that took its training abroad, then came home and sat down opposite the national university to show its working.

If you've got time before or after, cross the road and go into the temple itself. In the second courtyard you'll find kway van kahk, the little red pavilion on four white pillars, the one printed on the hundred thousand dong note. Have one in your pocket and compare.
