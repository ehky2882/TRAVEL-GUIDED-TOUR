# Concrete Flakes

It looks unfinished. It's meant to.

You're in a narrow lane off leh van hoo Street, just south of the French Quarter, outside number twenty one. Look up at the building in front of you. Raw grey concrete slabs and walls, stacked into a deep frame, with a fine layer of stainless steel mesh in front, and openings cut through so the light and air get in behind it. It's called Concrete Flakes.

It was designed by the Vietnamese practice G eight A Architecture and finished in twenty twenty five. It's not a home. It's an extension to the headquarters of open asia, a company whose offices G eight A had already designed nearby, in a building called The Bridge.

Have a look at the structure. The concrete frame carries the building on the outside, like an exoskeleton, and the facade sits within it in layers. The idea is climate before style. In Hanoi's heat, a deep, shaded, perforated front keeps the direct sun off the glass, lets the breeze through, and turns hard daylight into something soft. The rough concrete is set against pale surfaces and bronze details inside.

The architects call their approach a sustainable ruin. The thinking goes like this: build a strong, simple, honest frame, the sort of thing that could outlast what it's first used for, and let everything else inside it change over time. On each floor there's a shared kitchen at the entrance, the room where people actually meet. On the roof, there's a photography studio and a space for exercise and meditation.

And here's the thing. Most new buildings in this city try very hard to look finished, polished and new. This one starts out looking a little like a ruin on purpose, so that it has nowhere to fall from. It's an odd idea, and rather a clever one.

It's a working office, so enjoy it from the lane. Then, if you're curious, look along the street. Every old house nearby has dealt with the same sun and the same rain using shutters, balconies and deep eaves. This is the same answer, given in concrete.
