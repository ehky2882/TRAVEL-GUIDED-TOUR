# Vietnam Military History Museum

Forty five metres of concrete, and it's only the front door.

You're on the approach to the new Vietnam Military History Museum, out on the western edge of Hanoi off tang long Avenue. In front of you rises the Victory Tower, a forty five metre concrete shaft that serves as the museum's grand entrance. Spread out on either side, low and severe, are the halls. If the concrete feels like it's leaning on you a little, it's meant to.

The museum opened at the start of November twenty twenty four, timed to the eightieth anniversary of the Vietnamese People's Army, which was founded in December nineteen forty four. The building took almost five years, the site covers more than thirty eight hectares, and it cost around two and a half trillion dong, something like a hundred million US dollars. That same year it won the top prize at Vietnam's national architecture awards.

The style is brutalist, in the Soviet tradition, big, bare, unapologetic concrete, used the way a composer uses brass.

Now look at the two courtyards either side. They're arranged by side. In the western courtyard: French, American and South Vietnamese equipment, a Chinook helicopter, a C one thirty transport, and a tower sculpted out of the wreckage of downed aircraft, brought over from the old museum. In the eastern courtyard: North Vietnamese, Soviet and Chinese hardware, T thirty four and T fifty four tanks, mig fighters, surface to air missiles. Even the layout tells a story.

Inside, four halls take you through the country's wars from around nine hundred BC to the present day, with about a hundred and fifty thousand objects. In the hall for the American war, look for tank number eight four three, a T fifty four from the fall of Saigon in nineteen seventy five.

For decades the museum was housed in a pale yellow French colonial building on dee en bee en foo Street, in the old centre. A national story told inside a colonial office. Now it has a building made for it, and it's chosen to speak in concrete. That says a good deal about how the country sees that history.

Before you go in, walk the full length of both courtyards, west and then east. Then climb as high as the building lets you, and look back at the city you've driven out from.
