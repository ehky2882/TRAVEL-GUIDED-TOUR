# long bee en Bridge

Here's something you'll notice straight away. The motorbikes on this bridge keep to the left.

You're on the narrow walkway at the Hanoi end of long bee en Bridge, with the railway track running down the middle and the Red River opening out ahead. Around you is a lattice of steel, riveted girders, rust and paint in roughly equal measure. If a train comes, you'll feel it.

The bridge was built between eighteen ninety nine and nineteen oh two, and opened to traffic the following year. More than three thousand Vietnamese labourers worked on it. It's well over two kilometres long, with nineteen spans, and in its day it was one of the longest bridges in Asia. The French called it the Paul doo mair Bridge, after the governor general of Indochina, who later became President of France.

You'll often hear that Gustave Eiffel designed it. He didn't. The engineering firm was day day ay pee yay, of Paris. It makes a better story with Eiffel in it, which is why the myth won't go away.

And those motorbikes on the left? Nobody seems to have written down why. The usual explanation is that the bridge was laid out by the French and simply never changed.

Now look along the line of trusses, and you'll see that it isn't all of a piece. For years this was the only bridge across the Red River linking Hanoi to the port of high fong, and so it was bombed again and again. On the eleventh of August nineteen sixty seven, American planes brought down the central span. More raids followed in nineteen seventy two. Each time, it was patched back together, and only about half of it still has its original shape.

So when you look at the joins, the trusses that don't quite match, the newer steel next to the old, you're looking at the war, repaired. Most cities would have pulled this down and built something new. Hanoi kept it, and still uses it.

If you've got twenty minutes, walk out towards the middle. Partway across, steps lead down to the long sandbank in the river, where people farm bananas and vegetables right under the bridge. And come back at dusk, when the light catches the rust.
