# bah ding Square

On the second of September, nineteen forty five, a man in a khaki suit stood on a wooden platform here and read out a declaration of independence.

You're at the edge of bah ding Square, a vast, flat lawn laid out in green squares, the most important open space in Vietnam. In front of you, across the grass, is a heavy grey granite building with columns and a sloping roof. That's the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum. And on the far side of the square, facing it, a much newer building with a pale stone base and a glowing green glass drum rising from it: the National Assembly.

The declaration, read by Ho Chi Minh, founded the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. He died in nineteen sixty nine. Work on the mausoleum started on the second of September nineteen seventy three, and it was opened in August nineteen seventy five. Its architect was the Soviet designer garol ee sakko vich, and it owes a clear debt to Lenin's tomb in Moscow, but look at the roof. It slopes, like a Vietnamese temple roof, and it softens the whole thing.

Have a look at the inscription at the top, in dark red stone, it's jade, from kow bung province, near where Ho Chi Minh lived when he first came back to Vietnam in nineteen forty one. It reads simply: choo tick Ho Chi Minh, President Ho Chi Minh. The guard at the door changes every hour. Ho Chi Minh himself had asked, in his will, to be cremated; the state decided otherwise.

Now find the National Assembly again. It's by the German practice gee em pee, and it was finished in twenty fourteen. The plan is a circle inside a square, heaven and earth, in traditional Vietnamese thinking. The circle is the debating chamber, a translucent jade green drum for nearly five hundred deputies. When they dug its foundations, they found the remains of the old royal city underneath.

So, two buildings, forty years apart, facing each other across the same lawn. One is built of grey granite to stand still forever. The other is built of glass, so you can see in. You can decide for yourself what each one is trying to say.

If you're here at nine in the evening, stay for the flag lowering ceremony. Otherwise, walk to the far side of the mausoleum: the yellow Presidential Palace and the stilt house where Ho Chi Minh actually lived are just beyond it.
