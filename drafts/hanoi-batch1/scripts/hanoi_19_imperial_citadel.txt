HANOI — 19 — IMPERIAL CITADEL OF THĂNG LONG
Single stop · Register: British (Atlas)
Vantage: in front of Đoan Môn, the south gate, inside the citadel grounds off Hoàng Diệu
Sources: Wikipedia (Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long) and its sources; Vietnam National Administration of Tourism ("An inside guide to the Thang Long Citadel"); Architecture Hunter Hanoi guide
---
In the year ten-ten, a king moved his capital here and gave it a name: Thăng Long. Ascending Dragon.

You're standing in front of Đoan Môn, the great south gate of the citadel — a long, solid wall of old brick and stone, with five arched gateways at the bottom and a two-storey watchtower on top. If you can see five arches side by side, you're in the right place.

Have a look at the arches. They're not all the same size, and that was the point. The tall one in the middle was for the emperor alone. The ones either side were for the royal family and high officials. Everyone else went another way. You could tell a man's rank by which hole in the wall he walked through.

The citadel was founded by King Lý Thái Tổ, and it was the seat of the Vietnamese court for most of the next eight centuries, until the capital moved south to Huế in seventeen eighty-nine. Most of what stood here was later demolished by the French. What survives is a scattering of pieces — this gate; the North Gate, which still has cannon damage on its face from the French attack of eighteen eighty-two; and, through the gate behind you, a pair of stone dragons flanking the steps of a palace that's no longer there.

Go through and find them. They're the steps of Kính Thiên Palace, carved in the fifteenth century, and they lead up to nothing but a platform and the sky.

And here's what makes this place unusual. Behind those dragon steps stands a plain, two-storey building called D67, built in nineteen sixty-seven. Underneath it is a bunker. During the American war, the Politburo and the army's high command met down there and directed the fighting. So one site holds the court of the Lý kings and the command room of the twentieth century, a few metres apart.

[beat]

That's why UNESCO made the citadel a World Heritage Site in twenty ten: not for any single building, but for the layers. A thousand years of Vietnamese power, all stacked on one patch of ground.

Before you leave, cross Hoàng Diệu Street to the excavation site at number eighteen. Walkways take you over the dug-up foundations of the old palaces, and there you can look straight down through the centuries.
