From up close, it's a big white block. From above, it's meant to be a lotus.

You're at the foot of the steps of the Ho Chi Minh Museum, just behind the Mausoleum. In front of you is a large, pale building of squared off forms, with a broad flight of stairs leading up into it. Close by is a tiny wooden temple on a single stone column: the One Pillar Pagoda. Keep it in mind.

The museum opened on the nineteenth of May nineteen ninety, the hundredth anniversary of Ho Chi Minh's birth. Ground was broken in nineteen eighty five. It was built with Soviet support, and the official account credits the design to the Russian architect garol ee sakko vich, the same architect who designed the Mausoleum, a few hundred metres away.

Have another look at the shape. The building is designed as a lotus flower in bloom, its forms opening outwards like petals. Guides sometimes call it the White Lotus. In Vietnam the lotus stands for purity: it grows out of the mud and stays clean. Here it's being used to say something about the man the museum is for.

Inside, the route moves in sequence: high, open, almost theatrical spaces, and eight chronological sections from his childhood in the eighteen nineties to his death in nineteen sixty nine, with more than a hundred and seventy thousand documents and objects.

Now turn to the One Pillar Pagoda. It was first built in ten forty nine, by a lee dynasty king, also in the form of a lotus rising from the water. The French destroyed it in nineteen fifty four, and it was rebuilt the following year.

So the museum is a quotation. A late Soviet building, designed by a foreign architect, borrowing the most Vietnamese shape there is from a nine hundred year old pagoda next door. Whether that's homage or appropriation is a question worth asking as you go up the steps.

Before you go in, walk over to the pagoda and look at it properly. It's small, a little crowded and rather perfect. Then look back at its giant cousin.
