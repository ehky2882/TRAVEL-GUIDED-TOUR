J W Marriott Hanoi

Most big hotels go up. This one lies down.

You're out in the new western side of Hanoi, a long way from the Old Quarter, on the edge of the artificial lake in front of the J W Marriott. Look back at the building. It's hard to take in all at once: a long, low, curving mass of glass and pale cladding, bending along the water, with sections that swing out over the ground below. Next door is the National Convention Centre; beyond it, the Hanoi Museum.

The architect was Carlos za pahta, whose studio is in New York, and the hotel opened in twenty thirteen. The brief he set himself was a reverse skyscraper, the floor space of a tower, laid on its side. So instead of fifty storeys stacked up, you get eight storeys stretched out, holding some four hundred and fifty rooms, a grand lobby, and a glass walled pool up on the roof.

Now look at the shape again, and let your eye run from one end to the other. It's meant to be a dragon. In Vietnamese myth the dragon is the ancestor of the people, and the city's old name, tang long, means ascending dragon. The building's long, sinuous body is also said to echo the line of the Vietnamese coast, a country that is itself long, thin and curving.

Have a look at the entrance, too. Guests arrive on a raised plaza, with the working levels tucked underneath, so the hotel seems to hover over its own forecourt rather than sit on it.

And here's the thing. For a thousand years, Hanoi's big statements were made in the old city, the citadel, the temples, the French boulevards. Out here, on what was farmland not long ago, the city is making new ones, and doing it in glass and steel rather than stone. Whether you find this one graceful or a little much, it's an honest picture of where the city is heading.

If you've got time, walk next door to the Hanoi Museum, the building shaped like an upside down pyramid. Stand between the two and look from one to the other. Two very different ideas of a modern Vietnamese landmark, a few hundred metres apart.
