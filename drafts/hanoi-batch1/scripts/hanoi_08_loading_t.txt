HANOI — 08 — LOADING T
Single stop · Register: British (Atlas)
Vantage: the gate of 8 Chân Cầm, a few metres from St Joseph's Cathedral
Sources: Architecture Hunter Hanoi guide; Table Journey; Evivatour (Hanoi vintage cafés)
Note: Architecture Hunter lists 15 Ngô Quyền (that is the Metropole's address). Loading T's map pin and every other source give 8 Chân Cầm.
---
One house, more than a dozen families, and a café that fits in two of the rooms.

You're on Chân Cầm, a short street just north of St Joseph's Cathedral, outside number eight. Behind the gate is a French colonial villa from around the nineteen thirties. There's a bakery on the ground floor. The café is upstairs: go through the gate and take the staircase on your left.

Before you climb, look at the whole house. It was built for one family — most likely a well-off merchant's, in the colonial years. Tall windows, green-grey shutters, small rounded balconies with patterned railings. It was meant to be lived in grandly.

After nineteen fifty-five, it wasn't. Like many villas in Hanoi, it was taken over by the new socialist government and divided up between more than a dozen households. Rooms became flats. Walls went up across the old reception spaces. And here's the curious thing: those partitions are still standing. The house never went back to being one house. Bit by bit, some of its pieces have simply become small businesses instead — and Loading T is one of them.

Upstairs, you'll see what that means. Two rooms, high ceilings, the original patterned floor tiles, weathered shutters, and furniture that doesn't match because it was never meant to. There's a little mezzanine, too, and a balcony that the café shares with everyone else at number eight. That's the part I love: you're having coffee on a communal landing.

Order something with cinnamon in it; that's their habit. The cinnamon egg coffee is the one people come back for.

[beat]

Most old houses tell you about the people who built them. This one tells you about everyone who came after — the merchant, then the families who split it into flats, then the people who opened it up for coffee. All three are still visible at once.

When you come down, walk the few steps to the cathedral square and look at St Joseph's from the side street. Then look back at number eight. The cathedral is the French city as it wanted to be seen. This house is what happened to it next.
