HANOI — 25 — CONCRETE FLAKES
Single stop · Register: British (Atlas)
Vantage: the lane at 21 Ngõ 2 Lê Văn Hưu, Hai Bà Trưng, looking up at the façade
Sources: Divisare (Concrete Flakes, G8A Architecture & Urban Planning, photographs by Hiroyuki Oki, 2026); Designboom (G8A, The Bridge, 2019); Architecture Hunter Hanoi guide
Note: Architecture Hunter describes it as a house; the architects describe it as an extension to OpenAsia's headquarters. It is a workplace, so the tour is written to be heard from the lane.
---
It looks unfinished. It's meant to.

You're in a narrow lane off Lê Văn Hưu Street, just south of the French Quarter, outside number twenty-one. Look up at the building in front of you. Raw grey concrete slabs and walls, stacked into a deep frame, with a fine layer of stainless-steel mesh in front, and openings cut through so the light and air get in behind it. It's called Concrete Flakes.

It was designed by the Vietnamese practice G8A Architecture and finished in twenty twenty-five. It's not a home. It's an extension to the headquarters of OpenAsia, a company whose offices G8A had already designed nearby, in a building called The Bridge.

Have a look at the structure. The concrete frame carries the building on the outside, like an exoskeleton, and the façade sits within it in layers. The idea is climate before style. In Hanoi's heat, a deep, shaded, perforated front keeps the direct sun off the glass, lets the breeze through, and turns hard daylight into something soft. The rough concrete is set against pale surfaces and bronze details inside.

The architects call their approach a "sustainable ruin". The thinking goes like this: build a strong, simple, honest frame, the sort of thing that could outlast what it's first used for, and let everything else inside it change over time. On each floor there's a shared kitchen at the entrance, the room where people actually meet. On the roof, there's a photography studio and a space for exercise and meditation.

And here's the thing. Most new buildings in this city try very hard to look finished, polished and new. This one starts out looking a little like a ruin on purpose, so that it has nowhere to fall from. It's an odd idea, and rather a clever one.

[beat]

It's a working office, so enjoy it from the lane. Then, if you're curious, look along the street. Every old house nearby has dealt with the same sun and the same rain using shutters, balconies and deep eaves. This is the same answer, given in concrete.
