noh troong yen

Some of the best cooking in Hanoi happens about a foot off the ground.

You're at the mouth of noh troong yen, one of the narrow alleys threaded through the Old Quarter. Look for the small shrine by the entrance, with an old see tree in front of it, a kind of banyan fig, roots and all. If you can see the shrine, the tree, and a row of plastic stools vanishing into the alley beyond, you're in the right place.

Hold on to that tree, because it's on the menu. One of the alley's best known lunch stalls is boon kah sum kay see, sum's fish noodles by the see tree. Rice noodles, fried fish, a light broth with tomato in it, and tables that jut out into the motorbikes. In Hanoi, a good stall doesn't need an address. A tree will do.

Now look along the alley itself. It changes shape through the day. In the morning it's a little market, vegetables, poultry, meat on low tables. By late morning the cooks have taken over. There's a duck noodle stall, boon ngan nyan, that you find by looking for the queue and the woman ladling soup out front; it's gone by mid afternoon. A squid and glass noodle place at number one keeps going through the lunch rush. And somewhere in here, of course, a pot of fuh baw, beef noodle soup, has been on since before you woke up.

Have a look above the food, too. Weathered facades, layers of signage, wiring, lanterns, motorbikes parked anywhere they'll fit.

And here's the thing. Nobody designed any of this. The stools, the awnings, the stall named after a tree, it's architecture made by hundreds of small decisions over a very long time, and it works better than most things that were planned. The furniture here costs a few pounds and gets stacked away every afternoon. The place has lasted centuries.

If you're hungry, the honest advice is simple: sit where there's a queue, and point. And afterwards, walk out the far end of the alley and turn towards hang back, Silver Street, where a few jewellers still work behind the shopfronts, as their guild has done for generations.
