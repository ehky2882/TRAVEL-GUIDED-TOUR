# HANOI — 18 — BA ĐÌNH SQUARE

*Single stop · Register: British (Atlas)*  
*Vantage: the edge of Ba Đình Square on Hùng Vương, facing the Mausoleum*  
*Sources: Wikipedia (Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum) and its sources; gmp Architekten / e-architect (Vietnamese National Assembly); Architecture Hunter Hanoi guide*  

---

On the second of September, nineteen forty-five, a man in a khaki suit stood on a wooden platform here and read out a declaration of independence.

You're at the edge of Ba Đình Square — a vast, flat lawn laid out in green squares, the most important open space in Vietnam. In front of you, across the grass, is a heavy grey granite building with columns and a sloping roof. That's the Hồ Chí Minh Mausoleum. And on the far side of the square, facing it, a much newer building with a pale stone base and a glowing green glass drum rising from it: the National Assembly.

The declaration, read by Hồ Chí Minh, founded the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. He died in nineteen sixty-nine. Work on the mausoleum started on the second of September nineteen seventy-three, and it was opened in August nineteen seventy-five. Its architect was the Soviet designer Garol Isakovich, and it owes a clear debt to Lenin's tomb in Moscow — but look at the roof. It slopes, like a Vietnamese temple roof, and it softens the whole thing.

Have a look at the inscription at the top, in dark red stone — it's jade, from Cao Bằng province, near where Hồ Chí Minh lived when he first came back to Vietnam in nineteen forty-one. It reads simply: Chủ tịch Hồ Chí Minh — President Hồ Chí Minh. The guard at the door changes every hour. Hồ Chí Minh himself had asked, in his will, to be cremated; the state decided otherwise.

Now find the National Assembly again. It's by the German practice gmp, and it was finished in twenty fourteen. The plan is a circle inside a square — heaven and earth, in traditional Vietnamese thinking. The circle is the debating chamber, a translucent jade-green drum for nearly five hundred deputies. When they dug its foundations, they found the remains of the old royal city underneath.

*[beat]*

So, two buildings, forty years apart, facing each other across the same lawn. One is built of grey granite to stand still forever. The other is built of glass, so you can see in. You can decide for yourself what each one is trying to say.

If you're here at nine in the evening, stay for the flag-lowering ceremony. Otherwise, walk to the far side of the mausoleum: the yellow Presidential Palace and the stilt house where Hồ Chí Minh actually lived are just beyond it.
