# HANOI — 17 — THE FRENCH QUARTER

*Single stop · Register: British (Atlas)*  
*Vantage: August Revolution Square, in front of the Hanoi Opera House, 1 Tràng Tiền*  
*Sources: Architecture Hunter Hanoi guide; Wikipedia (Hanoi Opera House; Ernest Hébrard); Hanoi city government ("Hà Nội, mirror of Indochinese architecture"); Rusty Compass on Hébrard's buildings*  
*Note: Architecture Hunter pins the French Quarter as a district. This tour anchors it at the Opera House.*  

---

Ten minutes' walk from the Old Quarter, and the city changes completely.

You're on the square in front of the Hanoi Opera House, at the top of Tràng Tiền Street. The building in front of you has columns, balconies and a grand flight of steps. Behind you, the streets run broad and straight, lined with big trees. That's the French Quarter.

The French took control of Hanoi in eighteen eighty-two, and they built their city here, to the south and east of the old one, on low, wet, swampy ground. Look around at the street pattern: wide boulevards, straight lines, villas set back in their gardens. It's the opposite of the tangle you've just come from. The idea was to build a European capital in Indochina.

The Opera House is that idea at its most confident. Construction started in nineteen-oh-one and finished in nineteen eleven, and it's modelled on the Palais Garnier in Paris — a smaller copy, in materials thought better suited to Hanoi. It was the centre of French cultural life in the colony. Then, on the nineteenth of August nineteen forty-five, a mass rally on this very square helped set off the August Revolution. The building made for colonial evenings out became the backdrop to the end of the colony.

Now, the weather. Hanoi is hot, humid and very wet, and a lot of what the French built here wasn't made for that. They adapted as best they could — deep verandas, shutters, high ceilings, and those enormous street trees for shade. Sometimes it worked; often it didn't.

And here's the interesting bit. In the nineteen twenties, an architect called Ernest Hébrard took charge of planning in Indochina and tried a different approach: borrow from Asian buildings instead of ignoring them. Wide sheltering roofs, layered eaves, local forms on a European plan. They called it the Indochine style.

*[beat]*

You can see it for yourself, just round the corner. Walk behind the Opera House to the National Museum of Vietnamese History, built in the nineteen twenties and thirties to Hébrard's design. Stand in front of it, then think back to the Opera House. One building copies Paris. The other is trying to belong here.
