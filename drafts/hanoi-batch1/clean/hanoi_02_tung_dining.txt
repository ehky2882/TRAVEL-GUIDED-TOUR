HANOI — 02 — T.U.N.G DINING
Single stop · Register: British (Atlas)
Vantage: outside 2C Quang Trung, a short walk south of St Joseph's Cathedral
Sources: Michelin Guide listing; The Dot Magazine; Epicure Vietnam; Vietcetera; Architecture Hunter Hanoi guide
---
Twenty-five seats. That's the whole of it.

You're on Quang Trung Street, outside number two C. There's very little to see from the pavement, and that's rather the point. The restaurant behind this door is T.U.N.G Dining — the letters stand for twisted, unique, natural and gastronomic, and they also happen to spell the chef's name.

Hoàng Tùng went to Finland to study business and came back a cook. In between, he worked his way through Nordic kitchens — the cold, precise, foraging school of cooking that made Copenhagen famous. He returned to Hanoi, won bronze at the Vietnamese selection for the Bocuse d'Or in twenty eighteen, and opened here that December, still in his early twenties.

Now, the door. Most restaurants hide the kitchen. This one walks you straight past it on the way in, so the first thing you see isn't a table but people working. After that the dining room goes quiet — cool colours, clean lines, almost Scandinavian in its restraint, and hushed in a way Hanoi very rarely is.

The food follows the same rule. A long tasting menu that changes every few months, and most dishes built from just three or four ingredients. Nordic technique; Vietnamese produce. The Michelin Guide recommends it, and the World's Fifty Best organisation has put it on its list of Asia's best hundred restaurants.

And here's the thing. When it opened, a tasting menu was a genuinely odd idea in Hanoi — a city that eats standing up, sitting low, and quickly. Tùng's bet was that the produce of northern Vietnam deserved the same slow, serious attention a Copenhagen kitchen would give a turnip. A good many kitchens in this city have followed where that bet led.

[beat]

Before you go, walk two minutes north to St Joseph's Cathedral, finished in eighteen eighty-six and looking very much like a smaller, sootier Notre-Dame. Stand in the square and look at the bell towers. You'll see why a chef who trained in the north felt rather at home round here.
