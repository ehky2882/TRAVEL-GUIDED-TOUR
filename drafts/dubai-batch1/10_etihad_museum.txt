Nations usually begin with a war. This one began with a signature — and you're standing a few steps from where the pen came down.

You should be at the western end of Jumeirah's beach road, where a white pavilion curves out of the ground like a sheet of paper caught mid-fold, with seven golden columns leaning out of it and an enormous flag flying beyond. The modest round building beside the pavilion is the reason all of this exists.

Find the flagpole first. It stands one hundred and twenty-three metres tall, and it marks the spot where the first flag of the United Arab Emirates was raised. On the second of December, 1971, as Britain wound up its treaties in the Gulf, the rulers of six emirates sat at a round table in that circular building — the Union House — and signed the declaration that made them one country. The seventh, Ras Al Khaimah, joined soon after. The room has been restored to exactly how it looked that morning, down to the chairs, and even the shoreline and gardens around you were relandscaped to match photographs from that day.

Now read the architecture of the newer building, because it's written in plain symbolism. The curved roof is the manuscript — the agreement itself, unfurled. The seven leaning columns are the pens that signed it. And most of the museum is underground, beneath the lawn: the story kept below, the signature celebrated above. As national monuments go, it's unusually literal, and better for it — and the gold catches the light differently every hour, so the manuscript appears to move.

Here's the thing to carry away, and it reframes every other stop in this city. The country you're standing in is younger than the Boeing 747. Everything you've seen or will see here — every tower, every man-made island, the entire skyline — postdates the piece of paper signed in that round room. Nineteen seventy-one is the hinge the whole place swings on: before it, a coastline of small trading towns; after it, this.
