The oldest building in this city of towers went up in 1787 — eleven years after the United States declared independence, and about two centuries before almost everything else you've seen today.

You should be outside Al Fahidi Fort, a low, sand-coloured stronghold with rounded and squared corner towers, old cannons resting by the walls, and a traditional wooden dhow displayed nearby. One honest note before anything else: the Dubai Museum housed inside has been closed for a long restoration, with no confirmed reopening date — so today, the walls are the exhibit. As it happens, they're a good one.

Circle it slowly. The fort is built largely of coral stone — blocks cut from the seabed itself, bound with gypsum, the same materials as the merchant houses nearby but stacked for defence rather than comfort. Look closely at any weathered patch of wall and you can sometimes make out the texture of the coral. This building is the sea, rearranged.

Then consider what it held. For most of its life, Al Fahidi Fort wasn't a fort the way Europe means the word — one military installation among a state's many buildings. It was the state. Ruler's residence at times, garrison, armoury, storehouse and jail, all inside one walled square you can walk around in a couple of minutes. When this was everything Dubai had, everything Dubai was fit inside it. The government of a town of a few thousand people, in one building smaller than a modern supermarket.

[beat]

That's the measurement worth taking away. Stand at any corner of the fort and look up: somewhere beyond the rooftops is a skyline with the tallest structure on earth in it. The entire distance between that and this — between a coral-walled square and a city of millions — was covered in a handful of generations. Most cities take a thousand years to grow that far. This one did it inside living memory, and a few of the people who remember the fort as the edge of town are still around to say so.

When the museum inside reopens, it will be worth your time. Until then, the outside tells the story well enough: small walls, enormous distance.
