One of the most photographed buildings on earth is best seen, free of charge, from a towel.

You should be on the sand at Kite Beach in Umm Suqeim, with the running track and the food kiosks behind you, kites carving over the water — and away down the shoreline, standing alone against the sky, the sail of the Burj Al Arab.

Walk to the waterline. The hotel rises from its own man-made island a few hundred metres offshore, connected to the coast by a private causeway, and it was finished in the late nineteen nineties with one explicit job: to put Dubai on the world's map. It worked. The shape is a sail at full stretch — a deliberate echo of the dhows that built the coast's first fortunes — and the famous "seven-star" label was coined by a visiting journalist. The hotel has never once claimed it, and has spent decades politely declining to. What it will own is the tennis court: the helipad near the top once hosted an exhibition match between two of the sport's greats, several hundred metres above the sea.

Now turn around and take in the beach itself, because this stop is really about the ground you're standing on. The track behind you runs some fourteen kilometres along the coast, and it's busy from first light — runners, cyclists, the whole city in trainers. The wind steadies in the afternoon, which is when the kitesurfers own the water. Food trucks, volleyball, showers, no ticket, no gate — this is where the city actually exercises. The light is kindest early and late, and so is the temperature.

And that's the quiet point of the place. This city runs a thriving trade in exclusive shorelines — beach clubs, resort sand, day passes. Yet its single most exclusive silhouette, the hotel most people will never enter, belongs visually to anyone with a towel and an afternoon. The best seat for Dubai's most private building is its most public beach.

Stay for the end of the day if you can. The kites come down, the runners come out, and the sail turns to silhouette — the famous view, at no charge.
