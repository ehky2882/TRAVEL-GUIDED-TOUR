From where you're standing, the whole argument of this city fits inside one rectangle.

You should be in Zabeel Park, looking up at two gold towers joined by a bridge across the top — a picture frame fifteen storeys tall, one hundred and fifty metres, faced in roughly fifteen thousand square metres of gold-toned steel. It holds the official record as the largest picture frame in the world, which is the most Dubai sentence in this entire tour.

Line up the view first, because the frame is aimed. Stand so you're looking through it toward the north, and the rectangle holds the old city — Deira, the creek quarters, the low rooftops where everything started. Walk around and look through it the other way, and it holds the new one: the towers of Sheikh Zayed Road marching off toward the Burj Khalifa. The structure was placed on this exact spot to do that — to hang both halves of Dubai on the same wall, in the same size window, with no favourite.

You can ride to the top, where a glass-floored bridge runs between the towers and the view does both directions at once. But the object works from the ground, which is rarer than it sounds — plenty of structures give you a view, and very few compose one.

One more layer, quietly. The design began as the winning entry in an international competition, and the architect who won it spent years afterwards in dispute over whether the built version was still rightfully his — a question the gold cladding declines to answer.

Here's what makes the Frame worth the detour, though. Every other monument in this city was built to be looked at. This is the only one built for looking — a viewing device the size of a tower block, pointed at the city that made it. Dubai spent fifty years constructing a skyline, and then built itself a way to see the whole thing at once.

[beat]

Come in the last hour of daylight if you can choose your moment. The cladding goes from gold to fire, and for a few minutes the frame outshines everything it frames.
