This is the best seat in Dubai for looking at a building you're probably not staying in.

You should be inside Madinat Jumeirah, among the waterways — stone walkways, arched bridges, palm shade, wooden boats sliding along canals — with the sail of the Burj Al Arab rising at the end of the water.

Find a bridge and stop in the middle. The view down the canal is not an accident: the whole resort was built in the early two thousands as a piece of old Arabia at luxury scale — lanes, lanterns, wind towers — and its waterways were dug and aimed so that walkway after walkway, bridge after bridge, frames the Burj Al Arab down a long reflective channel. The hotel next door is the icon. This place is the tripod. Even the bridges arch higher than foot traffic strictly needs, because a higher bridge makes a better frame.

Wander into the souk when you're ready — dozens of small shops under carved wooden ceilings, lantern light, waterfront cafés — and watch the boats as you go. They're abras, the same wooden craft the creek has used as a water bus for a century, re-employed here as gondolas: slower, scripted, and gliding past dinner tables instead of dhow cargo. It's the most honest borrowing in the resort, and somehow the most surreal. Prices run resort-grade and nobody pretends otherwise; you are paying for the setting, which at least has the virtue of being spectacular.

Here's the thing to notice, though, and it's the reason this stop earns its place. The Burj Al Arab is free to look at — from the public beach up the coast, from the road, from half the city. What Madinat sells is the foreground: the water, the arches, the palm fronds, the composed shot. Dubai understood earlier than most cities that a view is a product with two halves — and that if the icon already exists, you can build the other half, and charge for the table beside it.

Come towards evening if you can choose. The lanterns come on, the canals go gold, and the sail catches the last light — a postcard assembling itself around you.
