DUBAI 21 — JBR THE BEACH (single stop)

Look past the umbrellas and up at the towers, and you will spot the detail that changes this beach: balconies. Hundreds of them, stacked forty storeys high, with plants and deck chairs and towels drying over the railings. People live here. The postcard has tenants.

You should be on the sand or the seafront promenade at JBR — Jumeirah Beach Residence — with the Gulf on one side and a curved wall of around forty residential towers on the other. Offshore, on its own man-made island, stands a great white observation wheel. And running behind the towers is The Walk, a street of shops and cafés nearly two kilometres long.

Turn your back to the sea for a moment. The Walk was one of Dubai's earliest experiments in having a street at all — a city assembled around highways and interiors deciding, for once, to build somewhere you stroll, window-shop, and sit outside watching other people do the same. Between the street and the sand sits The Beach, an open-air cluster that works like a mall with the roof left off: dozens of restaurants and shops arranged around lawns and an outdoor cinema screen.

Now the sand itself. It is free, it is lifeguarded, it is groomed daily, and parts of it never really close — you can swim under floodlights long after dark. The wheel offshore is Ain Dubai, two hundred and fifty metres to the top, the tallest observation wheel ever built. Whether it happens to be turning on the day you visit has become one of the city's small lotteries, but as an object on the horizon it earns its place.

When these towers went up in the two thousands, this was among the largest residential projects ever attempted in a single go — an entire seafront neighbourhood delivered as one gesture. Kite Beach, up the coast, is where the city goes to play. This is where a slice of the city actually lives, and the difference is visible: the beach below is a holiday, but the balconies above are a Tuesday.

Find a spot where you can hold both in view — the swimmers on the sand and the deck chairs in the sky. Most cities keep their postcards and their addresses separate. This one stacked them.
