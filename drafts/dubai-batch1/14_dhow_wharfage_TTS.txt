Somewhere on this quay, a refrigerator is waiting to cross the sea on a wooden boat.

You should be on bah nee yas Road, on the day rah bank of the creek, where the water is lined for hundreds of metres with wooden cargo dows — moored two and three deep in places, hulls painted blue and white and rust-red, sterns rising high and square like sideboards. The quay in front of them is stacked with freight in the open air: shrink-wrapped appliances, towers of tyres, boxed televisions, sacks and crates addressed in three alphabets.

Pick one boat and stay with it. Watch how it loads — by hand and by small crane, cargo walked up a plank and lashed down in layers until the deck disappears. The crews live aboard, and the boats say so: laundry drying on a line rigged from the mast, cooking pots by the wheelhouse, a man asleep in the one patch of shade. Some of these hulls are decades old, built by shipwrights working from memory rather than blueprints, and they run to Iran, to oh mahn, down to India and East Africa — the same short-sea routes this creek has served for well over a century, now carrying flat-screens where the pearls and the dates used to go.

And notice that none of it is staged for you. There is no ticket booth and no interpretive panel, because the trade simply hasn't stopped. The largest dows moved years ago to a bigger facility near the creek mouth, and the quay under your feet has been freshly rebuilt — the city keeps investing in a business some people assumed it would quietly outgrow.

Here's what's worth standing still for. Everything the creek started — the ports, the towers, the man-made islands — grew up and moved out. This stretch is the part that never left. The city's oldest business model, buy it here and sail it there, still runs on wood and rope within sight of the metro line.

Stay for one departure if you can. A loaded dow easing out into the channel, riding low, is the old city leaving for work — the way it has every working day since long before anyone thought to watch.
