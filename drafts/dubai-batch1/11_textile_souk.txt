Every city used to shop like this — under one long roof, in the shade, at talking distance. Most cities stopped. This one kept the arcade and simply built the malls elsewhere.

You should be in the Textile Souk in Bur Dubai, under the restored wooden arcade that runs back from the abra station. High timber roof, carved brackets, a straight shaded street of small shops with their goods spilling out to meet you: silk, cotton, pashmina, sequinned fabric by the bolt, scarves in every colour that exists and several that shouldn't.

Step in under the roof and slow down to souk pace. The shopkeepers will greet you — that's not pressure, that's the format. Commerce here is conversational and always has been: you're expected to touch the cloth, ask the price, look unconvinced, and hear a better one. Nobody is offended by a polite no. The haggle isn't a trick played on tourists; it's the original price mechanism of every port on this ocean, still running.

And a port is exactly what you're standing in. For generations, wooden dhows crossed from India and beyond with cloth in their holds, and this arcade is where that cargo met its customers — the retail end of a trade route measured in monsoons. The tailors in the surrounding streets are part of the same system: buy the fabric here in the morning, collect the finished garment before your ship — or these days, your flight — leaves. The goods on the shelves have changed with the centuries. The geometry hasn't: boats at one end, bargaining at the other, shade over everything.

[beat]

That's what a souk really is, and why the word doesn't quite translate to "market." It's infrastructure for conversation — a street engineered so that strangers from different ends of the sea can stand comfortably in the shade long enough to reach a price.

Walk the arcade end to end once without buying anything, just to learn its length. Then, if a bolt of blue silk has been quietly following you the whole way — and one usually has — go back and begin the conversation.
