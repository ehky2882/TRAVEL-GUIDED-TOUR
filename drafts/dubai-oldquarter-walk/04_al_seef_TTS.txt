Here are the wind towers again — the same sand-coloured walls, the same timber shutters, the same lanes you just left. Except every stone of this was built in the last ten years.

You're on the al seef waterfront, the third kind of old, and the walk's final exhibit. Look closely and the tells emerge: the plaster is distressed with suspicious evenness, the weathering repeats, and behind one heritage facade you'll find a coffee chain with a menu in six languages. The buildings were designed aged — constructed new and antiqued by design, the way a furniture maker distresses a table. Then look up the promenade and let the view argue back: dows on the water, towers behind, families out walking in the evening. As a piece of city, whatever else it is, it works.

So finish the walk by settling the question it has been asking since shin dah gah. You've now seen the restored, the genuine, and the invented, all inside two hours. The restored teaches. The genuine survives. The invented — this — is the strangest and maybe the most honest of the three, because it admits something the other two can't: that this city loves its past the way it loves everything else, as a project. Old Dubai isn't a place that was left behind here. It's a place the city keeps actively deciding to have.

Find a seat by the water. The creek in front of you is the same water you stood over at the mouth this morning, still working, still carrying boats. Three kinds of old on one bank — and out there, the original article, older than all of them, not pretending to be anything.

That's the walk. Stay for the light.
