Inside the lanes now, the city goes quiet. Walls close to shoulder height on either side, sand-coloured plaster, and above the rooflines, the square towers this quarter is famous for — wind towers, the old architecture of catching a breeze.

The name is the story here. Before this was the al fah hee dee Historical Neighbourhood, everyone called it bah stah kee yah — after bastak, a town across the water in Persia. The merchants who built these houses a century ago came from there, drawn by a ruler who had abolished trade taxes and was openly collecting other cities' businessmen. They arrived with capital, built in the style of home, and gave the quarter its name, its towers, and its courtyards. What you're walking through is not just old Dubai. It's the built evidence that this city was recruiting immigrants before it had electricity.

Wander without a plan — the lanes are short and you can't stay lost for long. The houses hold small galleries, cafes, and cultural centres now, and most doorways are open; step into any courtyard and you'll find the same arrangement of shade, still air, and rooms turned inward around it. Notice how few windows face the lane. Wealth here looked inward. The street got a wall; the family got the sky.

This is the genuine old at its most complete — a whole quarter of it, kept. Hold the texture of these lanes in your mind, because you're about to see the same architecture again, ten minutes away, with one profound difference.

Follow any lane south and downhill toward the water, and when you emerge onto the waterfront, turn right. The promenade ahead, with its wind towers and timber balconies, is al seef — our last stop.
