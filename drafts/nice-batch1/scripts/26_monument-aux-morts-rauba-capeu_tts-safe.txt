Mon oo mon oh mor duh rowba kapeyoo

The wind on this corner is so strong that the locals named it after it. Rowba kapeyoo means steal the hat.

You should be on the kay rowba kapeyoo, at the eastern end of the Promenade, where the road swings round the foot of the Castle Hill. On the cliff side, set into the rock, is a tall arched niche with a great stone urn inside it, framed by carved reliefs. That's Neece's memorial to the First World War.

It was designed by a nee swah architect, ro zhay say ah sal, who had won the pree duh rom, the top award for a French architect, in nineteen thirteen. Work began in nineteen twenty four, and the monument was inaugurated in nineteen twenty eight by marshal fosh, who'd commanded the Allied armies at the end of the war.

The urn is the centre of it. On it are thousands of nameplates, one for each of the men of Neece who died, more than three and a half thousand of them. On either side are two high reliefs by the sculptor alfred zhan yo. One shows war. The other shows peace. With everything around it, the whole composition rises over thirty metres up the rock face.

Here's a curious footnote. In twenty eighteen, during work on the monument, a hidden reliquary was found inside it, shaped like an eagle, holding plaques with around two thousand more names. Nobody knew it was there. No record mentioned it.

What I find quietly extraordinary is the choice of place. say ah sal didn't put the monument in a square. He set it into the oldest rock in the city, the hill Neece was born on, looking out to sea.

Before you leave, look at the base. There's a sundial inscribed into it. Then step out to the sea wall and look along the bay. It's the classic view of Neece, and you'll probably have to hold on to your hat.
