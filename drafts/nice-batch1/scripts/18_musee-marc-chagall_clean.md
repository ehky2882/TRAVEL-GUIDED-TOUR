# Musée National Marc Chagall

This is a rare thing, a museum designed around an artist while he was still alive to argue about it.

You should be on Avenue du Docteur Ménard, on the lower slopes of Cimiez, at the entrance to a low white building half-hidden in a garden. You'll notice that the planting is mostly in blue and white. That's not an accident.

In nineteen sixty-nine the French culture minister, André Malraux, decided to build a home for a gift. Marc Chagall had given the nation seventeen huge paintings on stories from the Bible, called the Biblical Message. The architect André Hermant designed the building to hold them, and the museum opened in nineteen seventy-three. Chagall came to the opening himself.

Look at the architecture first. It's sober and low, pale stone and glass, more like a modern villa than a monument. It sits in a Mediterranean garden designed by Henri Fisch, who chose, with Chagall's agreement, cool colours and white and blue flowers, so that the outside prepares your eye for the inside.

Then there's the gift detail, and it's inside the auditorium. Chagall designed the stained-glass windows for that room himself, working with the master glassmaker Charles Marcq. They tell the creation of the world, in deep blues, and when the sun comes through, the whole concert hall turns the colour of the sea. He also made the large mosaic that you'll see reflected in the pool outside.

[beat]

What I find rather moving is that this isn't a museum about Chagall's career. It's a museum built for one idea he had, and he was able to shape every bit of it, down to the flowers. Most artists get a building after they're gone. He got to choose the colour of the garden.

If you've got the energy afterwards, keep walking up the hill to Cimiez. Twenty minutes or so brings you to the Musée Matisse, among the olive trees. Two of the century's great colourists, on the same hillside.
