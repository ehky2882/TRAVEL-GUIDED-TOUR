# Moolan ah weel doleev al zee ah ree

Look for the blue and yellow tins. They've become the unofficial flag of nee swah olive oil.

You should be on roo san fron swah duh pole, a few steps from the opera house. The little shop with its shelves of painted tins is al zee ah ree. If the door's open, you'll smell it before you see it.

The business goes back to eighteen sixty eight, when a man named say zar mar tan bought a ruined old mill in the mad lenn district, in the hills on the west of the city, and rebuilt it. In nineteen hundred his son in law, nee ko lah al zee ah ree, took it over and gave it his name. The mill is still working today, the oldest of its kind still running in Neece.

The shop came later. Nicolas's daughter Pauline opened it here at some point between the wars, and it's been the house's best known address ever since. The tins came with it, in that azure blue and sunflower yellow, decorated in the pro von sal style.

The local olive is called the kai uh tyay, small and dark, and it's the only variety grown in this area. It's also the olive that ends up, brined and black, in a salade nee swahz.

And here's the gift detail. nee ko lah al zee ah ree's idea was to blend, to mix oils from different olives to get a flavour he liked, rather than rely on any single one. That's why a tin from here never tastes quite like anybody else's. A family recipe for oil, of all things, kept going for more than a century.

When you've bought your tin, walk on to the koor sah lay ah, a few minutes away. In autumn and winter, look out for the market stalls selling the season's olives. That's where all of this begins.
