Maison Auer

The fruit in this window has been soaking in sugar for weeks.

You should be on Rue Saint-François-de-Paule, directly opposite the opera house, at the western edge of the old town. Look for the old shopfront with the name Auer above it, and behind the glass, rows of fruit that shine as if they've been lacquered. They have, in a way.

The house goes back to eighteen twenty. Its founder, Henri Auer, was a Swiss confectioner who came to the Côte d'Azur and was dazzled by the fruit, the oranges, lemons, figs and apricots that grew here almost without trying. His son Henri-Chrétien took over in eighteen ninety and made the family's candied fruit famous far beyond the city. Five generations of the family ran it after that.

The candying is the gift detail. A piece of fruit is soaked in syrup that's made stronger, little by little, over weeks, until the sugar has replaced the water inside it. A whole clementine done this way glows like amber, and keeps for months.

Step inside if you can. The interior is in a Florentine style, with gilded counters and mirrors, and it has barely changed. It's less a shop than a jewellery box, and it's listed as part of the city's heritage.

[beat]

Here's what's worth knowing. Before refrigeration, candying was how you kept summer. Fruit that would rot in days could be eaten at Christmas, posted across Europe, served to kings. What looks like a treat in the window was once one of the cleverest bits of food technology this coast had.

When you leave, look across the street at the opera house. Then walk a few doors along the same street to Alziari, the olive-oil shop. Between them, these two shops have been selling the flavours of this coast for well over three hundred years combined.
