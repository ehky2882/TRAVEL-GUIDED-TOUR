Hôtel Windsor

Nobody planned for this hotel to become an art gallery. It happened one room at a time.

You should be on Rue Dalpozzo, a quiet street a couple of blocks back from the Promenade des Anglais. The building in front of you, number eleven, is a nineteenth-century building in what the hotel calls the Eiffel School style. That's the Windsor.

The Redolfi family has run it for around eighty years. In nineteen eighty-nine, Bernard Redolfi did something no sensible hotelier would do. He handed one room, number eighty-three, to the artist Joël Ducoroy and let him do what he liked with it. Then he did it again. And again.

Today most of the rooms are the work of a different contemporary artist, and no two are alike. Some of the names will be familiar if you know French art. Ben, the Nice artist famous for his handwritten slogans, is here. So is the British cartoonist Glen Baxter, and the video artist Pierrick Sorin. Every year a new artist takes over the lobby.

The other surprise is behind the building. There's a tropical garden, thick with palms and flowering plants, a heated pool, and a giant rubber tree that breakfast is served under in summer. At night, an installation by Mauro Benetti called La Luna is reflected in the water.

[beat]

What I find rather lovely about the Windsor is that it's the opposite of the grand seafront palaces a few streets away. They spent fortunes to look impressive. This one spent its imagination instead, and it has ended up with a collection a small museum would envy.

If you can, step into the bar for a drink. The installation in there is called Bar barrit, by Nicolas Rubinstein, and it's the quickest way to see what the fuss is about.
