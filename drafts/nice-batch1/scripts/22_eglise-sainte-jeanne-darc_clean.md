# Église Sainte-Jeanne-d'Arc

Nice calls it the meringue. Once you've seen it, you'll understand why.

You should be on Avenue Saint-Lambert, in the residential streets north of the railway station. In front of you is a church that looks like nothing else in the city, a white mass of rounded, egg-shaped domes, and beside it a tall, thin bell tower that rises more than sixty metres.

It was built by the architect Jacques Droz. The foundations had been laid before the First World War, but the church you're looking at was started in nineteen twenty-six and finished in nineteen thirty-three. It's reinforced concrete, poured as a thin shell, three great oval domes and eight smaller ones, swelling out of each other like rising bread.

That was a bold thing to do with a church in the nineteen-twenties. Concrete was the material of bridges and factories, not of places of worship. Droz used it because it let him make shapes that stone simply couldn't. Inside, the domes give you a vast open space with no columns getting in the way, and the walls carry frescoes by the Russian painter Eugène Klementieff.

[beat]

And here's the thing. For decades, Nice's architectural reputation rested on the old town and the seafront palaces. This church spent much of the twentieth century being called odd. In nineteen ninety-two it was listed as a historic monument. The odd building turned out to be one of the most original things the city ever built.

If the door's open, go in. Stand under the central dome and look up. The light coming through the shell is the best argument for concrete you'll ever see. And if you're heading back into town, Cimiez is uphill to the east, for the Matisse and Chagall museums.
