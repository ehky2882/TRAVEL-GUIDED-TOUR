=== 01 | hotel-le-negresco | Hôtel Le Negresco | Oh tel luh neh gress ko
The man who built this hotel trained as a sweet-maker.

Stand on the sea side of the Promenade des Anglais and look inland. The long white wedding cake of a building in front of you is the Negresco. You'll know it by the dome on its corner, pink, with a green cap, and by the doormen out front in eighteenth-century livery and hats with red plumes.

Henri Negresco was born Alexandru Negrescu, the son of a Romanian innkeeper. He trained as a confectioner in Bucharest, worked his way across Paris and the Riviera, and ended up running the municipal casino here. Then he did what every hotel manager dreams of. He built his own. It opened just before the First World War, designed by Édouard-Jean Niermans, the architect of choice for Belle Époque pleasure palaces, and Niermans gave it that dome.

Here's the bit the brochures sometimes get wrong. You may hear that the great glass roof inside was made by Gustave Eiffel. It wasn't. Eiffel never worked here. The building is Niermans from top to bottom.

Inside, under that roof, hangs a Baccarat chandelier of more than sixteen thousand crystals. It was ordered by Tsar Nicholas the Second. The Russian Revolution got in the way, the Tsar never took delivery, and it ended up here instead.

[beat]

And that's rather the story of the whole building. Within a couple of years of opening, the war turned it into a hospital, the rich stopped coming, and Negresco lost the lot. He died in Paris at fifty-two. The hotel he dreamed up has outlived him by more than a century, and since nineteen fifty-seven, when the Augier family bought it, it has filled up with paintings and sculpture as if it were a private museum with beds.

If you've got a few minutes, and you're dressed for it, ask at the door about seeing the Salon Royal. The chandelier is worth the detour. Then come back out and look along the seafront at the dome once more. It's still the most recognisable roofline on the whole Baie des Anges.

=== 02 | hotel-la-perouse | Hôtel La Pérouse | Oh tel lah pay rooz
This hotel doesn't sit at the foot of the cliff. It's built into it.

Find yourself on the Quai Rauba Capèu, at the far eastern end of the bay, where the Promenade des Anglais runs out of room and the Castle Hill drops straight into the sea. The building pressed against the rock face in front of you is La Pérouse. Look up from the entrance and you'll see that the hotel climbs the hill in terraces, the bare limestone showing between them.

The name comes from Jean-François de La Pérouse, the eighteenth-century French navigator who sailed off to map the Pacific and was never seen again. Fitting, for a building that spends its whole life staring at the horizon. The hotel says it has been carved into this rock since nineteen thirty-six.

Two details make the place. The first is the garden. Behind the front door there's a patio planted with lemon trees, some of them a century old, with jasmine climbing the walls. In the middle of a city it smells like a farmhouse in the hills. The second is the pool, which is cut into the rock itself, so you swim with the cliff at your back and the whole bay in front of you.

What I love about this spot is the trick of it. From the Promenade, this end of the bay looks like a dead end, all rock and no room. Somebody looked at that cliff and saw a hotel. Climb high enough and every window has the view that the rest of the seafront pays a fortune for.

[beat]

From here, you don't need to be a guest to get the view. Carry on along the quay for a minute, past the war memorial cut into the same cliff, and take the steps or the lift up the Castle Hill. At the top, look back down at La Pérouse. From above, you'll finally see how much of it is hiding in the rock.

=== 03 | hotel-du-couvent | Hôtel du Couvent | Oh tel doo koo von
For nearly four hundred years, this was a place for people who'd given up the world. Now it's a hotel.

You should be in the upper part of the old town, at the corner of Rue Honoré Ugo, where the lanes start climbing towards the Castle Hill. The long building with the old stone walls and shuttered windows is the Hôtel du Couvent. Look for the terraces rising up the slope behind it, green with trees.

It was built as a convent in sixteen oh four, by nuns of the Order of Saint Clare. In the early nineteenth century the Visitation sisters moved in, and they stayed until the early nineteen-eighties. 

The man who brought it back, Valéry Grégo, spent roughly ten years on it, and the approach is what makes it worth a stop. The architects, Studio Mumbai with Studio Méditerranée, called it frugal. Timber and stone found on the site were reused. Some of the desks in the rooms are made from four-hundred-year-old beams pulled out of the convent's own roof.

Then there's the water. Up the hill in Cimiez are the ruins of the Roman baths of the ancient town of Cemenelum. The hotel has built its own version, a run of pools that get warmer as you go, fed down through terraced gardens with more than three hundred kinds of plant.

[beat]

So the convent ends up doing very nearly what it always did. It grows its own herbs, bakes its own bread with flour milled on site, and sends most guests away a bit calmer than they arrived. The vows have gone. The rhythm hasn't.

If you're passing on a Saturday morning, look in. There's a farmers' market in the courtyard, and it's the easiest way to see the cloister without booking a room.

=== 04 | hotel-windsor | Hôtel Windsor | Oh tel windsor
Nobody planned for this hotel to become an art gallery. It happened one room at a time.

You should be on Rue Dalpozzo, a quiet street a couple of blocks back from the Promenade des Anglais. The building in front of you, number eleven, is a nineteenth-century building in what the hotel calls the Eiffel School style. That's the Windsor.

The Redolfi family has run it for around eighty years. In nineteen eighty-nine, Bernard Redolfi did something no sensible hotelier would do. He handed one room, number eighty-three, to the artist Joël Ducoroy and let him do what he liked with it. Then he did it again. And again.

Today most of the rooms are the work of a different contemporary artist, and no two are alike. Some of the names will be familiar if you know French art. Ben, the Nice artist famous for his handwritten slogans, is here. So is the British cartoonist Glen Baxter, and the video artist Pierrick Sorin. Every year a new artist takes over the lobby.

The other surprise is behind the building. There's a tropical garden, thick with palms and flowering plants, a heated pool, and a giant rubber tree that breakfast is served under in summer. At night, an installation by Mauro Benetti called La Luna is reflected in the water.

[beat]

What I find rather lovely about the Windsor is that it's the opposite of the grand seafront palaces a few streets away. They spent fortunes to look impressive. This one spent its imagination instead, and it has ended up with a collection a small museum would envy.

If you can, step into the bar for a drink. The installation in there is called Bar barrit, by Nicolas Rubinstein, and it's the quickest way to see what the fuss is about.

=== 05 | chez-pipo | Chez Pipo | Shay pee po
Chickpea flour, water, olive oil and salt. That's the whole recipe, and people queue for it.

You should be on Rue Bavastro, a narrow street running up from the old port towards Place Garibaldi. Look for the low frontage with the name Chez Pipo above the door, and very likely a queue outside it. Peer through the window, and at the back you'll see the oven glowing.

The dish is socca, Nice's chickpea pancake. The batter is poured thin into enormous round copper trays and pushed into a wood-fired oven running at around three hundred degrees. A few minutes later it comes out blistered and charred at the edges, still soft in the middle, and is cut into rough pieces with a scraper. You eat it with your fingers and plenty of black pepper.

Chez Pipo opened in nineteen twenty-three, named after its founder, and it's been doing more or less the same thing ever since. The oven is the heart of the room, and you can see it from the tables, which are long and shared, so you'll probably end up eating elbow to elbow with a stranger.

Here's what's worth knowing. Socca began as working food, cheap, filling and eaten with your hands, not something you'd sit down to in a dining room. This place is one of the very few that has kept it exactly that simple.

[beat]

Which is rather the point of coming. Plenty of restaurants in Nice will sell you a plate of local tradition with a tablecloth underneath. Here, the tradition is the plate, and the tablecloth never made it.

If you'd like the full set, order a slice of pissaladière too, the onion tart with anchovies and black olives. Then walk five minutes down to the port and eat the rest looking at the boats.

=== 06 | la-merenda | La Merenda | Lah meh ren dah
The chef at this tiny restaurant gave up two Michelin stars to cook here.

You should be on Rue Raoul Bosio, a short street at the western edge of the old town, a couple of minutes from the Opéra. La Merenda is the small dining room with the plain shopfront. There's not much to see from outside, and that's rather deliberate. There's no telephone, either. If you want a table, you come here in person and book it.

The name is Niçois for a snack, a workman's bite to eat. It opened in nineteen sixty-six as a family bistro. Then, in nineteen ninety-six, Dominique Le Stanc bought it. He'd been running the restaurant at the Negresco, with two Michelin stars to his name, and he walked away from all of it for a room with around twenty seats and diners perched on stools.

The cooking is Niçois home food, done with a grand chef's precision. Fried courgette flowers. Stuffed sardines. Salt cod, and tripe in the old way. And the gift detail is the chef himself. Le Stanc has long been known for riding his old bicycle to the market every morning to choose what he'll cook that day.

[beat]

Here's the thing that's worth thinking about. Every serious chef eventually has to decide what all the training is for. Le Stanc decided it was for this, for cooking the food of the city properly, for whoever turned up in person to ask for a table.

If it's closed, or you can't get in, walk two minutes south to the Cours Saleya market and look at what's on the stalls. The courgette flowers in season will tell you exactly where this kitchen does its shopping.

=== 07 | chez-acchiardo | Chez Acchiardo | Shay ah kyar doh
The same family has been cooking in this room since nineteen twenty-seven.

You should be about halfway along Rue Droite, the narrow lane that runs through the heart of the old town. Look for number thirty-eight, a plain old shopfront with the name Acchiardo over the door and, at lunchtime, the clatter of a busy dining room coming out of it.

The family came to Nice from the Val Maira, a valley in the mountains of Piedmont, on the Italian side of the Alps. That isn't as far-fetched as it sounds. For most of its history Nice wasn't French at all. It belonged to the House of Savoy, ruled from Turin, and only joined France in eighteen sixty. Families moved down from those valleys to the coast for generations, and they brought their cooking with them.

Four generations on, the menu is still the food of that world. Daube, the beef stewed for hours in wine. Gnocchi. Stuffed vegetables, the Niçois way. Tripe, for the brave. And red mullet with tapenade, the paste of black olives and capers that tastes of this whole coast.

The restaurant carries the Cuisine Nissarde label, which the city gives to places cooking the genuine local repertoire. But the label is rather beside the point. Nobody here needs telling.

[beat]

What I like about Acchiardo is that it isn't preserving anything. It simply never stopped. While the old town around it filled up with souvenir shops and gelato counters, this room went on serving lunch to people who live here.

Before you go, walk north along Rue Droite for a minute or so, and you'll come to the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century palace squeezed into the same lane. It's a reminder that this narrow street was once the smartest address in town.

=== 08 | peixes | Peixes | Pay shesh
In a city famous for cooked-down stews and slow braises, this place hardly cooks its fish at all.

You should be on Rue de l'Opéra, on the western edge of the old town, a few steps from the opera house. Look for a small, bright dining room tiled in white and nautical blue. That's Peixes.

The name is Portuguese for fish, and it opened in twenty fifteen with a simple, slightly radical idea for Nice. Raw fish. The menu is built around ceviche and tiradito, two South American ways of preparing it. Ceviche is diced fish cured in citrus juice until it firms up. Tiradito is sliced thin, more like sashimi, and dressed just before it reaches you. Here it's done with Mediterranean fish and Mediterranean flavours.

That combination is the clever part. Nice has always had wonderful fish at the market. What it hadn't really had was a local restaurant that trusted the fish to need almost nothing done to it. Peixes took a technique from Peru and gave it a local accent, and the Michelin guide has noticed.

[beat]

And here's why it belongs in a guide to this city. Nice has always been a crossroads, Italian and French, mountain and sea. The food has absorbed everything that came through the port. A ceviche bar by the opera house isn't a break with that tradition. It's the latest chapter of it.

If you fancy seeing where the fish comes from, the fish market on Place Saint-François, a ten-minute walk into the old town, runs most mornings. Go early. By lunchtime the best of it is already on plates like the ones here.

=== 09 | le-plongeoir | Le Plongeoir | Luh plon zhwar
This is a restaurant on a rock in the sea, at the top of an old diving board.

Follow the coast road east from the port, round the headland towards Mont Boron, and stop when you see it. Below the road, on a rocky outcrop just off the shore, is a white tower of platforms joined by a staircase, with a restaurant terrace on top. The terrace sits a little over six metres above the water.

The rock has been feeding people for a long time. From the eighteen-seventies, a fishing boat moored here served meals, tea and cocktails to Belle Époque visitors, and was tied up to a little kiosk on the rock. Then, in nineteen forty-one, the boat was badly damaged, and an architect named René Livieri replaced it with this, a three-level Art Deco diving board, with a staircase you could climb before jumping off into the sea.

For generations of Niçois, that was the game. Climb up, look down, work up the nerve. Later it fell out of use and was left to crumble.

In twenty fifteen it was brought back as a restaurant, and the platforms were kept. So now, instead of jumping off the top, you sit there and eat Mediterranean fish with the sea all round you.

[beat]

I find it rather lovely that the building's real job was always about nerve. Somebody had the nerve to put a boat-restaurant on a rock. Somebody else had the nerve to build a diving board in the middle of a war. And now you need a bit of nerve, if not for the jump, then at least for the bill.

If you've got time, walk back towards the port along the coast road. With the lighthouse, the harbour and the Castle Hill ahead of you, it's one of the loveliest walks in Nice, and it's free.

=== 10 | les-distilleries-ideales | Les Distilleries Idéales | Lay dee stee luh ree ee day al
This bar looks as if it's been here for a century. It hasn't.

You should be on Rue de la Préfecture, in the old town, at the corner where the bar's terrace spills out onto the lane. Look for the old-fashioned lettering over the windows, the dark wood frontage and, after six in the evening, a crowd of locals with drinks in hand.

The name means the ideal distilleries, and the room is dressed for it. Inside there's a Belle Époque and industrial look, all wood, metal and old bottles, the sort of décor that makes you assume your great-grandfather drank here. In fact it's been on this corner for a couple of decades, which by old-town standards is practically yesterday.

That doesn't make it a fake. It makes it rather good at something Nice has always done. This whole city is a bit of a stage set. The seafront hotels were built to look like palaces, and the Promenade itself began life so the English winter visitors could have somewhere to stroll. A bar dressed to look old fits right in.

What's actually in the glasses is simple. There are around eighteen beers on tap, a reasonable list of wines, and boards of charcuterie to go with them. And the crowd is mostly from Nice, not from tour groups, which is the best review any bar in this part of town can have.

[beat]

So treat it as what it is, the neighbourhood's front room. The apéritif hour between six and eight is the moment to come.

When you've finished your drink, walk a couple of minutes towards the sea. You'll come out onto the Cours Saleya, and if the market is packing up, you'll catch the last of the flower stalls being folded away.

=== 11 | la-part-des-anges | La Part des Anges | Lah par day zonj
The name refers to the wine that escapes from the barrel into the air. In this shop, very little goes to waste.

You should be on Rue Gubernatis, a quiet street in the city centre, a few minutes north of Place Masséna. Number seventeen is a narrow shopfront with wine bottles stacked to the ceiling and a few tables tucked between them. That's La Part des Anges.

The phrase means the angels' share. Winemakers use it for the small amount that evaporates through the wood as wine ages, the portion that goes up to heaven. It's a lovely name for a wine shop, and Olivier Labarde, who opened this one in nineteen ninety-eight, has spent the years since filling it with natural wine. That means wine made with as little interference as possible, from organic grapes, without much added in the cellar.

It's a shop, a wine bar and a lunch counter at once. You can buy a bottle to take away, or sit down in the middle of the shelves with a glass and a plate of something hearty. In twenty twenty the French wine magazine La Revue du Vin de France named it the best wine shop in the country.

Look out for one local name on the shelves, Bellet. It's Nice's own wine appellation, tiny and old, with vineyards in the hills just behind the city. Most visitors have no idea that Nice makes wine at all.

[beat]

That's what a good wine shop is really for. It's not the famous names. It's the bottle you'd never have found on your own, from a hillside you can very nearly see from here.

Before you leave, ask for a Bellet to take home. Then, if you fancy, the Musée Masséna's garden on the Promenade, fifteen minutes' walk south, is a fine place to sit and decide when to open it.

=== 12 | cafe-de-turin | Café de Turin | Ka fay duh too ran
The arcades over your head were built by an architect from Turin, which is why the café underneath them is named after his city.

You should be standing on Place Garibaldi, under the arcades. Look for the café's terrace and its counter piled with ice and shellfish. The man in the apron opening oysters at the front has the most important job here.

Café de Turin has been an institution in Nice for more than a hundred years, and it does one thing. Seafood platters, all sizes. Oysters, clams, prawns, crab, mussels. In season, it's also one of the places to try oursins, the local sea urchins, broken open and eaten raw with a spoon. They taste of nothing so much as the sea itself.

The name isn't decoration. When this square was laid out in the seventeen-seventies, Nice belonged to the House of Savoy, ruled from Turin. The square was designed as a grand gateway at the end of the road from Turin, with matching arcades on the Turin model so that people could walk in the shade. For most of its life, Nice faced north towards the Alps and Italy, not towards Paris.

[beat]

So eating oysters here is a small piece of time travel. The arcades are Piedmontese, the statue in the middle is the Italian hero Garibaldi, who was born in this city, and the café is named after a capital Nice no longer answers to.

If you've got a few minutes afterwards, head south-east out of the square, towards the port. Walk a couple of blocks down to the water and you'll see the boats that supply a good deal of what's on these platters.

=== 13 | cafe-du-cycliste | Café du Cycliste | Ka fay doo see kleest
The cyclists here are about to ride up mountains. The coffee is part of the training.

You should be on the Quai des Docks, along the east side of the old port. Look for the café and shop at number sixteen, with bicycles leaning outside and riders in Lycra standing about with espresso cups in hand. That's Café du Cycliste.

It's a clothing brand first. It was founded in Nice in two thousand and nine, and the story goes that it was dreamt up in a traditional café in the hills above the coast, the kind of place riders stop at halfway up a climb. In twenty fifteen it moved down here, to a big space on the port, and became a shop, a café and something like a clubhouse at once.

The reason it's here, and not in Paris, is outside the door. Nice has the sea on one side and the Alps on the other, and the roads that link them are some of the most famous in cycling. Professional riders live along this coast because they can train on serious climbs within half an hour of their front door. The Col d'Èze, behind the city, is one of the famous climbs of the Paris to Nice race. The routes start right here.

[beat]

What I like about this place is that it gets something right about Nice that the postcards miss. People come here for the beach. The locals, quite a lot of them, come here for the hills.

If you're not riding, order a coffee and watch the groups set off. Then walk to the end of the quay and look up at the hills behind the port. Every one of those switchbacks has someone on a bicycle somewhere on it.

=== 14 | maison-auer | Maison Auer | Mayzon ow er
The fruit in this window has been soaking in sugar for weeks.

You should be on Rue Saint-François-de-Paule, directly opposite the opera house, at the western edge of the old town. Look for the old shopfront with the name Auer above it, and behind the glass, rows of fruit that shine as if they've been lacquered. They have, in a way.

The house goes back to eighteen twenty. Its founder, Henri Auer, was a Swiss confectioner who came to the Côte d'Azur and was dazzled by the fruit, the oranges, lemons, figs and apricots that grew here almost without trying. His son Henri-Chrétien took over in eighteen ninety and made the family's candied fruit famous far beyond the city. Five generations of the family ran it after that.

The candying is the gift detail. A piece of fruit is soaked in syrup that's made stronger, little by little, over weeks, until the sugar has replaced the water inside it. A whole clementine done this way glows like amber, and keeps for months.

Step inside if you can. The interior is in a Florentine style, with gilded counters and mirrors, and it has barely changed. It's less a shop than a jewellery box, and it's listed as part of the city's heritage.

[beat]

Here's what's worth knowing. Before refrigeration, candying was how you kept summer. Fruit that would rot in days could be eaten at Christmas, posted across Europe, served to kings. What looks like a treat in the window was once one of the cleverest bits of food technology this coast had.

When you leave, look across the street at the opera house. Then walk a few doors along the same street to Alziari, the olive-oil shop. Between them, these two shops have been selling the flavours of this coast for well over three hundred years combined.

=== 15 | moulin-a-huile-dolive-alziari | Moulin à Huile d'Olive Alziari | Moolan ah weel doleev al zee ah ree
Look for the blue and yellow tins. They've become the unofficial flag of Niçois olive oil.

You should be on Rue Saint-François-de-Paule, a few steps from the opera house. The little shop with its shelves of painted tins is Alziari. If the door's open, you'll smell it before you see it.

The business goes back to eighteen sixty-eight, when a man named César Martin bought a ruined old mill in the Madeleine district, in the hills on the west of the city, and rebuilt it. In nineteen hundred his son-in-law, Nicolas Alziari, took it over and gave it his name. The mill is still working today, the oldest of its kind still running in Nice.

The shop came later. Nicolas's daughter Pauline opened it here at some point between the wars, and it's been the house's best-known address ever since. The tins came with it, in that azure blue and sunflower yellow, decorated in the Provençal style.

The local olive is called the Cailletier, small and dark, and it's the only variety grown in this area. It's also the olive that ends up, brined and black, in a salade niçoise.

[beat]

And here's the gift detail. Nicolas Alziari's idea was to blend, to mix oils from different olives to get a flavour he liked, rather than rely on any single one. That's why a tin from here never tastes quite like anybody else's. A family recipe for oil, of all things, kept going for more than a century.

When you've bought your tin, walk on to the Cours Saleya, a few minutes away. In autumn and winter, look out for the market stalls selling the season's olives. That's where all of this begins.

=== 16 | confiserie-florian | Confiserie Florian | Kon fee zuh ree flo ree on
You can eat a violet here. You can eat a rose petal, too.

You should be on the Quai Papacino, along the west side of the old port, near where it meets Rue Robilant. Look for the shopfront with Florian over the door, and the window full of fruit glazed until it shines. That's the confiserie.

The Florian name has been tied to this harbour for about a century. A chocolate maker called Florian worked on the port here from the nineteen-twenties. The confectionery family that now runs it began up in the hills near Grasse, in the Gorges du Loup, and took over this shop in nineteen seventy-four.

The gift detail is the flowers. Grasse, an hour inland, is the perfume capital of the world, surrounded by fields of rose and jasmine. The violets come from the village of Tourrettes, in the same hills. Here, the flowers are crystallised, coated in sugar until they keep, and they still taste faintly of their scent. It's like eating a perfume bottle, in the nicest possible way.

The fruit is the other half of the business. Clementines, lemons from Menton, oranges, cherries. A big piece of fruit can soak for up to three months, with the syrup changed every couple of days, before it's ready.

[beat]

What I find rather wonderful is that nothing about this is new. People along this coast have been sugaring their fruit and flowers for centuries because they had too much of both. What began as a way of not wasting the harvest has become one of the prettiest windows on the port.

If you have a little time, ask inside about a tour. They've long offered free visits of the workshop, with tastings. And on the way out, look at the boats in front of you, the little wooden ones painted in bright colours. They're called pointus, and they're as Niçois as anything in the shop.

=== 17 | mamac-nice | MAMAC, Musée d'Art Moderne et d'Art Contemporain | Mah mak, the museum of modern and contemporary art
Four marble towers, linked by glass bridges, standing on top of a river you can't see.

You should be on Place Yves Klein, on the edge of the long park called the Promenade du Paillon, looking at a large white building made of four square towers. They're clad in smooth white marble from Carrara, and they're joined high up by glazed walkways. That's the museum of modern and contemporary art, which everyone here calls the MAMAC.

It was designed by the architects Yves Bayard and Henri Vidal and opened in nineteen ninety. The four-legged form was a deliberate choice. The building stands astride the course of the Paillon, the river that once ran through the middle of Nice and has since been covered over, so it acts as a sort of grand gateway between the old town and the newer city.

The square in front is named after Yves Klein, and that's the clue to what's inside. Klein was born in Nice in nineteen twenty-eight and became famous for a single, impossibly deep blue that he patented. He and the artists around him, the New Realists, many of them from Nice, are the heart of the museum's collection. Klein has a room of his own.

A word of warning. The museum is closed for a long renovation and isn't expected to reopen until twenty twenty-eight, so for now this is a building to look at rather than walk into.

[beat]

Which turns out to be no bad thing. From out here you can see what Bayard and Vidal were up to. They didn't put the art in a box. They built a set of white frames against the Mediterranean sky, and in this light, the white is doing what Klein's blue did. It makes you look.

Before you leave, walk round the back of the building towards the Tête Carrée, the giant square head next door. It's by Sacha Sosno, and it's part of the same story.

=== 18 | musee-marc-chagall | Musée National Marc Chagall | Mew zay nah syo nal mark shah gal
This is a rare thing, a museum designed around an artist while he was still alive to argue about it.

You should be on Avenue du Docteur Ménard, on the lower slopes of Cimiez, at the entrance to a low white building half-hidden in a garden. You'll notice that the planting is mostly in blue and white. That's not an accident.

In nineteen sixty-nine the French culture minister, André Malraux, decided to build a home for a gift. Marc Chagall had given the nation seventeen huge paintings on stories from the Bible, called the Biblical Message. The architect André Hermant designed the building to hold them, and the museum opened in nineteen seventy-three. Chagall came to the opening himself.

Look at the architecture first. It's sober and low, pale stone and glass, more like a modern villa than a monument. It sits in a Mediterranean garden designed by Henri Fisch, who chose, with Chagall's agreement, cool colours and white and blue flowers, so that the outside prepares your eye for the inside.

Then there's the gift detail, and it's inside the auditorium. Chagall designed the stained-glass windows for that room himself, working with the master glassmaker Charles Marcq. They tell the creation of the world, in deep blues, and when the sun comes through, the whole concert hall turns the colour of the sea. He also made the large mosaic that you'll see reflected in the pool outside.

[beat]

What I find rather moving is that this isn't a museum about Chagall's career. It's a museum built for one idea he had, and he was able to shape every bit of it, down to the flowers. Most artists get a building after they're gone. He got to choose the colour of the garden.

If you've got the energy afterwards, keep walking up the hill to Cimiez. Twenty minutes or so brings you to the Musée Matisse, among the olive trees. Two of the century's great colourists, on the same hillside.

=== 19 | musee-matisse | Musée Matisse | Mew zay mah teess
Most of the windows on this villa aren't windows at all. They're painted on.

You should be in the park of the Cimiez Arènes, on the hill above the city, among the olive trees. The big red villa in front of you, with its rows of tall pale-framed windows and balconies, is the Musée Matisse. Look closely at the facade and you'll start to notice that some of those windows, and some of the shutters, are painted.

=== 26 | monument-aux-morts-rauba-capeu | Monument aux Morts de Rauba-Capèu | Mon oo mon oh mor duh rowba kapeyoo

[beat]
