The mural on this building had a title. eye kool too rah en nwes trah koh moo nee dahd. There Is Culture in Our Community.

You should be at Eighteenth and ruh seen, looking south down ruh seen at the three-story brick building a few doors along — the one carrying painted figures across its front.

It opened in nineteen oh five as Howell Neighborhood House, a settlement house for the Czechs and Poles and Italians packed into these blocks: English classes, a clinic, somewhere to leave your children. By nineteen seventy the families here were largely Mexican, many of them displaced from the Near West Side by an expressway and a university campus. The settlement house took a new name that year: Casa ahs tlahn. ahs tlahn is the northern homeland the meh shee kah described migrating out of, centuries earlier — so a community center on ruh seen Avenue took the name of a place out of a migration account.

Ray paht lahn painted the interior in nineteen seventy, while he was still an art student, and the front between nineteen seventy-one and nineteen seventy-three, working with about forty neighborhood teenagers on a city youth-jobs program. paht lahn was born in Chicago; his parents came from gwah nah hwah toh. The faces they put up here — kwow teh mock, beh nee toh whar ess, free dah kah loh — belonged to people who had never set foot in Illinois, and in one case had been dead four hundred years. None of the painters had seen any of them either; they were working from pictures of pictures.

Nothing on this wall originated on this wall. The iconography came north, the name came out of a migration story, the building came out of a Protestant settlement movement, and a Chicago-born art student put the three together on one facade. Faithful reproduction was never the assignment.

The center closed in twenty thirteen and the building holds apartments now. The front was painted over in twenty seventeen and repainted later that year by paht lahn and the muralist roh bair toh vah lah dess. paht lahn died in twenty twenty-four.

Walk west — barely a block, and Eighteenth crosses Allport. Stop where you can see the limestone building on the corner and the church facing it.
