Go as far as the foot of the station stairs and look up.

You're at Eighteenth and paw lie nuh. From the sidewalk you can see the mosaic beside the entrance and the paint climbing the stairwell; the platform walls carry more of it, past the turnstiles.

Trains have stopped here since eighteen ninety-six, and the station was rebuilt in the nineteen nineties. When the work was finished, Francisco men doh zah painted the place — stairwells, corridors, platform walls — with students, through a city youth arts program and a transit-authority scheme that let artists adopt a station. The museum at the far end of this walk helped put it together. men doh zah grew up in South Chicago and taught art in schools around here for more than twenty-five years. He died in twenty twelve.

On the platform he and his students painted the Aztec sun stone, and the moon stone beside it. The original sun stone is basalt, carved in teh nauch teet lahn five centuries ago, nearly twelve feet across, and it sits today in a museum in Mexico City with a rope around it. This one is house paint on steel panel, at about the height of your shoulder, on a platform where people are watching the arrival board.

The stone never moved. The image did — and on the way it lost the basalt, lost the scale, lost the temple, and picked up a handrail. Nobody here was attempting a monument. They were putting something on a wall that the students painting it would recognize on the way to school, which is a different job, done well.

Cross Eighteenth, go one block west and one block south, into Harrison Park. The long building with the ball fields around it is the last stop.
