# MIAMI 27 — Overtown, NW 2nd Avenue under the interchange
T2 · B6a · pays S4 · docket 3, 17 · TTS-safe · 24 Sept 2026

---

On a street map of Miami from the early nineteen fifties, the ground where you're standing is drawn full: narrow lots, churches on corners, storefronts along the avenue and residential streets running west, where now there are decks overhead and columns where the lots were.

You're on Northwest Second Avenue beneath the interchange where Interstate Ninety Five meets Interstate Three Ninety Five and the Dolphin Expressway, at the southern edge of Overtown. For decades this neighborhood was officially called Colored Town, and it was the part of Miami where the law allowed Black residents to live, which made it a confinement and a capital at once, with doctors, lawyers, hotels and churches packed into a few dozen blocks.

The first line drawn through here was a railroad: when the Florida East Coast tracks came south in eighteen ninety six they split the new city, white Miami to the east and Black Miami to the west, and the neighborhood took its shape from someone else's route.

The road overhead has a destination, and it matters. In the nineteen sixties, planners routed the expressways through the middle of Overtown, and the east west spur, Interstate Three Ninety Five, runs straight to the MacArthur Causeway and across the bay to Miami Beach. Homes, shops and churches were condemned block by block; most residents rented, renters were offered little or nothing, and families scattered north and west to Liberty City and Brownsville. Much of Miami's leadership backed the route as progress, while others warned that no one was planning where the people would go.

The road built to carry visitors to the Beach was laid across the neighborhood that had housed the Beach's own performers when the Beach refused to, and the second color line in this city turned out to be a highway, set higher than the first and far harder to cross.

Overtown did not vanish: its surviving churches still hold services, and the community that built this corner of the city remains in the blocks beyond the columns, a neighborhood underneath a road designed to carry other people past it. The highway is being rebuilt higher, with open ground and a trail planned beneath it, and whether that knits anything back together is Overtown's to judge.

Before you walk on, look up once at the underside of the deck, then bring your eyes back to street level, where this neighborhood has always kept its business.
