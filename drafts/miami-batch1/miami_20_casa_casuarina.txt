# MIAMI 20 — Casa Casuarina, exterior only
Tier 2 · B3 · docket 9 · locked treatment 3 · clean · 24 September 2026

---

Across the Caribbean, in the old colonial quarter of Santo Domingo, stands a coral-stone palace built around 1510 for Diego Colón, the son of Christopher Columbus, and the house behind this wall is its deliberate, scaled-down copy.

You are on the sidewalk outside eleven sixteen Ocean Drive, looking at Casa Casuarina, the three-story Mediterranean Revival house behind the high wall and the iron gates. Everything worth seeing for this stop can be seen from exactly where you stand, so there is no need to go any closer.

It went up in 1930 for Alden Freeman, an heir to a Standard Oil fortune, who modeled it on the Alcázar de Colón, that Santo Domingo palace, down to the rough coral-rock blocks; a stone from the original is said to be set into the wall beside the entrance. Freeman lived on the top floor and rented the rest out as apartments to friends and tenants, and he named the house for the casuarina, the Australian pine, though nobody recorded exactly why.

The copy outlived the life it was built for. After Freeman's death in 1937 it spent half a century as an ordinary apartment house on a declining street, and by the 1980s its rooms rented for almost nothing while the fittings Freeman had installed survived, dusty and intact, behind the same wall. In 1992 the Italian designer Gianni Versace bought it, together with the hotel next door, and rebuilt it into the palace most people now picture; he was killed on its front steps in 1997. Since 2015 the house has operated as a small hotel, The Villa Casa Casuarina.

*[beat]*

So the building has worn four identities in a century: a rich man's tribute to a colonial palace, a rooming house, a designer's showplace, and a place where travelers can book a night. None of them erased the one before, and through all of them the face it shows the street has stayed the same, a borrowed front from another island and another century.

Let your eye rest on the stone and the ironwork a moment longer, then walk on, since the most interesting thing about this house is not any one owner but how many times it has quietly become something else.
