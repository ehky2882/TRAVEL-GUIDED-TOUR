If it's anywhere between October and March, look at the very top before you look at anything else. The stepped pyramid up there is a winter roost for turkey vultures, and they have been coming back to this roof for so long that their return used to get written up in the newspaper as the city's version of the leaves changing. In summer the roof is empty and you'll have to take my word for it.

You should be on the north sidewalk of West Flagler Street, across from the courthouse, facing its front. The columns at the entrance run up a full three stories, a statue of Henry Flagler stands near the doors, and a few steps away at Northwest First Avenue a newer glass tower rises with a curved, mirrored bubble on its face. That's the replacement, and the old tower is reflected in that bubble on purpose.

Now the building. Twenty-eight stories, three hundred and sixty-one feet, begun in nineteen twenty-five and finished in nineteen twenty-eight, and for about thirty years after that nothing downtown stood taller. It was built for everything at once: courtrooms, the city and county governments, and, under that stepped roof, the jail; the top floors were cells with a view. Al kuh pohn was tried in one of the courtrooms in nineteen thirty, on a perjury charge, and acquitted.

Look at the dates again. Begun in the boom of nineteen twenty-five, when lots in this county were changing hands twice in an afternoon; finished in nineteen twenty-eight, after a hurricane in September of nineteen twenty-six had ended the boom a full three years ahead of the rest of the country's crash. The courthouse is a sentence that began in one economy and finished in another, and downtown is full of them: buildings whose foundations were poured by optimists and whose upper floors were paid for, slowly, by whoever was left.

And the sentence isn't finished. In December of twenty twenty-five the civil courts moved into the glass tower after nearly a century here, and this building emptied. The county has tried to sell it; the historic designation forbids demolition and protects the lobby, the atrium and the courtrooms inside; the auction in the summer of twenty twenty-six drew lookers and no bids. It stands here, for now, waiting for the next economy to decide what it's for.

Boom, storm, bust, and then a long patience. The vultures, to be fair, have never once missed a season.
