# MIAMI 29 — Little Haiti Cultural Complex and the Caribbean Marketplace
T2 · B6b · pays S5 · contested-change block · docket 8, 14 · clean · 24 Sept 2026

---

You'll know the building a block before you reach it, because nothing else on Northeast Second Avenue is painted this shade of yellow, and the roofline, with its steep gables and iron fretwork, belongs to another city entirely.

This is the Caribbean Marketplace, Mache Ayisyen, and it is a deliberate copy of the Iron Market in Port-au-Prince, the covered bazaar of the Haitian capital. Haitians began arriving in Miami in numbers in the 1970s, and through the 1980s many came by sea in small boats while the United States ran a policy of interdiction, stopping vessels offshore and returning most of those aboard; the ones who reached this neighborhood, then still known as Lemon City, made it the heart of Haitian Miami.

The building itself was a rescue twice over. In 1984 the local architects' association and a Haitian community group held a competition to remake a derelict building on this corner, and the winner, Charles Harrison Pawley, born in Haiti to American parents, drew on the Iron Market and the gingerbread houses of Port-au-Prince; the market opened in 1990, won a national architecture award, and closed in 1999 when the money ran out. The city took it over in 2005 intending to tear it down, and protests from the neighborhood saved it, so the cultural complex beside it, opened in 2006 at the urging of the commissioner Arthur E. Teele, was built around a survivor.

*[beat]*

Exile usually leaves a doorway behind; here it rebuilt the marketplace people had left, in the place they arrived, and the neighborhood has fought twice to keep the copy standing.

Little Haiti sits on a limestone ridge, higher than most of the city, and two positions are held about what that means. Researchers at Harvard reported in 2018 that homes at higher elevations across the county had gained value faster than homes near the coast, and named this neighborhood as one where residents could be priced out by buyers seeking higher ground; researchers at the University of Miami answered in 2022 that the buyers arriving in Little Haiti were drawn by location and price, not elevation. A large mixed-use development a few blocks north is the ground the two positions argue over, and both are reported here without a verdict.

The market keeps weekend hours, so if the doors are open, go in, and if they are closed the courtyard and the colors are the visit.
