The tower with the pale octagonal top and the small cupola above it is the one you want. Seventeen stories of cream stucco and terra-cotta trim, wrought iron at the balconies, and a crown that belongs in Seville, because it does: the design borrows the Giralda, the bell tower of Seville's cathedral.

You should be on the Biscayne Boulevard sidewalk, on the west side of the street, with the tower directly ahead of you and the arena across the boulevard at your back. This stop is written from the sidewalk on purpose, because the museum inside keeps its own hours and its own tickets, and the building's story is legible from out here.

Two dates. In 1925 this opened as the headquarters of a newspaper, the Miami News, and the tower was a deliberately loud announcement: a boomtown declaring that it would have the architecture of an old city immediately, without waiting to be old. The presses ran downstairs, and the words went out from here.

Then in 1962 the direction reversed. The federal government took over the building as the Cuban Refugee Emergency Center, and for twelve years, until 1974, people arriving from Cuba came through these doors to be registered, given medical checks and financial help, and pointed toward work and somewhere to sleep. Hundreds of thousands passed through. In Spanish it's La Torre de la Libertad, and it is often called the Ellis Island of the South, a comparison that works as far as it goes: Ellis Island processed people and sent them onward, while this building processed people who, mostly, stayed within a few miles.

That is the detail worth holding. A receiving station is a threshold, and a threshold is supposed to be brief. Here the threshold became the address. The families who signed in upstairs settled the streets west of here and along Eighth Street, arrivals kept coming from other coasts in other decades, and what a receiving station looks like once nobody leaves is, more or less, the city you're standing in.

The newspaper moved out in 1957; in 2005 it was given to Miami Dade College, in 2008 it was named a National Historic Landmark, and in September 2025 it reopened for its hundredth birthday.

*[beat]*

Look at the top again. It was built to broadcast a city outward and ended up as the room the city walked into. Same tower, both directions.
