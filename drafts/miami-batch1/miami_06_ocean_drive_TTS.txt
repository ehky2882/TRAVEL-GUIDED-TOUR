In the middle of the nineteen seventies, the most realistic future for the row of hotels across the street was a bulldozer. The buildings were about forty years old, small and stucco and cracked, painted in beiges and off-whites, and filled largely with retirees living on fixed incomes; developers and a good part of city government saw a tired strip waiting to be cleared for towers, and almost nobody thought these were buildings worth a fight.

You are in lum us Park, on the beach side of Ocean Drive, looking west at the row. Most of it went up through the Depression years of the nineteen thirties and into the early nineteen forties, which is why it is modest: three and four stories, rounded corners, flat roofs finished with a stepped parapet or a thin spire, and horizontal bands scored across the front to suggest speed in buildings that go nowhere. The style is a local, budget version of Art Deco, built quickly by architects who had to make inexpensive hotels look like ocean liners.

The colors you are looking at came afterward. Leonard hor uh wits chose the pastel palette in the early nineteen eighties, painting over the beige with pinks and lemons and blues, so what reads as original Deco is partly a preservationist's idea laid over the real thing, and it worked. The district now holds one of the largest concentrations of Art Deco architecture in the world, most of it saved when saving it looked foolish.

Walk the row slowly and keep the argument underneath it in mind: the city did not keep these buildings because anyone thought them precious, but because a few stubborn people insisted that cheap, ordinary, overlooked things could be history too.
