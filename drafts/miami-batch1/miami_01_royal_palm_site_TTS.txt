It's March of eighteen ninety-six, and everything around you is woods. Three houses on a few cleared acres, and a crushed-rock lane running up from the river where a handful of shacks and tents pass for downtown. A man named John soo ul steps off a steamer with orders to strip a square mile of it bare.

You should be on the Riverwalk at the mouth of the Miami River, on the north bank, where the river opens into bis kayn Bay. Face the bay and the river is on your right; behind you, glass towers stand on the old hotel's ground, give or take a block; across the water, a low green point closes the far bank. This bank is what soo ul was clearing.

Picture the hotel. Five stories of yellow-painted wood, six at the center, running close to seven hundred feet along this bank, four hundred and fifty rooms, sixteen cooks, and the first electric lights, elevators and swimming pool this place had ever seen. It opened in January of eighteen ninety-seven and it was called the Royal Palm. Not a board of it survives; a hurricane in nineteen twenty-six broke it, termites finished it, and it came down in nineteen thirty.

The reason it existed at all is the reason the city does. A woman named Julia tut ul owned the land on this bank and wanted a railroad, and a railroad man named Henry Flagler had stopped his line at Palm Beach and saw no reason to come farther. After the freezes of the winter of eighteen ninety-four and eighteen ninety-five killed citrus across the north of the state, she offered him land, and he took it on condition that he build a railroad, a hotel and a town. The famous version says she mailed him a box of orange blossoms to prove the frost had never reached her. That's a legend, and a good one; what the record shows is that his own agent came down, saw the groves intact, and reported back.

So the deal is the founding act: no harbor to speak of, no reason for a city here except that two people talked each other into one. On the twenty-eighth of July, eighteen ninety-six, three hundred and sixty-eight men voted to incorporate, a large share of them the Black laborers who had cleared and built the place. Julia tut ul couldn't vote; neither could any woman.

Somewhere along this bank, on a January evening in eighteen ninety-seven, a room full of people from the north looked out at this water under the town's first electric light and were sold. Stand a minute where they stood. The pitch still works.
