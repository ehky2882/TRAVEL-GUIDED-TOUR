# MIAMI 26 — Lyric Theater, Overtown
T2 · B6a · pays S4 · docket 18 · clean · 24 Sept 2026

---

In 1915 the Miami Metropolis called this building "possibly the most beautiful and costly playhouse owned by colored people in all the Southland," and the phrasing tells you two things at once: that a white downtown paper had noticed, and that the city had already sorted its theaters by the color of the people who owned them.

You're on Northwest Second Avenue in Overtown, in front of a two-story stucco building on a stretch the neighborhood once called Little Broadway. A businessman from Georgia named Geder Walker built the Lyric in 1913 and ran it himself; it seated about four hundred and showed movies, vaudeville, boxing and graduations, because in a segregated city a Black theater had to be every kind of hall at once.

When Walker died in 1919, his estate included a bakery, an ice cream shop, a café and land across the neighborhood, so the theater was one piece of a small economy he had assembled lot by lot, and his widow, Henrietta, kept its doors open for another forty years.

The crossing to the Beach ran one way at eight in the evening and the other way at two in the morning. From the 1930s into the 1950s, Black entertainers headlined the big hotels on Miami Beach, and when the last set ended they were not permitted to take a room in the buildings they had just filled, so they rode back across the bay to Overtown, where hotels on this side of the tracks put them up and the clubs along this avenue kept going until dawn. Nat King Cole, Ella Fitzgerald and Count Basie were among the guests, and a promoter named Clyde Killens made a career of bringing the late shows here.

*[beat]*

The Beach bought the music and sent the musicians home at night, and the home it sent them to was this avenue, so the resort everyone came to see was running on a second city it would not let its own headliners sleep in.

A church used the Lyric from 1959, and in the late 1980s the Black Archives, which owns it now, restored it and saw it listed on the National Register in 1989; the Archives describes it as the last building standing from Little Broadway.

If a show is on the bill when you pass, buy a ticket, since a full house is what this room was built for.
