MIAMI — 18 — Miami River, Lummus Park downtown, Fort Dallas barracks and the Wagner homestead
Tier 2 · Batch B2 · pays S7, S1 · docket 1, 2 · Clean version
---

The sign says Fort Dallas. Hold that name loosely; the building was a fort for about a decade and several other things for longer.

You should be inside Lummus Park, the downtown one, not the beach one, on the north bank of the Miami River just west of the interstate. The river is along the park's west edge; the highway is east, and a big columned temple, the Scottish Rite building, stands north. Two old structures sit in the grass: a long, low building of rough gray limestone, and, near it, a small two-story wooden house with a porch.

The stone building has had four lives. It was built in the 1840s at the mouth of the river, not here, as part of a plantation belonging to a man named William English, and it was built as quarters for the enslaved people who worked that plantation. When the Army returned to the river during the Third Seminole War in the 1850s, it took over the plantation buildings and used this one as a barracks, hence the name. The land later passed to Julia Tuttle, who bought it in 1891 and made the stone building part of her homestead. It was on her ground when she made the deal that started the city.

In 1925, at the top of the boom, a hotel was planned for that ground, and the building was taken down stone by stone and put back together here, in what is usually called the city's first act of preservation.

The wooden house is older still, or nearly: William Wagner built it around 1855 upriver, and by most accounts it is the oldest house still standing in the city. Wagner was an Army veteran who stayed on with his wife, Everline, and the house was moved here in 1979, ahead of the elevated rail line.

Both buildings stood on the river, both were in the way, and both were carried to a park to make room for something newer. This is how the city has always handled its past: not by leaving it where it was, but by lifting it out of the path, which is why the oldest things in Miami are so rarely in their original places, and why the ground they came from keeps turning out to hold older things still.

*[beat]*

A building made for enslaved people, then for soldiers, then for a woman who sold a city, then for a park. Four lives, one set of stones, and every one of them began at the same river mouth.
