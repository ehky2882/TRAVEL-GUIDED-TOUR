# MIAMI 09 — Cuban Memorial Boulevard, the Bay of Pigs monument
T1 · B4 · pays S5 · docket 6 · clean · 24 Sept 2026

---

In gold letters above the names on these plaques, the words read Mártires de Playa Girón: the martyrs of Girón beach. You're a block east of Domino Park, where Southwest Thirteenth Avenue meets Calle Ocho and a broad, tree-shaded median runs south between the traffic lanes, and the first thing on it is this column of gray marble, a little taller than two people, with the brigade's crest on its face and an urn on top holding a flame meant never to go out.

That wording is not the phrase most English speakers would reach for. In the United States the landing of April 1961 is remembered as the Bay of Pigs; the exile force that went ashore, Brigade Twenty-Five Oh Six, and the families who raised this column name it for the beach where they landed, Playa Girón, as they knew it. The landing failed within days, most of the brigade was captured, and at the end of the following year the prisoners were released to the United States, many of them settling in this neighborhood. Ten years to the day after the landing, the community dedicated the column, and the flame was first lit by the daughter of one of the men it remembers.

The flame does not stand alone. Along the median the boulevard becomes a sequence the community assembled over decades: a bronze map of Cuba, a memorial to José Martí, a statue of the Virgin, a bust of the independence general Antonio Maceo, a statue of the brigade veteran Nestor Izquierdo, a bust of a tango star who left the island in 1959, a small plaza for Cuban journalists, and a great ceiba tree, sacred in Afro-Cuban religious tradition, where small offerings sometimes appear at the roots and are best left undisturbed.

*[beat]*

Cities usually put official monuments downtown; this one stands on a residential median, raised by the families of the men it names, carrying their own dead and their own heroes, and people gather at it on their own anniversaries. Whatever a visitor thinks about the politics of the island, this is a community's memorial, placed where its members live and worded in their language.

The boulevard runs south from here under its trees, past the monuments and on into quiet residential blocks, and the flame stays at the head of it, facing Calle Ocho.
