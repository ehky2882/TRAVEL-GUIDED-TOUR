# MIAMI 22 — Holocaust Memorial Miami Beach
Tier 2 · B3 · docket 19 · TTS-safe · 24 September 2026

---

This memorial was begun by the people it speaks for. In nineteen eighty four a small committee of Holocaust survivors living on and around Miami Beach decided that their community needed a permanent place of remembrance, and the city set aside this corner of Meridian Avenue and Dade Boulevard for it, on land that had been the home of a survivor family who came here from Rotterdam.

You are at the memorial's entrance, where a path lined with palms leads inward toward a reflecting pool, a long wall of black granite, and a large bronze sculpture at the center. It is open to the public without charge, and there is no right way through it except slowly.

The memorial was designed by the Miami architect and sculptor Kenneth try ster and dedicated on the fourth of February, nineteen ninety, with the writer el ee vee zel, himself a survivor, among those who spoke. Its street address on Meridian Avenue runs from nineteen thirty three to nineteen forty five, the years themselves.

The people who built it had reasons to build it here. By the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, South Beach was home to a large community of elderly Jewish retirees living in the small apartment hotels a few blocks east, and many of them had survived the camps or lost nearly everyone they had known in Europe. They wanted a place that would outlast them, where their children could stand, and where the names of the people they had lost could be written down in the city where they had finally settled.

The names on the granite were submitted by survivors and their families, many of whom lived nearby, often for parents, children and siblings who left no grave behind. The wall is less a monument than a record kept by the families themselves, which is what gives this garden its particular weight: it was made by witnesses, for the people they carried with them.

Stay on the path as long or as briefly as you like, at whatever pace feels right. The memorial asks nothing more of a visitor than attention, and nothing less.
