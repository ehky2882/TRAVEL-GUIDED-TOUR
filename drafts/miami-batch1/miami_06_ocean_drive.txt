# MIAMI 06 — Ocean Drive, Lummus Park, the Art Deco row
Tier 1 · B3 · plants S3 · clean · 24 September 2026

---

In the middle of the 1970s, the most realistic future for the row of hotels across the street was a bulldozer. The buildings were about forty years old, small and stucco and cracked, painted in beiges and off-whites, and filled largely with retirees living on fixed incomes; developers and a good part of city government saw a tired strip waiting to be cleared for towers, and almost nobody thought these were buildings worth a fight.

You are in Lummus Park, on the beach side of Ocean Drive, looking west at the row. Most of it went up through the Depression years of the 1930s and into the early 1940s, which is why it is modest: three and four stories, rounded corners, flat roofs finished with a stepped parapet or a thin spire, and horizontal bands scored across the front to suggest speed in buildings that go nowhere. The style is a local, budget version of Art Deco, built quickly by architects who had to make inexpensive hotels look like ocean liners.

Her name was Barbara Baer Capitman. She came to Miami Beach in the mid 1970s and in 1976 she and a small group of designers, among them Leonard Horowitz, founded the Miami Design Preservation League to argue that this unloved row was a historic district — at a time when historic meant old, and old meant the nineteenth century at the latest. The argument won on paper on the fourteenth of May, 1979, when the district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places; the state's own marker describes it as the first urban district of twentieth-century buildings to receive that listing. It lost some battles on the street, where Capitman stood in front of bulldozers and several hotels came down anyway.

*[beat]*

The colors you are looking at came afterward. Leonard Horowitz chose the pastel palette in the early 1980s, painting over the beige with pinks and lemons and blues, so what reads as original Deco is partly a preservationist's idea laid over the real thing, and it worked. The district now holds one of the largest concentrations of Art Deco architecture in the world, most of it saved when saving it looked foolish.

Walk the row slowly and keep the argument underneath it in mind: the city did not keep these buildings because anyone thought them precious, but because a few stubborn people insisted that cheap, ordinary, overlooked things could be history too.
