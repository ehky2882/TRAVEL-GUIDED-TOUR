# MIAMI 28 — Bacardi Building, Biscayne Boulevard
T2 · B6b · pays S5 · clean · 24 Sept 2026

---

Pilots see this building before you do. Painted across the plaza in front of it is a company logo large enough to read from the air, laid out for passengers descending toward the airport, and the tower behind it was designed with the same confidence, to be recognized from a distance by people who had not yet arrived.

You're on Biscayne Boulevard at Twenty-First Street, looking at a slim tower faced on two sides with blue and white ceramic tile, designed by the Cuban architect Enrique Gutiérrez and finished in 1963, and beside it a smaller square building lifted on a single pedestal, its walls made of stained glass, added in 1973. The tile murals are the work of the Brazilian artist Francisco Brennand, and up close they break into small ceramic pieces; from the far curb they read as one blue field.

The company arrived the way its customers did, and Bacardi had been a Cuban firm since the 1860s; when the Cuban government took its assets in 1960 the family carried the business into exile and chose this boulevard for its American headquarters, then commissioned a building that no one could mistake for a temporary arrangement. The president who ordered it, José "Pepín" Bosch, wanted a landmark, and the tile, the glass and the plaza were the argument: a company that had lost its home was announcing that it had found another.

*[beat]*

Most of what this city calls exile is told through people, and it looks like a park bench, a memorial or a movie house; here it is told through a corporation, and it looks like a headquarters, which is what arrival looks like when it comes with a payroll.

The company itself moved to Coral Gables in 2009, and the buildings sat empty until residents pushed the city to protect them as historic, which it did; in 2012 the family sold the complex, at a price well below its market value, to a national arts foundation that has kept the exteriors exactly as they were, and in 2018 the two buildings reached the National Register more than a decade before their fiftieth birthday.

Don't try to count the tiles. Cross to the west side of the boulevard, find the spot where both façades line up, and let them read as a single surface, the way they were meant to be seen by someone still on approach.
