# MIAMI 07 — Lincoln Road, the Lapidus mall
Tier 1 · B3 · pays S1 · clean · 24 September 2026

---

By his wife Jane's account, Carl Fisher peeled a stick on a walk through the mangroves here, and when the two of them reached clean sand he drew streets and squares on the ground with it; she later wrote that the damp sand he drew on became Lincoln Road.

You are on Lincoln Road, the pedestrian mall that crosses the Beach from near Washington Avenue west toward the bay, with shops and restaurants on both sides, shade structures and plantings running down the middle, and no cars. Stand anywhere along it and you are standing on that drawing.

Go back to before any of it existed. Fisher was an Indiana man who had made a fortune in automobile headlights, helped build the Indianapolis Motor Speedway, and pushed for the Lincoln Highway, an early road meant to cross the whole country. He arrived here at the end of 1912 to find a narrow, mosquito-ridden island of mangrove and scrub, and he and his partners spent staggering sums clearing it and dredging it into building lots, long before a single sale paid them back. He named this street for the president whose name he had already put on a highway, and he wanted it to become the Fifth Avenue of the South.

What Fisher was really selling was not land but an idea of winter. He advertised sunshine to people shoveling snow in Indianapolis, Chicago and New York, staged speedboat races and publicity stunts, and filled the island with buyers who had never seen it; by the 1940s the fashionable department stores had storefronts here. When the street faded after the war, the city hired the architect Morris Lapidus, who closed it to traffic in 1960 and gave it the canopies and follies you are walking among.

*[beat]*

The city across the bay began when two people agreed to make one. The Beach is that same move made bigger and bolder: one promoter, a stick, a stretch of sand nobody wanted, and a sales campaign aimed at strangers a thousand miles away, and it worked so well that the island Fisher described now looks like the one he advertised.

Whichever direction you head from here, every step is on a line first drawn by a man who could see a city on an empty beach, and who was persuasive enough to make everyone else see it too.
