# Library of Congress — the Jefferson Building

---

The man who drew this city was fired before any of it was built.

Pierre L'Enfant was a French engineer who had come over to fight in the Revolution and stayed. In 1791 he was handed a stretch of farmland and tidal flats and asked for a capital. He walked it for weeks, and what he drew was not a town but a diagram: two seats of power on two heights, long avenues cut diagonally across a grid, and open ground between them for everything a country might one day want to say about itself. Within a year he had quarreled with everyone who paid him, pulled down a landowner's half-built house because it sat in the way of an avenue, and was let go. He died poor in 1825, and the plan went on without him.

You should be standing on First Street Southeast, across from the Capitol's east side, looking at a building of gray granite with a green copper dome and a gilded torch on top. In front of it, a fountain full of bronze sea gods and horses — Neptune, flanked by tritons. The grand staircase rises above the fountain to three arched doors. If you have a timed pass, the visitor entrance is tucked underneath that staircase; the free passes run out, so this is written for the sidewalk.

Eighteen ninety-seven.

That is when this opened, and the only reason it exists is that the Capitol ran out of room for books. Congress had kept its library inside the Capitol since 1800. The first collection burned when the British burned the building in 1814, and it was restarted with the private library of a president who needed the money and had more books than anyone else in the country, which is why this building carries his name. By the eighteen-eighties the collection was piled on the floors, and Congress gave in and built next door.

Look at how much of the building is decoration. Every window on the ground floor has a carved head above it. Every surface that could take a figure has one. This was the country, a century on, deciding that it had something to show, and the architects, Smithmeyer and Pelz, were told to spend accordingly. It holds, by most counts, more items than any other library anywhere, and most of them are in plainer buildings behind and beside this one.

[beat]

Now go back to L'Enfant. He put Congress on the high ground and the Library sits on the same rise, a step behind it, which is what he intended for the whole Hill — the top of the plan, the place the rest of the city would be laid out to look toward. He never saw a stone of it. In 1909 his bones were moved from a Maryland farm to a hilltop across the river, with a view straight down the avenue he drew. The city finally gave him the vantage point. He had already given it everything else.
