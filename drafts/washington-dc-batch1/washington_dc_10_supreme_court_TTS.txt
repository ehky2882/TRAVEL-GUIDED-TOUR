There is a law about the ground under your feet.

You should be standing on the east side of First Street Northeast, on the sidewalk in front of a white marble building with a wide flight of steps and a deep portico of tall columns, directly across the street from the Capitol's east front. Above the columns, four words carved into the stone: Equal Justice Under Law. Below the steps, a broad marble plaza with two fountains and two flagpoles.

So.

The Court has only had its own building since nineteen thirty five. For nearly a century and a half before that it borrowed a chamber inside the Capitol across the street. The man who finally won it a home was William Howard Taft, the only person to be president and then chief justice, and he did not live to see it finished. The architect was Cass Gilbert, fresh from a New York skyscraper, who gave the Court a Roman temple instead.

Walk up to where the sidewalk ends and the marble begins, and stop there.

That line is the law. The sidewalk is public ground in the fullest sense, anyone may stand on it, carry a sign, make a speech. The plaza one step up belongs to the Court, and since nineteen forty nine no assembly or display has been allowed on it. In twenty fifteen a federal appeals court was asked where the line should fall, and it said: here, where the concrete stops. One step is the difference between a public square and a courthouse.

The steps have a second story. Until twenty ten every visitor climbed them and walked in under those four words. That year the Court closed the front doors as an entrance, for security, and moved the public to doors at plaza level either side of the stairs. Two justices objected in writing, arguing the steps were not a way in but the meaning of the building. You can still come out that way. You can no longer go in.

This is a federal building on a federal plaza facing a federal hill, and it is also a corner in a neighborhood, where the sidewalk belongs to the city and the step belongs to the Court. Two cities meet here more plainly than anywhere else, because here the line between them is marked in stone and has been ruled on. Stand on the concrete. Look up at the four words. They were carved to be read from exactly where you are.
