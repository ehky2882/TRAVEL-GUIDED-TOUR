# Franklin D. Roosevelt Memorial — a room with no roof

---

In 1941 the president told a friend what he wanted, if anything: a plain block about the size of his desk, in front of the National Archives, with his name on it and nothing else. He got it, in 1965; it's still there on Pennsylvania Avenue. Thirty-two years later he also got this.

You should be at the entrance off the Tidal Basin path, facing a long low wall of rough reddish granite that runs away from you into trees, with water sounding somewhere ahead. The memorial is not a building. It is four outdoor rooms laid end to end along the shore, one for each of his terms, and you walk through them in order, west to east, the way you'd read a timeline.

Go in.

The designer, Lawrence Halprin, was a landscape architect, and it shows: there is no roof, no steps up to anything, nothing taller than the trees. Each room has a waterfall, and they change as you go — a single quiet fall for the first term, broken stepped water for the Depression years, a chaotic crash of stone and water for the war, and a still pool at the end. You hear the history before you read it.

Look at the figures as you pass: the bronze bread line in the second room, five men in overcoats; the seated president in the third, a cloak over his legs and a small dog at his feet. At the entrance, added in 2001 after a long argument, is a second statue of him in the wheelchair he used for most of his adult life and rarely let the public see. The memorial opened without it in 1997; the people who loved him most were on both sides of the question.

Nothing here rises, and that is deliberate. The whole city is kept short by a law from 1910 that ties a building's height to the width of the street it faces — street width plus twenty feet, roughly — and that law is why the skyline is a horizon and not a wall. Halprin took it further: a memorial you cannot see from across the water, only from inside.

*[beat]*

Walk it slowly and let the water change behind you. He asked for a desk-sized block. The city gave him a quarter mile of granite and never let it rise above the trees — which, in this town, is the deepest form of respect it has.
