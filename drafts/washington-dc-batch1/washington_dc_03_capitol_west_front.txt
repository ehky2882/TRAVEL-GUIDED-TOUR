# United States Capitol — the West Front, from the Mall

---

This building is here because of a dinner.

In the summer of 1790 the new country owed a great deal of money and could not agree on where to put its government. Alexander Hamilton wanted the federal treasury to take over the states' war debts. James Madison wanted the capital on the Potomac, close to Virginia. Over one dinner in New York the two of them traded, and the deal held: the debts were absorbed, and the government would move south to a site on this river, to be ready in ten years. Congress arrived in 1800 to a building that was one wing, a few boarding houses, and a lot of mud.

You should be standing on the Mall at Third Street, with the Capitol Reflecting Pool in front of you and the Capitol beyond it. Between you and the building, General Grant on his horse, flanked by cavalry and artillery in bronze — that memorial marks the foot of the Hill. Behind him the ground climbs to the terraces and then the dome. If the lower terrace is open, go up; it has often been closed off with barriers since 2021, and if it is, this is the view the building was composed for anyway.

Why here, though.

The man who laid out the city put the house of Congress on the one real hill in his plan, and the president's house on a ridge a mile and a half to the northwest, with an avenue running between them. A compromise capital got a compromise geometry: two seats of power, each on its own rise, in sight of each other and not quite together.

The dome is cast iron, close to nine million pounds of it, painted to look like the stone below. It went up during the Civil War, and the work was kept going on purpose — a government saying out loud that it expected to still be here when the fighting stopped. On top is a bronze figure nearly twenty feet tall, called Freedom, facing east, away from you, toward the sunrise.

The man who worked out how to take her plaster model apart so she could be cast in pieces was Philip Reid, who was enslaved, and who was paid only for the work he did on Sundays, because Sundays were his own. Enslaved people quarried and hauled the sandstone in the walls beneath the dome. Inside, two blocks of that original stone carry a marker placed in 2012 that says so.

On January sixth, 2021, a crowd broke into this building through this side of it.

[beat]

The thing about a compromise is that nobody got what they wanted, and it worked anyway. Hamilton's debt deal gave the country its credit. Madison's river got the city. The building that came out of the bargain has been burned, rebuilt, re-domed, added onto, and broken into, and the ten-year deal that put it here has now run on this hill for more than two hundred and twenty-five years. The argument moved in before the roof was on, and it has not left, which is more or less what the place was built for.
