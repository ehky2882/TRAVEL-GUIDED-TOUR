Nearly every big house on this avenue was built by a fortune and sold by a crash.

You should be at Sheridan Circle, on Massachusetts Avenue a half mile northwest of doo pont Circle, where the avenue starts to climb. In the middle of the circle a general on horseback, in bronze, reins in hard. Around the circle and along the avenue in both directions are houses the size of small hotels, limestone, brick, iron gates, and most of them fly a flag that is not this country's. Behind you, downhill, the avenue runs back toward doo pont. Ahead, uphill, it climbs toward the National Cathedral, two miles on.

The houses are the show. The flags are the point.

Take the house with the flag of Indonesia, back toward doo pont at the corner of Twenty first Street: a huge, curving pile of stone. It was built in nineteen oh three by a man who had struck gold in Colorado, and his daughter later bought the Hope Diamond and wore it to parties here. The money went, the house was sold in nineteen fifty one to a country only a few years old, and it has been an embassy ever since. That is the pattern on this avenue. The mansions went up between the eighteen eighties and the First World War, when this hill was where new money built. The crash of nineteen twenty nine emptied them, and foreign governments, which needed grand houses near the capital, bought in at Depression prices. The horseman is from nineteen oh eight, by the sculptor who later carved four presidents into a mountain in South Dakota.

Each of those flags marks something odd about the ground. The house behind it is, by treaty, a place no one from this city's government, or this country's, can enter uninvited; the ground is American and the rules inside are not. Washington has its federal city and its local one, and up this hill it has a third kind of ground in several dozen pieces, each one a house's worth of somewhere else. Up the avenue, past the mosque with the single minaret, the climb ends at a circle around an observatory, and the white house inside the fence there is the Vice President's.

The hill was built to show off, and it still does. Only the owners changed, and what they are showing off is not money anymore but presence, every flag here is a country saying it has a seat in this city. The houses were built for exactly that, by people who meant something else by it.
