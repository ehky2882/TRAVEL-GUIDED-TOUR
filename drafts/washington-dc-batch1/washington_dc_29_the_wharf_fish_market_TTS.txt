Fish have been sold on this piece of waterfront since eighteen oh five.

You should be standing on Maine Avenue Southwest where the barges are, a row of long, flat boats tied along the seawall, each one a market stall under a roof, with the Washington Channel beyond and a long green park across the water. The counters are on the boats. Depending on the tide, the people behind them are above you or below you. Walk along until you can see the whole row, and stop.

The claim you will hear is that this is the oldest open air fish market in the country still running, and it is probably true, though what you are looking at is not what stood here in eighteen oh five. Then it was a landing where boats came up the river with the catch. For a century and a half it was sheds and stalls on the shore, with a market hall behind. The boats stopped coming when the fish began arriving by truck. The sheds are gone because of the nineteen fifties.

That decade the city decided to clear nearly all of Southwest, the neighborhood between here and the Capitol, most of it poor, much of it Black, all of it old, and start again. Most of a neighborhood was moved out and its streets erased. The fish market was supposed to go too. The vendors would not leave. The compromise, in nineteen sixty, was to put them on the water: barges tied to the wall, which were technically not on the land being cleared. The longest running stall has been here since nineteen thirty nine, and afloat for most of that.

Now look past the barges. The mile of new buildings along the channel, hotels, piers, music halls, apartments, was finished in twenty twenty two, and the same developers hold the lease on the market. They rebuilt the old oyster shed behind you, added a market hall, and left the boats where they were, because the boats were the point.

The rivers worked before the city did. Long before anyone drew a Mall, the water was how things arrived, fish, flour, stone, people, and the shore was where the work happened. Most of that waterfront has been tidied into promenades. This stretch kept its job. The stalls sit on the tide, and when you buy a crab here you are paying into something that has not stopped since the first one was sold.
