# Dupont Circle

---

Get to the middle of the circle before you do anything else. Everything here depends on it.

You are in Dupont Circle, the round park at the meeting of Massachusetts, Connecticut and New Hampshire Avenues with Nineteenth and P Streets. Stand by the fountain in the center — a white marble basin, a single column, three carved figures around the shaft. Turn slowly. Ten streets leave this circle, spaced around you like the hours on a clock face. Connecticut Avenue is the busiest: it runs under the circle in a tunnel, so the cars you hear on it are below you, and the ones you see are on the ring road at the edge.

A circle is what the 1791 plan does whenever one of its long diagonal avenues meets the ordinary grid of lettered and numbered streets. Two systems were laid on top of each other — a grid for the blocks, diagonals for the long views — and wherever they collided the plan drew a circle or a square, so the collision became a place instead of a problem. The plan drew dozens of these. Most were never built or were later filled in. This one was, and was kept, and so you are standing in the device itself: the thing that lets Massachusetts Avenue run from one side of the city to the other without once meeting a corner.

The fountain is from 1921. The three figures are the sea, the stars and the wind — the things a sailor needed — and they were carved by the sculptor who made the seated Lincoln at the far end of the Mall, working with the same architect. Before the fountain there was a bronze statue of the sailor himself: a Civil War admiral named Du Pont, put here in 1884 by the government, which named the circle for him. His family preferred something grander, paid for the fountain, and had the admiral shipped to Delaware, where he still stands.

Under your feet, the other way, is a streetcar station. It opened in 1949, closed in 1962 when the city gave up streetcars, sat sealed for half a century, and is used for art shows. The entrances are the walled stairways at the edge of the circle, on the Connecticut Avenue side.

[beat]

An intersection is a place where you decide which way to go. The plan took the busiest ones and made them somewhere you could stop. This one has chess tables.
