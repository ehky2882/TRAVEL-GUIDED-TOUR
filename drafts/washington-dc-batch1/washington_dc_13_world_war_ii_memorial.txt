# World War II Memorial — the newest thing on the oldest line

---

Of the three great stops on this axis, this is the youngest by eight decades: it opened in 2004, in the one place the Mall's guardians had spent a century arguing nothing should ever be built: the pool on the direct sightline between the Monument and Lincoln. They lost that argument, and most of them later decided it had turned out fine.

You should be at the rim of the oval plaza, with the fountains in the center, the Monument rising behind you to the east and the Reflecting Pool beginning just beyond the far side. Two tall arched pavilions face each other across the water — Atlantic to your left if you're facing west, Pacific to your right — and between them a ring of fifty-six granite pillars, one for each state and territory of the time, each with a bronze wreath.

Take the pillars first.

They are set in the order the states joined the union, alternating north and south around the oval, so that neighbors on the ring are rarely neighbors on the map. The architect, Friedrich St. Florian, wanted the war remembered as something the whole country did at once, and the ring is how he said it: no center, no front rank, every pillar the same height.

Now walk toward the far side, past the fountains, to the low curved wall under the Lincoln end. The gold stars there are the memorial's way of counting what the war cost, and it is done without a number — the stars stand in for the dead, and the water in front of them is kept still so you can see them twice.

One more thing, and you'll have to hunt for it. Behind each pavilion, tucked where a service door would be, someone carved a small cartoon face peering over a line — the doodle soldiers chalked on walls across Europe and the Pacific, with its three-word caption. The builders put two of them here, half hidden, on purpose.

*[beat]*

What makes this place work is who visits. On most clear mornings, groups of very old men and women arrive together, flown in for the day from somewhere else in the country, and move slowly through the ring looking for their state. The memorial was built as a monument; it is used as a meeting place. That was the Mall's lesson from the wall down the path, and this one took it.
