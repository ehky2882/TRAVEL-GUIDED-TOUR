The canal under this bridge was supposed to reach Ohio. It got about halfway.

You should be on the Thomas Jefferson Street bridge, one block south of M Street, looking down into a stone walled channel. The towpath runs along the far side, with the backs of old brick warehouses and mills rising straight from the water. A wooden lock gate is a little way along to your right, with the small brick visitor center beside it. Behind you, up the slope, is M Street. Ahead and below, two blocks further on, is the puh toe mik. If there is no water in the channel today, that is repair work on the walls, not neglect, the canal has been drained and refilled more than once in its life.

A correction, before anything else. Washington was not built on a swamp. It was built on farmland and low hills a few miles downstream of a port, and the port came first. Georgetown was laid out in seventeen fifty one, as a Maryland tobacco town, at the last point on the puh toe mik a seagoing ship could reach, the rapids begin a few miles upstream. Forty years later the federal city was drawn in next door, downstream, where there was room. The capital is here because this landing was here. That is the whole reason, and it is still visible: walk up to M Street and the buildings are older, lower, closer together, and the streets do not quite line up with the ones on the other side of Rock Creek.

The canal was Georgetown's attempt to stay a port once the river stopped cooperating. Silt was closing the channel, and the money was moving to Baltimore. So in eighteen twenty eight a president turned the first spade of earth for a canal that would run from this spot to the Ohio River and bring the whole interior down to the Georgetown wharves. On the same day, in Baltimore, another group broke ground on a railroad heading to the same place. The railroad got there. The canal reached kum ber lund, Maryland, in eighteen fifty, a hundred and eighty four miles, seventy four locks, and stopped, and spent the next seventy years carrying coal downhill to a town that had already lost the race. A flood closed it in nineteen twenty four.

The towpath under you is a national park, the full length, because in nineteen fifty four a Supreme Court justice walked all hundred and eighty four miles of it with a group of reporters to stop it being paved into a road. The mills along the water became offices and apartments. The brick stayed. Nothing on M Street rises very high. The law in this city would keep it that way, but here it was already true.

Georgetown had forty years as the only town on this stretch of river, and has spent the rest as the older neighbor of the city that grew up downstream to replace it. The river is quieter than it was, and the town that started all this is still the oldest thing in sight.
