# Jefferson Memorial — the fight about trees

---

This building was a fight about trees. In 1938, when the bulldozers arrived to clear this bank, a group of women chained themselves to the cherry trees to stop it, and for a few days the question of whether the third president got a memorial here came down to how many trees it would cost. He got it; so, mostly, did they — the trees were moved rather than felled, and the ones along the water now are their descendants and replacements.

You should be on the north steps, the Tidal Basin at your back, facing the round white portico with the dome behind it. Turn around to see why the spot mattered more than the trees: straight across the water is the Monument, and behind it the White House. You are standing on the southern end of a line that was drawn in 1791 and left empty for a hundred and fifty years.

Now the building itself.

John Russell Pope designed it as a Roman temple — shallow dome, ring of columns, open to the air — because Jefferson had designed his own house and his university that way and would have recognized it. Pope died in 1937 before a stone was laid; the memorial opened in 1943, mid-war, with a plaster stand-in where the statue belonged because the bronze was needed elsewhere. The nineteen-foot figure arrived in 1947. Jefferson faces north, toward the house he once lived in.

Inside, the walls carry his words, edited — the committee trimmed and stitched passages to fit the panels, and scholars have pointed out the seams ever since.

Then look down. Everything under you is fill. The Tidal Basin was dug in the 1880s and this shore was made from what came out of the river; the memorial sits on pilings driven through it to firm ground, and it has settled and been shored up more than once. A marble temple on a riverbed — the plan asked for a terminus here, and the engineers manufactured a place to put it.

*[beat]*

So this is where the city's great cross finally closed: the Capitol to the Monument to the Lincoln Memorial, east to west, and the White House to the Monument to Jefferson, north to south. It took until the nineteen forties, a war, a dead architect, and a stand-off over cherry trees. Look back along the line from the steps: the whole plan is visible from here, and from nowhere else.
