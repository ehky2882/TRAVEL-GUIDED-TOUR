# Tidal Basin — the machine and the trees

---

The water in front of you is a machine. Twice a day the river rises, gates on the far side open and let it in, then close; when the tide drops, gates on this side open and let it out through the channel to your left — and the rush scours the channel clean. The Tidal Basin was dug in the 1880s to keep a harbor from silting. The view came later.

You should be on the shore path, with the Jefferson Memorial across the water or beside you, depending on where you've joined, and the Monument to the north. The path has been rebuilt — wider than it used to be, and higher, on a seawall finished in 2025 and 2026 after the old one sank by more than a man's height in places and let the river onto the walkway every afternoon. The young trees along the new edge were planted to replace the ones the work took out; give them a decade.

Now the trees.

They are Japanese flowering cherries, mostly a variety called Yoshino, and they are here because of a woman named Eliza Scidmore who came back from Japan in 1885 and spent twenty-four years asking anyone who would listen to plant some. The first shipment, in 1910, arrived full of insects and disease and was burned on the Mall. The second, in 1912, three thousand trees, came from the mayor of Tokyo, and two of those originals still stand near the little stone lantern a short walk up the shore. In 1965 Tokyo sent more. In 1981 Washington sent cuttings back, when a flood had damaged the parent trees in Japan.

The bloom lasts about ten days, somewhere between late March and mid-April, and the city plans a festival around a date it cannot know in advance. Everything else here — the paddle boats, the bridge, the pink haze on every phone for two weeks a year — grew up around a drainage project.

*[beat]*

So read the Basin in order. First a harbor that needed flushing, then a shore that needed something on it, then a present that needed a place to go. The engineering was never meant to be looked at. It is the part of all this that has worked without a pause for a hundred and forty years — and the trees, which were always meant to be looked at, have to be replaced one by one.
