# Vietnam Veterans Memorial — the wall that answers

---

The ground drops away as you walk in, and the wall rises to meet you. By the time you reach the center you are ten feet below the lawn, and the Monument and the Lincoln Memorial — the two arms of the wall point at them — are the only parts of the city still visible over the top.

You should be on the paved path, walking down the slope along the black granite, with the Reflecting Pool behind you through the trees. The two wings meet at an angle; the names begin at the center and run outward, date by date, so that the first and last casualties sit side by side at the apex. From the west end you move forward in time; from the east end, backward.

Watch the stone first.

It is polished until it mirrors. You read a name and see your own face behind it, and the trees, and whoever is standing next to you. That was the point. The designer was a twenty-one-year-old architecture student named Maya Lin, who won an open competition in 1981 with a drawing many people hated — too dark, too low, no flag, no figures. Both were added nearby within three years, as a compromise, and the memorial has held the two ideas together ever since: her wall below the grass, the three bronze soldiers standing at the tree line, looking at it.

Now look at the base of the wall. Letters, photographs, a folded shirt, a can of beer, dog tags, a child's drawing. Every object left here is collected by the Park Service, cataloged, and kept — the collection runs to the hundreds of thousands and nothing is thrown away except the flowers. No one asked for this, and few memorials anywhere are answered this way.

The wall carries more than fifty-eight thousand names. Say that once and leave it; the stone does the rest.

*[beat]*

Here is what this place proved, and the whole Mall learned from it. A memorial does not have to tell you how to feel. It can be a surface — low, dark, quiet — and let people bring what they bring. The things at your feet are the proof that they do. Walk the length of it slowly and come up the far ramp into the daylight, and the city will feel briefly louder than it is.
