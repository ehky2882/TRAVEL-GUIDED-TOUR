# African American Civil War Memorial

---

There are more than two hundred thousand names on the wall behind that statue, and not one of them is a count of the dead.

You should be at the top of the Metro escalators where Vermont Avenue meets U Street, at Tenth. The two streets cross at an angle and leave a wedge of plaza between them. The bronze figures in the middle of the wedge are the statue. The low curved walls of dark polished stone behind them, in a half circle, carry the names. Across U Street, the red brick school with the tall windows is the museum.

The names are the memorial. The statue is the way in.

The statue was dedicated in 1998 and made by Ed Hamilton, a sculptor from Kentucky. On the street side are soldiers and a sailor in uniform, ready to go. Walk around it and the back is a family: a man leaving, a woman, children. The war is on one face and the house is on the other, and the sculptor put the house where the names could see it.

The names are the United States Colored Troops, the Black soldiers and sailors of the Union army and navy, listed by regiment, with the officers who led them. They are service rolls, not casualty lists, which is why this one place in the city can give you a number. The first of those regiments was raised here, in Washington, in 1863, and drilled on an island in the Potomac off Georgetown before it marched.

The man who argued hardest for letting Black men enlist lived across the river, in the house on the hill in Anacostia. Frederick Douglass recruited for these regiments once the government agreed, and two of his sons signed up with one of the first. The museum that keeps the records is in the old school building across the street.

Washington's other war memorials are on the Mall, among the departments that fought the wars. This one was put at the top of a subway exit in the neighborhood Black Washington built for itself, and that is the right address. It is the one national memorial to these men, and it is a hundred and thirty-three years late, which the people who built it knew.

[beat]

Most of the men on that wall came home. That is what a service roll means, and it is why this is the one memorial in the city where the number is a comfort rather than a weight.
