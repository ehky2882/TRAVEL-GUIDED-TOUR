Everything that matters inside this building is kept in the dark.

You should be standing on the north side of Constitution Avenue, between Seventh and Ninth Streets, looking across at a temple: a flight of broad steps, a portico of eight columns, a carved pediment, bronze doors. Two seated figures of stone flank the steps, each on a block the size of a small house. The one on your right, as you face the building, is a woman with a child and a sheaf of wheat; on your left, a man with a helmet and a sword across his knees.

Why a temple?

Because the architect, John Russell Pope, was asked in the nineteen thirties for a building to hold the country's papers and understood the request as a shrine. He gave it the bones he would give the Jefferson Memorial a few years later, columns, pediments, a dome you cannot see from the street, with the public rooms on the avenue and twenty one levels of stacks behind them in a block that reads as a windowless wall. It opened in nineteen thirty five on the site of the city's central market, which fed Washington for sixty years and was demolished for this.

The two seated figures are called Heritage and Guardianship. The inscription under the man reads: Eternal vigilance is the price of liberty. Around the corner, on the Pennsylvania Avenue side, two more figures flank the other steps, and one of them carries a line from Shakespeare: What is past is prologue. Four statues, four mottoes, two fronts, and the building has no back.

Here is the part worth standing still for. Inside, in a hall kept dim, under glass that filters the light, are three documents, and one is the Constitution. In seventeen eighty seven it set aside, in a single sentence, ground for the seat of government, not more than ten miles on a side, to be chosen later. The dinner that chose the river came three years after; the city came after that. The paper inside this building authorized the ground it sits on.

So the compromise that put the capital here runs in a straight line: a clause, a deal, a survey, a city, and finally this vault on the avenue, holding the clause. Pope built the temple around the sentence. You do not need to go in to feel the weight of it. Few buildings anywhere hold something older than the city around them, and this one is standing in the middle of the result.
