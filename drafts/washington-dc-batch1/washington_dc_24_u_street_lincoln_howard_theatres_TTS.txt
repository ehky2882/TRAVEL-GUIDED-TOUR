The reason for this street is a mile up the hill, out of sight.

Howard University was chartered by Congress in eighteen sixty seven, two years after the war ended, and named for the general who ran the Freedmen's Bureau. It brought teachers, doctors, lawyers and several thousand students to the slope north of here, and a neighborhood grew downhill from it to house them, feed them, and give them somewhere to go on a Saturday night. You are standing in that neighborhood.

You should be on U Street at Thirteenth, on the south sidewalk, looking across at the north side of the block. The theater with the vertical sign reading Lincoln is the Lincoln Theatre, from nineteen twenty two. Beside it is a chili counter that opened in nineteen fifty eight. Down at the corner of Twelfth, the tall brick building with the arched windows is the True Reformer Building, and it is the oldest of the three.

Three buildings to keep straight: the one on the corner, the one across the street, and the one you cannot see from here.

The corner building went up in nineteen oh three for a Black fraternal order, and it is said to be the first building in the country designed, financed, built and owned entirely by Black Americans. The architect was a Black man from Virginia. In the hall upstairs, a teenager named Duke Ellington played some of his first paid dates. He had been born in this city in eighteen ninety nine and raised on T Street, one block behind you.

The one you cannot see is the Howard Theatre, four blocks east at Seventh and T, built in nineteen ten and among the first large theaters in the country built for Black audiences. The Lincoln across from you came twelve years later, a movie palace with a dance hall behind it.

Here is why there were two. The theaters downtown would not seat Black Washingtonians, or sent them to the balcony, so this corridor built its own, and everyone who played the circuit played here. Ellington, and all who came after him. People called the street Black Broadway. The name came from outside, and the street answered to it.

The neighborhood kept growing. In nineteen fifty seven Washington became, by most reckonings, the first large American city with a Black majority, and it stayed one until twenty eleven.

Then the news came from Memphis on the evening of April fourth, nineteen sixty eight, and by the next day the fires had started at Fourteenth and U, one block west of where you stand, and spread along Fourteenth, Seventh and H Streets. What burned here took decades to come back. The Lincoln went dark, and so did the Howard, and the chili counter stayed open through all of it. The subway arrived in nineteen ninety one, the Lincoln was restored with city money in nineteen ninety four, and the Howard reopened in twenty twelve after a long time shut.

The theaters on this street were built because the doors downtown were closed. Both sets of doors are open, and the marquee in front of you is lit most nights of the week. A name given to a street under a condition nobody would choose has outlasted the condition, and the street is wearing it.
