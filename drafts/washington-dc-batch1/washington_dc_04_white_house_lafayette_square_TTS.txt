This is as close as the city gets.

You should be standing in Lafayette Square, the seven acre park north of the White House, facing south. Between you and the house is Pennsylvania Avenue, closed to cars since nineteen ninety five and paved as a walkway, then a fence, then the north lawn, then the north front: a porch of columns, a fan window over the door, white stone. In the middle of the square behind you, a general on a rearing horse, Andrew Jackson, eighteen fifty three. At the four corners, four foreign officers of the Revolution, Lafayette among them. To your right, a small yellow church with a gold dome: Saint John's, eighteen sixteen.

The site was chosen by George Washington, who never lived here. He picked the ridge, a mile and a half from the Capitol, because the plan wanted the two seats of government apart and in sight of each other, and he picked the architect, James ho bun, an Irishman who had learned his trade in Dublin and who modeled this on a duke's house there. Enslaved people quarried the sandstone and laid much of it, alongside hired hands and Scottish masons. The second president moved in during November eighteen hundred, to wet plaster and no proper stairs, and the capital had arrived, ten years after the deal that placed it.

The British burned it in eighteen fourteen. ho bun rebuilt it. The porch you are looking at was added afterward, and the color that gives it its name was there from the beginning, a whitewash over the stone, long before the name became official in nineteen oh one.

Turn around and look at the square itself. From the first day the city was laid out, it was two kinds of ground on one plan: the public reservations, the squares, the avenues, the Mall, this park, kept by the government, and the lots between them sold off to private owners to pay for the public buildings. That was the founding arrangement, written into the land before a wall went up. This square was carved off the president's own grounds when the avenue was cut through, and it has been the city's front room to the federal house ever since: a public park facing a private residence that belongs to the public. People come here to be seen from that porch. They have for two hundred years.

Two more facts and no more. The two blocks of Sixteenth Street north of this square were painted with words in June twenty twenty; the painting was removed in March twenty twenty five. On the east side of the grounds, out of your view, the East Wing was taken down in twenty twenty five and a new building is going up on its site.

The house was built to be looked at from where you are standing, and the city was laid out so this square would be where the looking happens. Everything between you and the porch, the closed avenue, the fence, the lawn, is distance the house has acquired over two centuries. The view has not moved. Stand here a while. It is the plainest arrangement in the city: the house, and the people in the park across from it.
