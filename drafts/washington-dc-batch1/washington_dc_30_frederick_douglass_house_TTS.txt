From up here, Washington is something you look across at.

You should be standing on the lawn at the top of the hill in front of a white house with a deep porch, columns, and a row of tall windows, Cedar Hill. Turn your back to the house. Below you is anna kostia, the neighborhood, with its rowhouses and church steeples; beyond it the river; beyond that, small and clear on a good day, the dome of the Capitol. The house can only be entered on a ranger tour with a ticket, and the tickets are free and go quickly. The grounds are open, and the view is the reason he bought the place.

Frederick Douglass was born enslaved on Maryland's Eastern Shore, escaped at twenty, and spent the rest of his life becoming one of the most listened to men in America, writer, orator, publisher, adviser to presidents. In eighteen seventy seven he was nearly sixty, newly appointed United States Marshal for the District, and looking for a house. This one was for sale. The deeds in this neighborhood, then called Uniontown, said no Black person could buy here. He bought it anyway, and lived in it for the last seventeen years of his life.

Walk around to the side yard. The small stone building with one door and one window is the Growlery, his word, a one room study where he went to work and to be left alone. The original fell down; this one stands on its footprint.

Now look back at the river. The anna kostia was the deep water when the city was laid out, the branch where a navy yard went in seventeen ninety nine, where ships could actually come up, and the farms upstream silted it in within a century. The grand side of the city faced the other river. This side did the work: the yard, the wharves, the ferry below you. Douglass chose the working side and set his porch to face the dome.

He held three federal appointments in this city, marshal, recorder of deeds, minister to Haiti, and he came home every night to a hill the city's own deeds said he could not own. When he died here in eighteen ninety five, his widow kept the house exactly as it was so people could see it, and they have, for more than a hundred and twenty five years. Stand where he stood. The dome is where he left it. So is the distance.
