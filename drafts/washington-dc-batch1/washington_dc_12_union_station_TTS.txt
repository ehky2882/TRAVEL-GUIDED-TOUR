Everyone who arrives in Washington by train is handed the same first view, and it was chosen for them in nineteen oh two.

You should be standing in Columbus Circle, in front of the station, with the fountain behind you and the long white facade ahead, three great arches in the middle, a colonnade either side, six stone figures along the roofline. Turn around for a moment. Straight down the avenue behind you is the Capitol dome. Then turn back.

Nineteen oh two.

That year a commission led by a senator named McMillan produced the second plan for the city, the first one, lahnfahnt's, had been half built and then ignored for most of a century. By nineteen hundred the Mall had a railroad running across it, with a station and train shed on the open ground below the Capitol. The McMillan Plan said, politely, that this would not do. The railroads agreed to leave the Mall if they were given one grand terminal to share, north of the Capitol. Daniel Burnham, who sat on the commission and did not believe in small plans, designed it himself. It opened in nineteen oh seven. The tracks came off the Mall. The view you just turned to see, the dome, down a clear avenue from the front door, was the payment.

The building is Rome by way of Chicago: the three arches from a triumphal arch, the vaulted hall inside from the ancient baths. The six figures on the roof, by Louis saint gaw dens, stand for things like fire, electricity, and agriculture, which tells you what a railroad in nineteen oh seven thought it was made of. The fountain behind you, Columbus at the prow of a stone ship, came in nineteen twelve.

One more thing about the floor in there. In January nineteen fifty three an express from Boston came in with its brakes gone, crossed the concourse, and dropped through the floor into the baggage room below. No one was killed. The hole was planked over, the inauguration crowds walked across it that week, and the locomotive came out in pieces afterward.

The station has been owned by the federal government since the nineteen eighties and run by it directly again since twenty twenty five; the hall is protected, and the tracks and sheds behind it are being replanned. None of that changes what Burnham built. He gave a country that arrived by rail a front door scaled to the idea of itself, and pointed it at the dome, so that the first thing anyone saw of Washington was the reason for Washington. The station is only the frame around it.
