# Eastern Market

---

Four blocks from the floor of the House of Representatives, someone will sell you a chicken.

You should be standing on Seventh Street Southeast, outside a long, low building of red brick with arched windows and a steep roof, between C Street and North Carolina Avenue. On a weekend the street is closed to cars and full of stalls — produce at one end, crafts and a flea market at the other — and the building is the still point in the middle. On a weekday it is quieter, and you can see the building.

Okay.

It opened in 1873, designed by Adolf Cluss, a German engineer who had left after the failed revolutions of 1848 and who, before he was an architect in Washington, had been a correspondent of Karl Marx. He built a good deal of this city in red brick — schools, a museum on the Mall, a downtown market that is gone — and this is the one still doing its job. Inside, one tall room with a wood ceiling, and counters selling meat, fish, cheese, bread, and flowers, which is roughly what they sold in 1873. In 2007 a fire took the roof. The city rebuilt it, reopened it in 2009, and the vendors came back.

Now look at where you are. The Capitol is a short walk west, past the Library and the House office buildings. Everything on that side of Second Street is federal ground, owned and run by Congress. Everything on this side is the District: rowhouses, corner stores, a market that belongs to the city and leases its counters to butchers. The name Capitol Hill means a workplace to half the country and a neighborhood to the people who live on it. Both are right, and both are standing on the same brick.

The market was planned, not accidental. The original layout of the city set aside ground for markets the way it set aside ground for Congress, and this one took its name from the eastern river. It has outlived its downtown sibling, a fire, and a century and a half of changes in how the city eats.

[beat]

So the Hill is two places, and this is where you stand in the second and can still walk to the first. Buy something. Eat it on the steps. The people around you are doing the plainest ordinary thing a city does, a few hundred yards from the most argued-over building in the country, and nobody here finds that strange.
