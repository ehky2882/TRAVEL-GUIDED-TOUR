MUMBAI, 18, KAA LAA GO DAA
---
kaa laa go daa means black horse. For more than fifty years, there wasn't one.

You're at the kaa laa go daa junction, in the middle of Bombay's art district, beside a black horse on a plinth, standing, saddled, and quite riderless. Around you: Victorian stone buildings, galleries, cafes, and, if you look south, the dome of the city's great museum behind its gardens.

Here's the story of the name. In the late nineteenth century, the Sassoon family, bag daa dee Jewish merchants who did a great deal to build this part of the city, presented a black stone statue of King Edward the Seventh on horseback, as Prince of Wales. It was everyone's landmark, so the district took its name. In nineteen sixty five, with independent India removing its colonial statues, the king and his horse were taken down and moved to the gardens at bye kulla, where you can still visit them. The neighbourhood kept the name and lost the horse. Then in twenty seventeen, the kaa laa go daa Association commissioned this one, the Spirit of kaa laa go daa, without a king.

Have a look around the junction. juh haan geer Art Gallery, the city's best known exhibition space. The David Sassoon Library. The pale blue front of kuh ness eth elly yah hoo Synagogue, a short walk away. And Esplanade Mansion, once Watson's Hotel, which is said to be the oldest surviving cast iron building in India.

And here's the thing. A horse without its rider is a rather neat image for what this city did after empire. It kept the buildings, the streets and the names, and quietly took the king off the top.

If you're here in February, the kaa laa go daa Arts Festival takes over these streets for nine days. At any other time of year, walk down vee bee gaan dee marg to the synagogue, past the old Rhythm House corner and the galleries, a whole afternoon within five minutes of this horse.
