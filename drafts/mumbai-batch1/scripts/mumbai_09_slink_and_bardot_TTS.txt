MUMBAI, 09, SLINK AND BARDOT
---
To find one of the smartest bars in Mumbai, you have to walk into a fishing village. That's rather the point.

You're in wor lee koh lee waa daa, the koh lee fishing village at the northern tip of wor lee, on the road that runs opposite the Indian Coast Guard station. In front of you, an old colonial bungalow called taa daa nee House. Inside it is Slink and Bardot. Behind you, the lanes of the village run down to the boats.

Have a look at the bungalow first. It's been refurbished rather than replaced, which, in wor lee, is the exception rather than the rule. Inside, it's a French bar and kitchen, with a speakeasy feel: low light, booths, lounge seating, a series of connected rooms, and music that starts unobtrusive and gets louder as the night goes on, with dee jays at the weekend. The drinks are French classics and aperitifs, the French seventy five is the one regulars point you to, and the food is small French plates.

Now look the other way, at the village. The koh leez are among the oldest inhabitants of the seven islands that became Bombay; the city's name, and the goddess moom baa day vee, belong to their world before they belonged to anyone else's. This koh lee waa daa still fishes. In the morning you'll see nets drying and boats coming in, a few hundred metres from tower blocks and the Sea Link.

And here's the thing. There are two Mumbais within a hundred yards of where you're standing, a bar where a cocktail costs more than a fisherman might take home in a day, and a village that was here first. Neither is going anywhere soon. Being honest about that, standing here, is part of seeing the city properly.

Walk on into the village while it's light. At the end of the lanes, on the point, is the little wor lee Fort, put up by the British in the late seventeenth century, with one of the best views of the Sea Link in the city.
