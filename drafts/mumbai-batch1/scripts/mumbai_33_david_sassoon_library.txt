MUMBAI — 33 — DAVID SASSOON LIBRARY AND READING ROOM
Vantage: on Mahatma Gandhi Road in Kala Ghoda, on the pavement in front of the library's entrance portico
---
This building was put up for the mechanics of the Mint and the Dockyard. These days the members come for the quiet.

Mahatma Gandhi Road, Kala Ghoda. In front of you, a building in pale yellow stone, with pointed arches, a steep roof, and a portico over the entrance. Above the portico, a white marble bust of a bearded man. That's David Sassoon. Inside, at the foot of the stairs, he appears again, full length, in marble.

The story begins in eighteen forty-seven, when European workers at the government Mint and Dockyard set up a Mechanics' Institution — evening lectures and a library for men who worked with their hands. They needed a home. David Sassoon, a Baghdadi Jewish merchant who had made his fortune in Bombay, gave sixty thousand rupees, and the government paid the rest. The building went up between eighteen sixty-seven and eighteen seventy, designed by J. Campbell and G. E. Gosling, in yellow stone quarried at Malad. In time the mechanics drifted away, and by the nineteen twenties it had become the David Sassoon Library and Reading Room.

Have a look at the columns of the porch, and the carving around the arches. Look closely, and you'll find animals worked into the stone. Then look at the roof. That steep slope is new, and old at the same time. In the nineteen sixties the original pitched roof was taken off and replaced with a flat concrete one, which leaked for half a century. In a restoration finished in twenty twenty-three, led by the conservation architect Abha Narain Lambah, the sloping roof went back on. They kept the old Minton floor tiles, the antique switches and the chandeliers, and rehoused nearly thirty thousand books.

[beat]

And that's what I like about this place. It isn't a monument. It's a working library, open long hours, where people still come to sit and read a newspaper on the balcony, as members have done for a century and a half.

The reading rooms are for members, but visitors can usually step into the ground floor and the garden behind, which is one of the calmest spots in the Fort. When you come out, Jehangir Art Gallery is just across the road, and the black horse of Kala Ghoda a minute's walk away.
