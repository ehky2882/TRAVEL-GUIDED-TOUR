# Mumbai 30 · Girgaon Chowpatty

*Vantage: on the sand at Girgaon Chowpatty, at the northern end of Marine Drive, facing the sea* · 335 words · about 134 s

Under the sand you're standing on, there's a motorway. That's not the strangest thing about this beach.

You're on the sand at Girgaon Chowpatty, at the northern end of Marine Drive, where the Queen's Necklace runs out at the foot of Malabar Hill. Behind you, a row of food stalls. Ahead, the Arabian Sea. To your left, the wooded hill with its mansions.

Have a look at the stalls first. Chowpatty is where the city comes to eat in the evening: bhel puri, the puffed-rice snack tossed with chutney, onion and sev; pav bhaji, a buttery vegetable mash with soft rolls; roasted corn on the cob; kulfi. Nobody comes here to swim. People come to eat, sit, walk and look at the sea.

Now the gift detail. In twenty twenty-four, Mumbai opened the first phase of its Coastal Road. To get it from Marine Drive to the north, engineers drove twin tunnels under Malabar Hill and under this beach — about twenty-five metres below the sand at Chowpatty. Every few seconds, beneath your feet, a car is passing.

And then there's the festival. Every year, at the end of Ganesh Chaturthi, hundreds of thousands of people come here with idols of the god Ganesh — some small enough to carry, some several storeys high on trucks — and immerse them in the sea. It's the single biggest crowd you'll see in Mumbai, and it happens on this sand.

[beat]

This has always been a beach for the whole city. Bal Gangadhar Tilak, the nationalist leader who did more than anyone to turn Ganesh Chaturthi into a public festival, was cremated here in nineteen twenty, and his statue stands nearby. It's fitting, really — the man who brought the festival into the street is remembered on the beach where it ends.

If you've the legs for it, climb the hill behind you to the Hanging Gardens on Malabar Hill. From the top, looking back at the bay after dark, you'll see the whole Queen's Necklace at once.
