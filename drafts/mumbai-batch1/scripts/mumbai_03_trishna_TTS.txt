MUMBAI, 03, TRISH NAA
---
The ropes are long gone from Rope Walk Lane. The crab, mercifully, is not.

You're on sigh baa baa marg, a narrow back street in kaa laa go daa, older maps call it Rope Walk Lane, after the ropemakers who once worked the length of it. Look for a modest doorway with the name trish naa above it. Nothing about the frontage tells you that this is one of the most talked about seafood rooms in the city. That's rather the charm.

trish naa serves the coastal cooking of mang guh lore and the kong kun shore, and has done so, by most accounts, since the nineteen sixties. Have a look at the menu if it's on display, and find the line that matters: butter, pepper and garlic crab. A whole crab, cracked, drowned in butter, sharp with garlic and black pepper, eaten with your hands. Regulars call ahead to make sure the crab has come in; when it hasn't, there's simply no crab.

Then the rest of the board, prawns koh lee waa daa, fried crisp in the style of the city's koh lee fishing villages; tandoori fish; raa vus, which is Indian salmon, cooked in a dozen ways. None of it is fussy. All of it is about what came out of the sea that morning.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that the name has travelled further than the room. In two thousand and eight a restaurant called trish naa opened in London, crab and all, and was taken by many British diners as a discovery. It was a homage. This is the original, still in its back street, still small, still full.

If you can't get a table, walk to the end of the lane and turn towards the kaa laa go daa junction, you'll be in the middle of the city's gallery quarter within a minute, and the queue may well have shortened by the time you come back.
