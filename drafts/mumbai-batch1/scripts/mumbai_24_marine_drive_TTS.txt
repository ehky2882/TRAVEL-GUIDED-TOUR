MUMBAI, 24, MARINE DRIVE
---
Behind you is the sea. In front of you is one of the largest collections of Art Deco buildings anywhere in the world.

You're on the promenade at Marine Drive, with the sea wall at your back and the six lane road in front of you. The promenade runs for about three kilometres in a long curve, from naa ree mun Point at the southern end to chow puttee Beach at the foot of Malabar Hill.

Have a look across the road. That row of cream, pale yellow and white apartment blocks, rounded corners, long balconies, horizontal bands, names on the fronts like kuh poor muh hul, zuh ver muh hul, kay vul muh hul, is Art Deco. Most of it went up from the late nineteen thirties, built largely by wealthy Parsi families on freshly reclaimed land, when the promenade itself was completed. Look for the details: porthole windows, ship like railings, sunburst grilles over the doors. This is a city that had just discovered streamlining, and decided it suited a sea front.

In twenty eighteen, you nesco inscribed these buildings, together with the Victorian Gothic ones across the Oval my daan, as a World Heritage Site. It's one of the few places where you can stand between two centuries of architecture, a few hundred metres apart.

Now, the Queen's Necklace. Come back at night, or look at photos taken from Malabar Hill after dark: the streetlights curve round the bay like a string of pearls. That's the nickname, and it's well earned.

And here's the thing about the promenade itself. It's the city's living room. Couples, joggers, retired men in pairs, families, vendors selling roasted corn, everyone comes to the sea wall, nobody is charged a rupee, and it's one of the only places in Mumbai where you can see the horizon.

If you've the energy, walk north along the sea wall to chow puttee Beach. It's about half an hour, the Deco blocks keep you company most of the way, and the last of the sunset is best watched from the sand.
