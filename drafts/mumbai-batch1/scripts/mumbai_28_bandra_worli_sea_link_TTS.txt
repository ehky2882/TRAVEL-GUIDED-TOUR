MUMBAI, 28, BAAN DRAA WOR LEE SEA LINK AND THE COASTAL ROAD
---
From a Portuguese watchtower, you're looking at the newest way into the city. Both were built to control traffic.

You're at Land's End in baan draa, on the old stone ramparts of baan draa Fort, looking south across maa him Bay. That great curve of bridge across the water, with its tall pylons and fans of cables, is the baan draa wor lee Sea Link. On the far shore, the towers of wor lee.

The fort first. The Portuguese built it in sixteen forty, as a watchtower over the bay and the sea. Its real name is kass tella day ag waa da, the fort of the watering place, because there was a freshwater spring here where passing ships could fill their casks.

Now the bridge. It's five point six kilometres long, eight lanes wide, and opened in two thousand and nine. Have a look at the central sections: those are cable stayed spans, hung from pylons around a hundred and twenty six metres tall, with hundreds of steel stays fanning down to the deck. The rest is concrete viaduct on piers in the sea. Before it opened, the drive from baan draa to wor lee could take the better part of an hour. Now it can take about ten minutes, for a toll.

At its southern end, the bridge now meets the Coastal Road, the first phase of which opened in twenty twenty four. It runs along the shore to Marine Drive, partly on reclaimed land and partly through twin tunnels, about two kilometres long, under Malabar Hill and under the sea off chow puttee Beach.

And here's the thing. Mumbai has always been a city of islands that refused to stay islands, the causeways, the reclamations, the railways. This is just the latest version. Whether it's progress or just more traffic is a fierce local argument, and you'll hear both sides.

If you'd like to cross it, take a taxi over the Sea Link at dusk, when the cables are lit. Or stay here a while: the bay at sunset, from the Portuguese walls, is one of the best free views in the city.
