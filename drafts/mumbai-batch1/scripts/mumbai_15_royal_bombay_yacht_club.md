# Mumbai 15 · The Royal Bombay Yacht Club

*Vantage: on Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Marg at Apollo Bunder, across the road from the clubhouse, with the Gateway of India behind you* · 326 words · about 130 s

Turn your back on the Gateway for a moment. The building across the road made the city change its mind about something.

You're at Apollo Bunder, with the harbour and the Gateway of India behind you. Across the road, a stone clubhouse with a steep tiled roof, gables and Gothic windows: the Royal Bombay Yacht Club.

The club was founded in eighteen forty-six, and Queen Victoria allowed it to add Royal to its name thirty years later. This building, designed by the architect John Adams, was finished in eighteen ninety-six. Have a look at the style: Venetian Gothic, with Indian touches worked in — pointed arches and deep shade, the look of a Venetian palazzo that has learned about the monsoon.

Now the story that matters. For more than a century, membership was for Europeans only. During his time as Governor of Bombay, Lord Willingdon tried to bring Indian friends in as his guests. They were refused. He resigned, and in nineteen seventeen founded the Willingdon Sports Club, on the other side of the city, specifically with no colour bar. The Yacht Club itself stayed European until the nineteen fifties. The first Indian invited to join, Kerse Naoroji, turned it down for several years before accepting in nineteen fifty-nine.

And here's the thing. The club still sails, still has its bar and its library, and still keeps residential rooms overlooking the Gateway — rooms you can only book through a member. In two thousand and thirteen, the restored residential chambers won a UNESCO heritage award. A building that once kept most of the city out is now valued by the whole city as part of its face to the sea.

[beat]

Before you go, look along the waterfront at the club's neighbours: the Gateway, the Taj, and, set back, the old Sailors' Home, also by John Adams, which is now the headquarters of Maharashtra's police. One architect, two very different ideas of who the waterfront was for.
