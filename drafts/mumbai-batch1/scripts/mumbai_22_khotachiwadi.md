# Mumbai 22 · Khotachiwadi

*Vantage: at the entrance to the lane, by the small red-painted chapel and cross* · 320 words · about 128 s

There used to be sixty-five houses in this lane. Twenty-seven are left, and every one of them is a decision.

You're at the entrance to Khotachiwadi, a narrow lane in Girgaon, a short walk from Charni Road station. Just at the start, a small red-painted chapel with a crucifix. Beyond it, two and three storey houses, mostly of timber, with overhanging balconies, latticed staircases running up the outside, and tower blocks looming over all of them.

The land was named in eighteen eighty after Dadoba Waman Khot, a revenue officer who leased it out in plots, and much of it went to East Indian Catholics — Bombay's own original Christian community, converted under Portuguese rule long before the British came. They built in the style they knew: open verandahs, carved balconies, outside staircases, with teak that came from Burma. The chapel at the front was put up in thanks for the village being spared in the plague of the eighteen nineties.

Have a look at the houses as you walk in. Some are beautifully kept, painted in bright colours. Others are visibly struggling. Part of the reason is simple economics: rents here have been frozen since the nineteen forties, so many owners have never earned enough to repair them. Some families have moved to the suburbs or abroad. Others have stayed, and the lane now includes Gujarati, Marwari and Jain families alongside the old residents.

And here's the thing. Every house left standing is one that someone has refused to sell to a developer, often for decades. That's what heritage in Mumbai mostly is: not a museum, but residents digging their heels in.

[beat]

Walk slowly, keep your voice down, and remember these are homes. If someone is out on a verandah, a polite hello often gets you a story. Then follow the lane back out to the main road, and you'll walk straight from the nineteenth century into the twenty-first.
