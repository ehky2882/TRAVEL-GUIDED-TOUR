MUMBAI, 23, GATEWAY OF INDIA

The king it was built for never walked through it. He got a cardboard version.

You're at Apollo bunder on the waterfront, in front of the Gateway of India: a single great arch in yellow basalt, twenty six metres high, with four turrets on top and the harbour beyond it. Behind you, the Taj muh hul Palace hotel. Ahead, the ferries heading out to elly fanta and to ah lee baag.

Here's why it's here. In December nineteen eleven, King George the Fifth and Queen Mary landed in India, the first reigning British monarch to do so. The city wanted a grand ceremonial entrance. There wasn't time to build one, so the royal party was greeted by a temporary structure, largely of cardboard. The foundation stone of the real thing was laid in nineteen thirteen, the Scottish architect George wittet's design was approved in nineteen fourteen, and the Gateway finally opened in nineteen twenty four, thirteen years after the visit it commemorates.

Have a look at the detail on the arch. wittet borrowed heavily from sixteenth century goo juh raa tee architecture, the carved stone screens, the arches, the bracketed balconies, and mixed it with a Roman triumphal arch. It's what's called Indo sarra sennick: an imperial building dressed in Indian clothes.

And here's the twist that makes it worth standing here. On the twenty eighth of February, nineteen forty eight, the last British troops to leave India, the First Battalion of the Somerset Light Infantry, marched through this arch to their ship, to a twenty one gun salute. The gate built to welcome the empire in became the door it left by.

If you have a couple of hours, take a ferry from the jetty steps to elly fanta Island. Its rock cut cave temples are more than a thousand years older than anything on this waterfront, and the view back at the Gateway from the water is the one wittet actually designed for.
