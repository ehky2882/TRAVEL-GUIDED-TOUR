MUMBAI — 26 — CHHATRAPATI SHIVAJI MAHARAJ VASTU SANGRAHALAYA
Vantage: inside the museum's garden gate on Mahatma Gandhi Road, Kala Ghoda, facing the domed main building
---
The Prince of Wales laid the first stone of this museum. By the time it opened, it was being used as a military hospital.

You're inside the garden gate on Mahatma Gandhi Road, in Kala Ghoda, looking along the path to a large domed building in grey and buff stone — the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya, which the guide that sent you here, and many older Bombayites, still call the Prince of Wales Museum.

The foundation stone was laid in November nineteen oh five by the Prince of Wales, later King George the Fifth. The design came from an open competition, and was won by George Wittet — the same Scottish architect who would go on to design the Gateway of India down the road. The building was finished in nineteen fifteen, but the First World War got there first: it served as a children's welfare centre and a military hospital before the museum finally opened in January nineteen twenty-two.

Have a look at the stone. The grey is local basalt quarried at Kurla; the warm buff is trachyte from Malad. Then the white dome, which Wittet modelled on the domes of the Deccan sultanates. Under it, a mix of Mughal, Maratha and Jain detail — the style called Indo-Saracenic, which tried to give imperial buildings an Indian face.

And here's the thing. In nineteen ninety-eight the museum was renamed after Shivaji, the seventeenth-century founder of the Maratha kingdom. A building begun to honour an English prince now carries the name of an Indian king. It hasn't changed a stone of it, which is perhaps the most Bombay thing about it.

[beat]

Go in if you have an hour. The museum holds around fifty thousand objects — Indus Valley finds, sculpture from the Elephanta caves, miniature paintings, and a suit of armour said to have belonged to the emperor Akbar. And if you haven't yet, the black horse of Kala Ghoda is just outside the gate.
