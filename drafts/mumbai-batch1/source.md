=== 01 | elco-pani-puri-centre | ELCO Pani Puri, Hill Road
Vantage: Hill Road, Bandra West, on the pavement outside the ELCO shopfront
---
The most famous name on this road belongs to a market most people have forgotten.

Hill Road, Bandra West. Look for the sign reading ELCO above a busy shopfront, with a counter at street level and a queue that never quite clears. Upstairs, a family restaurant. At the counter, a man filling small, hollow puris faster than you can count them.

Here's how it began. The Bhagnani family were Sindhi merchants, uprooted by Partition in nineteen forty-seven, who rebuilt their lives in Bombay. In nineteen seventy-five their son, Mohandas, set up a single hand cart outside the ELCO market, then one of the smartest places to shop in Bandra. His pani puri was good — properly good — and his customers started calling him the ELCO pani puri wala. The man at the cart outside the market. The name stuck so firmly that the business outlived the reputation of the market it was named after.

Now have a look at what they're actually serving. Pani puri is a thin, crisp shell, punctured with a thumb, filled with potato or chickpea, then dunked in tamarind water and a sharp, green, spiced water. You eat it in one go, or not at all. The family's own account puts the secret down to two unglamorous things: good Sindhi puris, and bottled water, at a time when nobody at a street cart bothered. One cart became two. Fifteen or twenty years on, they bought a shop on this spot, and later the floors above it.

And that, if you ask me, is the real story. Mumbai street food is usually talked about as something wild and anonymous. This is the opposite — one family, fifty years, one corner, and a reputation built almost entirely on not making people ill.

[beat]

If you're eating, stand at the counter and let them hand you each puri as it's made — that's how it's meant to go. And if you fancy a walk afterwards, Hill Road is one long market in both directions — clothes, shoes, fruit and phone cases, all the way along.

=== 11 | willingdon-sports-club | Willingdon Sports Club
Vantage: on the road outside the main gate of the Willingdon Sports Club, Tardeo
---
This club exists because another club refused to let some Indians in.

Now, inside those gates, the clubhouse was designed by F G B Hawkins, the architect of the city's Improvement Trust, and laid out with tennis courts, croquet lawns, a cricket ground and one of the first full-size polo fields in India. And one small claim to fame: the club is said to be where eggs Kejriwal — fried eggs on cheese toast, with green chilli — was invented, by a member who couldn't eat eggs at home.

And here's the thing. It would be easy to tell this as a story of a liberal governor and a happy ending. It's subtler than that. This was still a club for the rich, and today membership is mostly closed except to members' children. But in its moment, a door opened here that had been shut elsewhere, and the city noticed.

If you fancy completing the story, the Royal Bombay Yacht Club is on the waterfront at Apollo Bunder, beside the Gateway. Stand outside both and you've seen both sides of the same argument.

Colaba was once two separate islands at the bottom of Bombay, reachable on foot only at low tide; at high tide you went by boat, and overcrowded boats sometimes capsized. In eighteen thirty-eight the East India Company finished a causeway to join them to the rest of the city. It was widened twice in the eighteen sixties, and the road you're standing on is that causeway, long since swallowed by buildings on either side.

Have a look at Leopold. It was founded in eighteen seventy-one by Iranis — Zoroastrian immigrants from Persia — and named after the King of the Belgians. In its early years it was a shop of one kind or another — accounts differ as to whether it sold cooking oil or medicines — before becoming the cafe and bar it is now. In November two thousand and eight it was one of the first places attacked in the assault on the city, and the bullet holes in the walls were deliberately left in place. Look for them inside.

And here's the thing. The guide that sent you here notes there are no high-end shops on the Causeway. That's not quite a criticism. This is a street where everything is for sale, cheaply, to everybody, as it's been for a century and a half.

If you've time, walk south along the Causeway into Colaba proper. The stalls thin out, and in fifteen minutes or so you'll reach Sassoon Dock, the fish market where the city's catch has come ashore since eighteen seventy-five. Early morning is when it's at its loudest.

=== 18 | kala-ghoda | Kala Ghoda
Vantage: at the Kala Ghoda junction, beside the riderless black horse statue
---
Kala Ghoda means black horse. For more than fifty years, there wasn't one.

You're at the Kala Ghoda junction, in the middle of Bombay's art district, beside a black horse on a plinth — standing, saddled, and quite riderless. Around you: Victorian stone buildings, galleries, cafes, and, if you look south, the dome of the city's great museum behind its gardens.

Here's the story of the name. In the late nineteenth century, the Sassoon family — Baghdadi Jewish merchants who did a great deal to build this part of the city — presented a black stone statue of King Edward the Seventh on horseback, as Prince of Wales. It was everyone's landmark, so the district took its name. In nineteen sixty-five, with independent India removing its colonial statues, the king and his horse were taken down and moved to the gardens at Byculla, where you can still visit them. The neighbourhood kept the name and lost the horse. Then in twenty seventeen, the Kala Ghoda Association commissioned this one, the Spirit of Kala Ghoda, without a king.

Have a look around the junction. Jehangir Art Gallery, the city's best-known exhibition space. The David Sassoon Library. The pale blue front of Knesseth Eliyahoo Synagogue, a short walk away. And Esplanade Mansion, once Watson's Hotel, which is said to be the oldest surviving cast-iron building in India.

And here's the thing. A horse without its rider is a rather neat image for what this city did after empire. It kept the buildings, the streets and the names, and quietly took the king off the top.

[beat]

If you're here in February, the Kala Ghoda Arts Festival takes over these streets for nine days. At any other time of year, walk down V B Gandhi Marg to the synagogue, past the old Rhythm House corner and the galleries — a whole afternoon within five minutes of this horse.

=== 19 | linking-road-bandra | Linking Road, Bandra
Vantage: on Linking Road, Bandra West, near the National College end among the street stalls
---
The name of this road is a description of the job it was given. It did it rather too well.

You're on Linking Road in Bandra West, among the stalls near the National College end. Look around: footwear piled high on folding tables, racks of kurtas and jeans, bags, belts, phone covers, and, behind them, the glass fronts of international brands. Expect traffic. The guide that sent you here warns about it, and it's right.

Here's the history. In the nineteen forties, Bandra and Juhu were still separate suburbs with fields and villages between them. This was one of the first roads built to link them — hence the name — and it originally carried the name of Dadabhai Naoroji, the Parsi economist and politician who was the first Indian elected to the British Parliament. Over the following decades the suburbs grew into each other along it, and so did the shops.

Have a look at the two economies sitting side by side. On the pavement, small traders selling shoes, copies and bargains from a few square feet of space; behind them, flagship stores paying some of the highest rents in the country. In the late two thousands, newspapers were calling this India's most expensive retail location. The stalls are still here anyway.

And here's the thing. Most cities separate their luxury shopping from their street trade. Mumbai rarely bothers. On Linking Road the two lean on each other: the stalls bring the crowds, and the crowds go on to the stores. If you bargain at a stall here, the convention is to start at about half of what you're asked. Smile while you do it.

[beat]

If the traffic gets to you, turn west off Linking Road towards Pali Village and Carter Road. Within ten minutes you'll be among old cottages and cafes, and then at the sea wall, which is a different suburb entirely.

=== 20 | phoenix-mills | Phoenix Mills and Palladium
Vantage: on Senapati Bapat Marg, Lower Parel, at the main entrance to the Phoenix Mills compound
---
There's a chimney in this shopping centre. Nobody spins cotton here any more, but somebody decided it should stay.

You're on Senapati Bapat Marg in Lower Parel, at the entrance to Phoenix Mills. Behind the gates, a compound of malls, restaurants, bars, a cinema and a hotel, with the Palladium at its smartest end. Look among the newer buildings for a tall brick chimney standing on its own.

Here's what it remembers. Phoenix Mills was incorporated on the twenty-seventh of January, nineteen oh five, as a cotton textile mill on about seventeen acres of land here in Lower Parel. This whole district was Girangaon, the village of the mills — rows of chimneys, workers' tenements, and hundreds of thousands of people making cloth for the empire and then for India.

Then came nineteen eighty-two. The great Bombay textile strike shut every mill in the city for more than a year. Many never properly recovered. Mill owners found that the land under their machines was worth more than anything the machines could make, and Phoenix was among the first to act. It began with a bowling alley in a corner of the old mill, while parts of the mill were still running. Shops followed, then the malls, then everything else. The chimney was kept.

And that, I think, is the real point. The chimney is a beautiful object, and it's also a slightly uncomfortable one. It marks a moment when this city decided that its future lay in selling things rather than making them — and the tens of thousands of mill workers who lost their jobs aren't the people shopping here now.

[beat]

If you fancy seeing what was here before, walk out of the compound and into the side streets of Lower Parel. Among the towers you'll still find chawls — the old tenement blocks of the mill workers — with their long shared balconies and washing hung out to dry.
=== 21 | chor-bazaar | Chor Bazaar
Vantage: on Mutton Street, in the middle of the antique and furniture shops
---
Chor Bazaar means Thieves' Market. It almost certainly started out as something else.

You're on Mutton Street, in the old Muslim quarter near Bhendi Bazaar, in south Mumbai. Around you, shopfronts overflowing onto the road: brass lamps, gramophones, ships' clocks, carved doors, old film posters, chandeliers, chairs stacked six high. The lanes either side carry on in the same vein for blocks.

Here's the trouble with the name. The story most people here will tell you is that this was once Shor Bazaar — the noisy market — and that the British couldn't, or wouldn't, pronounce it, and turned shor into chor, the word for thief. Nobody has ever quite proved it. But it's the sort of explanation that sticks because it fits: the place is noisy, and over the years enough stolen goods passed through it that the new name earned itself.

Then there's the other story — that Queen Victoria lost a violin, or some other possessions, while visiting India, and that they turned up for sale here. It's a lovely tale. It also has a small flaw: Queen Victoria never visited India.

Have a look at what's actually on sale. A great deal of the furniture is genuinely old, rescued from bungalows and mansions as the city tore them down. A great deal of the rest is skilfully made to look old, in workshops round the corner. The Bollywood posters are a speciality — hand-painted ones from the old cinemas are the real prize. The skill is telling one from the other, and the shopkeepers are, it must be said, better at it than you are.

[beat]

The market has been here for something like a hundred and fifty years, through every redevelopment scheme the city has thrown at it. If you've time, walk east into Bhendi Bazaar for kebabs and sweets on the main road — and haggle here, politely and at length. Nobody in this street expects the first price to be the last.

=== 22 | khotachiwadi | Khotachiwadi
Vantage: at the entrance to the lane, by the small red-painted chapel and cross
---
There used to be sixty-five houses in this lane. Twenty-seven are left, and every one of them is a decision.

You're at the entrance to Khotachiwadi, a narrow lane in Girgaon, a short walk from Charni Road station. Just at the start, a small red-painted chapel with a crucifix. Beyond it, two and three storey houses, mostly of timber, with overhanging balconies, latticed staircases running up the outside, and tower blocks looming over all of them.

The land was named in eighteen eighty after Dadoba Waman Khot, a revenue officer who leased it out in plots, and much of it went to East Indian Catholics — Bombay's own original Christian community, converted under Portuguese rule long before the British came. They built in the style they knew: open verandahs, carved balconies, outside staircases, with teak that came from Burma. The chapel at the front was put up in thanks for the village being spared in the plague of the eighteen nineties.

Have a look at the houses as you walk in. Some are beautifully kept, painted in bright colours. Others are visibly struggling. Part of the reason is simple economics: rents here have been frozen since the nineteen forties, so many owners have never earned enough to repair them. Some families have moved to the suburbs or abroad. Others have stayed, and the lane now includes Gujarati, Marwari and Jain families alongside the old residents.

And here's the thing. Every house left standing is one that someone has refused to sell to a developer, often for decades. That's what heritage in Mumbai mostly is: not a museum, but residents digging their heels in.

[beat]

Walk slowly, keep your voice down, and remember these are homes. If someone is out on a verandah, a polite hello often gets you a story. Then follow the lane back out to the main road, and you'll walk straight from the nineteenth century into the twenty-first.

=== 23 | gateway-of-india | Gateway of India
Vantage: on the Apollo Bunder waterfront, in front of the Gateway's central arch, facing the harbour
---
The king it was built for never walked through it. He got a cardboard version.

You're at Apollo Bunder on the waterfront, in front of the Gateway of India: a single great arch in yellow basalt, twenty-six metres high, with four turrets on top and the harbour beyond it. Behind you, the Taj Mahal Palace hotel. Ahead, the ferries heading out to Elephanta and to Alibaug.

Here's why it's here. In December nineteen eleven, King George the Fifth and Queen Mary landed in India, the first reigning British monarch to do so. The city wanted a grand ceremonial entrance. There wasn't time to build one, so the royal party was greeted by a temporary structure, largely of cardboard. The foundation stone of the real thing was laid in nineteen thirteen, the Scottish architect George Wittet's design was approved in nineteen fourteen, and the Gateway finally opened in nineteen twenty-four, thirteen years after the visit it commemorates.

Have a look at the detail on the arch. Wittet borrowed heavily from sixteenth-century Gujarati architecture — the carved stone screens, the arches, the bracketed balconies — and mixed it with a Roman triumphal arch. It's what's called Indo-Saracenic: an imperial building dressed in Indian clothes.

[beat]

And here's the twist that makes it worth standing here. On the twenty-eighth of February, nineteen forty-eight, the last British troops to leave India — the First Battalion of the Somerset Light Infantry — marched through this arch to their ship, to a twenty-one gun salute. The gate built to welcome the empire in became the door it left by.

If you have a couple of hours, take a ferry from the jetty steps to Elephanta Island. Its rock-cut cave temples are more than a thousand years older than anything on this waterfront, and the view back at the Gateway from the water is the one Wittet actually designed for.

=== 24 | marine-drive | Marine Drive
Vantage: on the Marine Drive promenade, at the sea wall opposite the Art Deco blocks near Churchgate
---
Behind you is the sea. In front of you is one of the largest collections of Art Deco buildings anywhere in the world.

You're on the promenade at Marine Drive, with the sea wall at your back and the six-lane road in front of you. The promenade runs for about three kilometres in a long curve, from Nariman Point at the southern end to Chowpatty Beach at the foot of Malabar Hill.

Have a look across the road. That row of cream, pale yellow and white apartment blocks — rounded corners, long balconies, horizontal bands, names on the fronts like Kapur Mahal, Zaver Mahal, Keval Mahal — is Art Deco. Most of it went up from the late nineteen thirties, built largely by wealthy Parsi families on freshly reclaimed land, when the promenade itself was completed. Look for the details: porthole windows, ship-like railings, sunburst grilles over the doors. This is a city that had just discovered streamlining, and decided it suited a sea front.

In twenty eighteen, UNESCO inscribed these buildings, together with the Victorian Gothic ones across the Oval Maidan, as a World Heritage Site. It's one of the few places where you can stand between two centuries of architecture, a few hundred metres apart.

Now, the Queen's Necklace. Come back at night, or look at photos taken from Malabar Hill after dark: the streetlights curve round the bay like a string of pearls. That's the nickname, and it's well earned.

[beat]

And here's the thing about the promenade itself. It's the city's living room. Couples, joggers, retired men in pairs, families, vendors selling roasted corn — everyone comes to the sea wall, nobody is charged a rupee, and it's one of the only places in Mumbai where you can see the horizon.

If you've the energy, walk north along the sea wall to Chowpatty Beach. It's about half an hour, the Deco blocks keep you company most of the way, and the last of the sunset is best watched from the sand.

---
Pali Hill has film stars. Pali Naka has traffic. Both are named after the small village you're standing in.

Around you: low cottages with tiled roofs and painted fronts, small shrines and crosses, narrow lanes, and, slotted in among them, cafes, bakeries and restaurants.

Bandra was once a scatter of villages — Catholic, fishing, farming — set among paddy fields, coconut groves and salt pans. Pali was one of them, established around the seventeen hundreds, and it's what's called a gaothan: a traditional village settlement, with its own rules and its own sense of who belongs. Pali Hill, now one of the most expensive addresses in the country, and the busy junction at Pali Naka both took their names from it.

And here's the thing. Pali has become fashionable — spas, healing centres, smart little restaurants — partly because it looks like the Bandra people remember. That's a double-edged sword. So be a considerate visitor: these are homes first. Then, if you fancy it, walk west to Carter Road and the sea wall, or north-west to Chimbai, the fishing village on the shore, which is one of the other old Bandra villages still standing.

=== 26 | chhatrapati-shivaji-maharaj-vastu-sangrahalaya | Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya

---
The Prince of Wales laid the first stone of this museum. By the time it opened, it was being used as a military hospital.

You're inside the garden gate on Mahatma Gandhi Road, in Kala Ghoda, looking along the path to a large domed building in grey and buff stone — the Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya, which the guide that sent you here, and many older Bombayites, still call the Prince of Wales Museum.

The foundation stone was laid in November nineteen oh five by the Prince of Wales, later King George the Fifth. The design came from an open competition, and was won by George Wittet — the same Scottish architect who would go on to design the Gateway of India down the road. [beat]

Go in if you have an hour. The museum holds around fifty thousand objects — Indus Valley finds, sculpture from the Elephanta caves, miniature paintings, and a suit of armour said to have belonged to the emperor Akbar. And if you haven't yet, the black horse of Kala Ghoda is just outside the gate.

=== 27 | kanchanjunga-apartments | Kanchanjunga Apartments
Vantage: on Bomanji Petit Marg, Cumballa Hill, looking up at the tower's corner terraces
---
Here's a problem. In Bombay, the side of a building with the best view and the best breeze also gets the worst sun and the worst rain. This tower is the answer.

You're on Bomanji Petit Marg on Cumballa Hill, in south Mumbai. Look up at the white tower with large square holes cut out of its corners — great double-height openings, with glimpses of plants and walls painted in bold colours inside. That's Kanchanjunga Apartments, by Charles Correa, one of India's great modern architects.

Correa worked on it from nineteen seventy, and it was completed in nineteen eighty-three. It holds just thirty-two apartments, of three to six bedrooms. The tower is about twenty-one metres square and eighty-four metres high — exactly four times as tall as it is wide.

Now the gift detail. To catch sea breezes and the views of the Arabian Sea on one side and the harbour on the other, a building here needs to face east and west. But those are exactly the directions of the hot afternoon sun and the monsoon rain. The old Bombay bungalow solved this with a verandah wrapped round the main rooms. Correa took that idea and stacked it: every flat has a double-height garden terrace at the corner, shaded by the floor above, so you can sit outside in the monsoon and stay dry. The apartments interlock in plan and section, which is why the cut-outs alternate up the facade. The solid end walls carry it all.

And here's the thing. Correa was arguing that a tower in India didn't have to be a glass box copied from somewhere else. It could be built from the logic of the old houses of the city. [beat]

Before you go, stand back across the road and find the colours inside the cut-outs. They're the only clue from the street to how complicated the inside is. Then walk down the hill to Kemps Corner, past older bungalows of the kind Correa was learning from.
