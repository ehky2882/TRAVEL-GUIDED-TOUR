ATLAS — CHICAGO
Walk 2: The Loop — Where the Skyscraper Was Born
Segment 01 — One thirty-five South luh sal / the Home Insurance Building site
TTS-SAFE version

---

The building that got Chicago called the birthplace of the skyscraper isn't here. It came down in nineteen thirty-one, forty-six years old, and nobody made much of a fuss.

Look for the plaque first — it's set into the wall of the tiered limestone tower on this corner. It marks the Home Insurance Building: ten stories, finished in eighteen eighty-five, William luh bar un jen ee. It's widely called the first skyscraper, and historians have argued the point for a hundred years and haven't stopped, mostly over how much of jen ee's iron and steel frame was really doing the work.

What nobody argues is what the frame was for. Ask why anyone wanted one and the answer people reach for is height, and height is the least of it. A masonry building carries itself on its outside walls, so the higher you go the thicker those walls get at the bottom — and thick walls eat the two things a landlord is actually selling. They eat floor space, room by room, on every floor. And they shrink the windows, which mattered enormously in a decade when the alternative to daylight was gas. A frame let an owner build up without paying for it in rentable square feet and light.

There were other reasons, and the honest version keeps all of them: the soft ground under the Loop, the passenger elevator, a building code that let Chicago go thinner than eastern cities could. Nobody serious gives one cause.

The forty-four story tower standing here now opened in nineteen thirty-four and is a good building in its own right. But the thing that happened on this corner left nothing behind.

The proof outlived the evidence.
