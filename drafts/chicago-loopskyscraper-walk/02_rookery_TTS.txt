This is the Rookery. Twelve stories, eighteen eighty-eight, bern um and Root, and generally called the oldest high-rise still standing in Chicago.

Right. The thing to know about it is that its owners hedged.

Two brothers from Boston, Peter and Shepherd Brooks, had been buying Chicago property since the eighteen sixties, and they kept a man here to do their thinking on the ground — Owen al diss, a lawyer out of Vermont who managed their buildings and talked them into taller ones. loo iss sullivan later named al diss as one of two men responsible for the modern office building, which is an odd thing to say about a property manager and probably fair.

Three years after jen ee's frame went up two blocks north, the Brooks brothers put up this. Heavy masonry on the outside, carrying its own weight, the way buildings had always done it. And inside, out of sight, an iron and steel frame carrying the floors and the light court, independent of those walls. Two structural systems in one building, because the new one worked and nobody was ready to bet a whole building on it yet.

You can see the hedge from where you're standing. The outside is arches, rough stone and heavy horizontal banding — a building that wants to look like it could stop a cannonball. If the lobby's open, weekdays and Saturday mornings through the Adams Street door, go in, because the middle of this building is the exact opposite: a glass-roofed court, white marble, thin ironwork, all of it light. Frank Lloyd Wright was brought in to rework that court in nineteen oh five and covered Root's dark iron over in marble.

Fortress on the outside, greenhouse in the middle. Same building, two answers, and its owners were hedging their bet in both directions at once.
