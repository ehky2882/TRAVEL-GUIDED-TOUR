City Palace

It took twenty two generations of one family to build this, and from the outside you'd swear it was designed in one go.

You should be in the great open courtyard just inside the Tripoliya, the triple arched gate, with the palace rising in front of you along the ridge. It's a wall of pale stone, balconies, towers and little domed pavilions, getting busier the higher it goes. Lake Pichohla is on the far side, below.

Start with the gate behind you. The Tripoliya was built in seventeen twenty five. Between it and the earlier Buddee Pole, the great gate, stand a row of marble arches. Tradition has it the Mahaaraanas were weighed here against gold and silver, which was then given away to the people.

Then the palace itself. Mahaaraana Ooday Singh the Second began building here in fifteen fifty nine, when he founded Oodaypoor. The story goes that he chose the spot on a hermit's advice. Over the following four centuries, his descendants of the Seesodiya clan kept adding to it. The result is eleven palaces joined into one, linked by courtyards and deliberately zig zagging corridors, designed to slow down any attacker who got inside.

Once you're in, look out for the Mor Chowk, the Peacock Courtyard. Its walls carry peacocks made of tiny pieces of coloured glass, which shimmer as you move.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is why it all looks so unified. These rulers had four hundred years to change their minds and very rarely did. Each generation added to the palace in the same language as the one before. That restraint is the real achievement, more than the size.

When you've seen the inside, take a boat out onto Lake Pichohla. Only from the water do you see the full length of the palace, rising straight out of the lake.
