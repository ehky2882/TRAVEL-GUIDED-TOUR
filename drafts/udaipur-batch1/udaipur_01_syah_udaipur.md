# Syah

Most restaurants want to be found. This one sits at the end of a farm track, six kilometres out of town, and only opens if you've booked.

You're at Kasotia Ji Farm, off the Kodiyat road, with the city behind you and the Aravalli hills folded up ahead. Low buildings, a lot of planting, and very little to suggest a kitchen of any ambition. That's Syah.

Have a look at the land around you first, because it is the menu. Syah calls itself farm to table, and it means the farm you're standing on. Much of what turns up on the plate is grown here or foraged nearby, and the kitchen changes with the seasons. It even keeps its own season. Syah opens from July to April and closes for the worst of the summer heat.

The meal itself is a single tasting menu, twelve courses, and nothing à la carte. The chef, Pankaj, takes Rajasthani ingredients and puts them through modern technique. You get small, exact plates that sometimes look nothing like the dish they began as.

And the story behind it is a curious one. Syah didn't start in Udaipur at all. It started in two thousand and sixteen, high in the Himalayas in Ladakh, where it was once called the most exciting culinary experience in the region. The team came down to these hills in two thousand and nineteen.

*[beat]*

Here's what I find rather lovely about that. A restaurant that learned its trade cooking with whatever a mountain desert would give it has ended up in a valley, cooking the same way. The landscape changed. The idea didn't.

If you've arrived early, take a slow turn round the grounds before you sit down. The light goes gold on the Aravallis just before sunset, and the vegetable beds are the best preview of dinner you'll get.
