Palacete Bolonha

It's 1905, and the plot on this corner is little more than a swamp, alive with insects and rats, which is not the obvious place to build a palace for the woman you love.

Across Avenida Governador José Malcher from you stands a tall and heavily decorated house with a lookout at the very top and an Art Nouveau iron gate at street level. That's the Palacete Bolonha, and the narrow lane running alongside it, Passagem Bolonha, was laid out by the same man.

He was Francisco Bolonha, an engineer born in Belém in 1872, who happened to be the biggest cement importer in Pará, which helps when your building site is a bog. Between 1905 and 1908 he brought in foreign engineers, pneumatic tools and pumps to drain the ground, and the house that went up was among the tallest in the city.

The story usually told is that he promised his wife Alice, a pianist, a home as fine as anything in Europe, and the music room on the first floor is still the heart of the visit. Inside, the house had gas, running water and an American electric refrigerator, which in Belém at the time was close to science fiction, along with French tiles, gilded plasterwork and a corridor lined in azulejos.

Here's what I love about the place, though: it was built to prove that Belém could hold its own with Paris, in a climate that disagreed: the house was poorly ventilated and hard to live in, and after Bolonha died in 1939 it was eventually left empty. The state listed it in 1982, and today the city runs it as a museum with much of the original decoration intact.

Free guided visits run on weekdays in groups of around fifteen, and as you step inside, look down at the entrance floor, where a mosaic spells out cave canem, beware of the dog, just as it would in Pompeii.
