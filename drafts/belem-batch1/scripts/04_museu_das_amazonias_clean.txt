Museu das Amazônias

This is the Museu das Amazônias, and the plural in its name is doing a great deal of work.

Porto Futuro, warehouse four, on the waterfront just north of Estação das Docas: the building ahead of you is one of five restored port warehouses from the early twentieth century, with the same English ironwork and red oxide metal as its older neighbour, and Guajará Bay running along just behind it.

The museum opened on the fourth of October 2025, a month before the United Nations climate conference came to Belém, and President Lula was here to open it; more than six and a half thousand people came through on the first day, many of them for Sebastião Salgado's black-and-white photographs of the forest and its peoples.

After a renovation, the inside reopened in November 2026 with a new long-term show on the ground floor called Amazônias no Plural, which runs from how the region was formed geologically to how people live, eat and work there now. Upstairs on the mezzanine, a shorter exhibition ties together water, fire and the Pantanal wetlands further south. The science behind it comes from the Museu Goeldi, the city's long-established research museum, and entry is free, though you'll need to book a ticket online, and it's closed on Wednesdays.

Outsiders tend to talk about the Amazon as a single thing, whether a forest, a lung or a cause, and the quiet argument of this building is that it has always been many places at once: rivers and floodplains, Indigenous nations, fishing towns, kitchens, and cities like this one, which has been sending boats up those rivers for four hundred years.

When you come back out, turn towards the gastronomy warehouse that opened alongside the museum, find something regional to eat, and carry it down to the water's edge, where the river the whole exhibition is about will be sliding past in front of you.
