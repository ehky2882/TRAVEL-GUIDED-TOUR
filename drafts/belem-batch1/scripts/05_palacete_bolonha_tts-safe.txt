Palasetchee Bolonya

It's nineteen oh five, and the plot on this corner is little more than a swamp, alive with insects and rats, which is not the obvious place to build a palace for the woman you love.

Across Avenida Govehrnador Zhozeh Malsher from you stands a tall and heavily decorated house with a lookout at the very top and an Art Noovoh iron gate at street level. That's the Palasetchee Bolonya, and the narrow lane running alongside it, Passahzhem Bolonya, was laid out by the same man.

He was Franseeskoo Bolonya, an engineer born in Belaym in eighteen seventy two, who happened to be the biggest cement importer in Pahrah, which helps when your building site is a bog. Between nineteen oh five and nineteen oh eight he brought in foreign engineers, pneumatic tools and pumps to drain the ground, and the house that went up was among the tallest in the city.

The story usually told is that he promised his wife Alice, a pianist, a home as fine as anything in Europe, and the music room on the first floor is still the heart of the visit. Inside, the house had gas, running water and an American electric refrigerator, which in Belaym at the time was close to science fiction, along with French tiles, gilded plasterwork and a corridor lined in azoolayzhoos.

Here's what I love about the place, though: it was built to prove that Belaym could hold its own with Paris, in a climate that disagreed: the house was poorly ventilated and hard to live in, and after Bolonya died in nineteen thirty nine it was eventually left empty. The state listed it in nineteen eighty two, and today the city runs it as a museum with much of the original decoration intact.

Free guided visits run on weekdays in groups of around fifteen, and as you step inside, look down at the entrance floor, where a mosaic spells out kahveh kahnem, beware of the dog, just as it would in Pompay.
