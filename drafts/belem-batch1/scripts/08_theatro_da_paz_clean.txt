Theatro da Paz

When it opened in February 1878, this theatre could seat around eleven hundred people, in a city that had grown rich on rubber almost faster than it could build.

Praça da República spreads out around you, shaded by tall mango trees, and the theatre sits along one edge of it: a long, pale neoclassical front with a run of columns across the upper floor and a triangular pediment on top. Stand back far enough to take in the whole width of it, because it's designed to be seen from the square.

The model was La Scala in Milan, and the building took from 1869 to 1874 to put up, with the opening delayed while irregularities in the works were investigated, which is a very theatrical start for a theatre. Almost everything that mattered was imported: English cast iron, Italian marble for the staircases, French chandeliers and Portuguese stone mosaics, while the floors were laid in Amazonian hardwoods.

Inside, the painted ceiling mixes Greek and Roman gods with the plants and animals of the Amazon, as if Olympus had relocated to the riverbank, and marble busts honour the writers José de Alencar and Gonçalves Dias alongside the composers Carlos Gomes and Henrique Gurjão.

And here's the thing: for a century and more, this place and the Teatro Amazonas, a thousand miles or so upriver in Manaus, were rivals, two rubber cities each insisting it had the finer opera house. In July 2026, UNESCO settled the argument by not settling it at all, adding them to the World Heritage list together as the Theatres of the Amazon.

If the box office is offering a guided visit, take it, because the ceiling is the reason to go in; and on your way out, walk the length of Praça da República, where the old bandstand and the mango trees have been keeping the theatre company since the rubber years.
