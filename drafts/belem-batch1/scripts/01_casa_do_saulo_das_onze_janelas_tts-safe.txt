Kahza doo Sowloo das Onzee Zhanellas

Late in the afternoon, the sun drops low over Gwazharah Bay and turns the water the colour of strong tea, which is a fine reason to book a late lunch.

The long, pale building beside you, with its row of tall windows facing the river, is the Kahza das Onzee Zhanellas, the House of Eleven Windows, and the restaurant spreads across its riverside terrace above the bay. Count the upper windows if you like, since the name is entirely literal, and off to your right as you face the water, the low walls of the old fort mark the spot where the Portuguese founded Belaym in sixteen sixteen.

The house began in the eighteenth century as a sugar planter's weekend retreat, until the colonial government bought it in seventeen sixty eight and had the Italian architect Antonyoo Landee turn it into the Royal Military Hospital. The army kept it for generations, and during the military dictatorship political prisoners were held here, which is worth carrying quietly to the table; since two thousand and two it has been a contemporary art space.

The kitchen belongs to Sowloo Jennings, who grew up on the Tapazhoss River near Santaraym and started his first restaurant at home with five tables in two thousand and nine. He calls his style Tapazhoss cooking, and in twenty twenty four United Nations Tourism named him its first gastronomy ambassador. Order the freshwater fish, peerahrookoo, tambakee or feelyotchee, with Santaraym beans and rice turned green with zhamboo, the herb that leaves your lips tingling.

What I find rather touching is the direction of travel. For centuries the rivers sent their wealth down to this corner of the city to be taxed and shipped away, and now a cook from upriver has moved into the old hospital and serves the rivers' own food back across the terrace.

Before you go, walk a couple of minutes north to the Fortchee doo Prezepyoo and look back from its walls, where all eleven windows line up along the waterfront exactly as the name promised.
