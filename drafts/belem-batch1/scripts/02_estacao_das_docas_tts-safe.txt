Estahsoung das Dokas

The iron holding up these warehouses crossed the Atlantic from England more than a century ago, and it was paid for, more or less, with rubber.

On the riverfront promenade at Estahsoung das Dokas, with Gwazharah Bay on your left as you walk north and three long warehouses on your right, you're on what became, from nineteen oh nine, the city's new commercial port. Along the water's edge, tall painted cranes still stand on the quay with their arms angled out towards the river.

Start with those cranes, which were built in the United States in the early twentieth century and once swung cargo aboard ships bound for Europe and North America, though these days their main job is framing everyone's sunset photographs.

The warehouses behind you have been given new work: one for regional crafts, one for restaurants and bars, and one for fairs and exhibitions, which also houses a small museum of the port, with photographs and objects dug up while the site was being restored. The restaurant warehouse is where Amazon Beer brews on site, and where Kahyroo, a local institution, scoops ice cream in flavours like koopwahsoo and bakooree that you won't find at home.

Then look up, because the best detail is overhead. In the evenings, musicians perform from a small stage that slides along a rail above the tables, so the band travels down the room to you rather than the other way round.

Here's the reframe worth taking with you: this quay was built to send the Amazon's wealth away, and after the rubber trade collapsed the waterfront slid into decades of neglect, with a reputation that kept most residents away; when the complex reopened in twenty hundred, it became the place where Belaym comes to look at its own river.

Walk to the far end of the promenade towards sunset, pick a spot beside one of the cranes, and watch the light go down behind the Eelya das Onsas across the water.
