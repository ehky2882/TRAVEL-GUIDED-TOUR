Vair oo Payzoo Market

It's sixteen twenty five, and the Portuguese have set up a post on this shore where every cargo coming off the river must be weighed and taxed, a place they called the Kahza jee Avair oo Payzoo, the house for checking the weight.

With your back to the city and the bay in front of you, the Merkadoo jee Fehhoo, the iron fish market, should be sitting right at the water's edge: a long hall with pointed towers at its corners, covered in small grey scales of zinc. Spreading out on either side are rows of stalls under awnings, and a great deal of shouting.

That iron hall opened in nineteen oh one, prefabricated in Europe and shipped over in pieces at the height of the rubber boom, which is why it looks like a railway station that has wandered down to the river. Further along, the older meat market has its own ironwork inside, and these days it carries the name of Franseeskoo Bolonya, the engineer whose palace stands across town.

The stalls in between are the real show, starting with the ahsahee, which arrives by boat in the very early morning and is sold by the basket, and then there are the herb sellers, with bundles of bark, roots and little bottles promising luck, love or a good night's sleep. It's often called the largest open air market in Latin America.

What's easy to miss is how the name has drifted. Avair oo payzoo, to have the weight checked, softened over the centuries into Vair oo Payzoo, to see the weight, and so a colonial tax office became the city's favourite thing to look at. Long before the Portuguese, the Toopeenambah traded on this shore, and a recent renovation for the climate conference has left some people worrying about losing the market's glorious chaos, while others welcome the cleaner stalls.

Come back at first light, when the ahsahee boats are pulling in, and buy a bowl from a stall right beside the water.
