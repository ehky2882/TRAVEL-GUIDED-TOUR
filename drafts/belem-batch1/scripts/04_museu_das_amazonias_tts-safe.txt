Moozayoo das Amazohneeas

This is the Moozayoo das Amazohneeas, and the plural in its name is doing a great deal of work.

Portoo Footooroo, warehouse four, on the waterfront just north of Estahsoung das Dokas: the building ahead of you is one of five restored port warehouses from the early twentieth century, with the same English ironwork and red oxide metal as its older neighbour, and Gwazharah Bay running along just behind it.

The museum opened on the fourth of October twenty twenty five, a month before the United Nations climate conference came to Belaym, and President Loola was here to open it; more than six and a half thousand people came through on the first day, many of them for Sebastchee owng Salgahdoo's black and white photographs of the forest and its peoples.

After a renovation, the inside reopened in November twenty twenty six with a new long term show on the ground floor called Amazohneeas noo Ploorow, which runs from how the region was formed geologically to how people live, eat and work there now. Upstairs on the mezzanine, a shorter exhibition ties together water, fire and the Pantanow wetlands further south. The science behind it comes from the Moozayoo Gurldee, the city's long established research museum, and entry is free, though you'll need to book a ticket online, and it's closed on Wednesdays.

Outsiders tend to talk about the Amazon as a single thing, whether a forest, a lung or a cause, and the quiet argument of this building is that it has always been many places at once: rivers and floodplains, Indigenous nations, fishing towns, kitchens, and cities like this one, which has been sending boats up those rivers for four hundred years.

When you come back out, turn towards the gastronomy warehouse that opened alongside the museum, find something regional to eat, and carry it down to the water's edge, where the river the whole exhibition is about will be sliding past in front of you.
