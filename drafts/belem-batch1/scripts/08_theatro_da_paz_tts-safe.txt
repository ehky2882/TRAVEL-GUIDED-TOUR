Tayahtroo da Pahz

When it opened in February eighteen seventy eight, this theatre could seat around eleven hundred people, in a city that had grown rich on rubber almost faster than it could build.

Prassa da Hepooblika spreads out around you, shaded by tall mango trees, and the theatre sits along one edge of it: a long, pale neoclassical front with a run of columns across the upper floor and a triangular pediment on top. Stand back far enough to take in the whole width of it, because it's designed to be seen from the square.

The model was La Skahla in Milan, and the building took from eighteen sixty nine to eighteen seventy four to put up, with the opening delayed while irregularities in the works were investigated, which is a very theatrical start for a theatre. Almost everything that mattered was imported: English cast iron, Italian marble for the staircases, French chandeliers and Portuguese stone mosaics, while the floors were laid in Amazonian hardwoods.

Inside, the painted ceiling mixes Greek and Roman gods with the plants and animals of the Amazon, as if Olympus had relocated to the riverbank, and marble busts honour the writers Zhozeh jee Alenkar and Gonsalvez Jeeas alongside the composers Karlos Gomez and Enreekee Goorzhowng.

And here's the thing: for a century and more, this place and the Tayahtroo Amazonas, a thousand miles or so upriver in Manowss, were rivals, two rubber cities each insisting it had the finer opera house. In July twenty twenty six, Unesco settled the argument by not settling it at all, adding them to the World Heritage list together as the Theatres of the Amazon.

If the box office is offering a guided visit, take it, because the ceiling is the reason to go in; and on your way out, walk the length of Prassa da Hepooblika, where the old bandstand and the mango trees have been keeping the theatre company since the rubber years.
