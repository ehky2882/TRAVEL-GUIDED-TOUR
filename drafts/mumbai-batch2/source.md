=== 31 | st-marys-school-mazagaon | St. Mary's School, Mazagaon
Vantage: on Nesbit Road, Mazagaon, at the school gate, with St. Anne's Church beside it
---
Somewhere behind these walls, a boy called Farrokh Bulsara once sat in a classroom. The world would come to know him as Freddie Mercury.

Nesbit Road, Mazagaon. In front of you, the gates of St. Mary's School, and beyond them a cluster of buildings with pointed Gothic windows. Beside the school, St. Anne's Church — the two have stood together since the eighteen sixties, and it's hard to say where one ends and the other begins.

The church came first, in eighteen sixty. Four years later, in eighteen sixty-four, Jesuit priests opened the school next door. It didn't begin as a school for the city's comfortable families. It began as a Catholic school and a military orphanage — a place for the children of soldiers, many of whom had no one else. Only later were the gates opened to Catholic families more generally, and the school grew to fill four buildings on this site. Have a look at the largest of them, the senior school. Its foundation stone was laid by Lord Linlithgow, Viceroy of India from nineteen thirty-six to nineteen forty-three — one of the last men to run the Raj, laying a stone for a building that would teach the generation who lived after it.

Now the gift detail. The list of boys who passed through here is long and rather surprising. The conductor Zubin Mehta, who left Bombay for Vienna at eighteen. The industrialist Azim Premji. An Attorney General of India. And Freddie Mercury, who came here to finish his schooling in India before his family left for Zanzibar and, eventually, England.

[beat]

And here's the thing. A school founded for orphans of the army became one of the places that shaped modern Bombay — its courts, its businesses, its music. Nobody planning a military orphanage in eighteen sixty-four could have pictured a conductor in Vienna or a rock singer at Wembley. That's rather the point of schools: they are built for one city and end up serving another.

This is a working school, so stay on the pavement and keep the gates clear at the start and end of the day. Then have a look at St. Anne's Church next door, and walk on into Mazagaon — one of the oldest neighbourhoods in the city, where old houses and churches sit a short walk from the docks.

=== 32 | bmc-head-office | BMC Head Office
Vantage: on the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus side of the junction, looking across at the corner tower of the BMC building
---
The city put its own town hall directly across the road from its greatest railway station, and gave both jobs to the same architect.

Stand on the station side of the junction, where Dadabhai Naoroji Road meets Mahapalika Marg, and look across at the building on the corner. Grey-gold stone, rows of pointed arches, and a tall tower rising from the angle of the two streets, with a dome behind it. This is the headquarters of the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation — the body that runs the whole of Mumbai. Turn round, and the station behind you is Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus. Both are the work of Frederick William Stevens.

Here's how it came about. When the city wanted a home for its corporation, two designs were put forward: a Gothic one by Stevens, and an Indo-Saracenic one by Robert Fellowes Chisholm, which borrowed from Mughal and Indian architecture. Stevens won, though if you look carefully you'll see he hedged his bets — the domes and some of the detailing are a nod to the other side. The Viceroy, Lord Ripon, laid the foundation stone in December eighteen eighty-four, and the building was finished in eighteen ninety-three. The tower is about seventy-seven metres high.

Now look up at the gable above the main entrance. There's a winged female figure there, standing over the city's coat of arms. She represents Urbs Prima in Indis — the first city in India. Bombay wasn't shy about it, and this was the building where it said so in stone. At street level, by the entrance, there's a bronze of Sir Pherozeshah Mehta, the lawyer often called the father of the city's municipal government.

[beat]

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that this isn't a museum. A great many people work in it, and the water you drink in this city, the roads, the drains, are all argued over inside. For more than a century you could only admire it from out here.

That changed in twenty twenty-one, when the corporation and the state tourism board started running heritage walks inside, usually at weekends, starting from Gate Two. If you can book one, do. Otherwise, walk down the side on Mahapalika Marg and look at the carving on the arches — among the foliage you'll find animals, including a monkey, if you look hard enough.

=== 33 | david-sassoon-library | David Sassoon Library and Reading Room
Vantage: on Mahatma Gandhi Road in Kala Ghoda, on the pavement in front of the library's entrance portico
---
This building was put up for the mechanics of the Mint and the Dockyard. These days the members come for the quiet.

Mahatma Gandhi Road, Kala Ghoda. In front of you, a building in pale yellow stone, with pointed arches, a steep roof, and a portico over the entrance. Above the portico, a white marble bust of a bearded man. That's David Sassoon. Inside, at the foot of the stairs, he appears again, full length, in marble.

The story begins in eighteen forty-seven, when European workers at the government Mint and Dockyard set up a Mechanics' Institution — evening lectures and a library for men who worked with their hands. They needed a home. David Sassoon, a Baghdadi Jewish merchant who had made his fortune in Bombay, gave sixty thousand rupees, and the government paid the rest. The building went up between eighteen sixty-seven and eighteen seventy, designed by J. Campbell and G. E. Gosling, in yellow stone quarried at Malad. In time the mechanics drifted away, and by the nineteen twenties it had become the David Sassoon Library and Reading Room.

Have a look at the columns of the porch, and the carving around the arches. Look closely, and you'll find animals worked into the stone. Then look at the roof. That steep slope is new, and old at the same time. In the nineteen sixties the original pitched roof was taken off and replaced with a flat concrete one, which leaked for half a century. In a restoration finished in twenty twenty-three, led by the conservation architect Abha Narain Lambah, the sloping roof went back on. They kept the old Minton floor tiles, the antique switches and the chandeliers, and rehoused nearly thirty thousand books.

[beat]

And that's what I like about this place. It isn't a monument. It's a working library, open long hours, where people still come to sit and read a newspaper on the balcony, as members have done for a century and a half.

The reading rooms are for members, but visitors can usually step into the ground floor and the garden behind, which is one of the calmest spots in the Fort. When you come out, Jehangir Art Gallery is just across the road, and the black horse of Kala Ghoda a minute's walk away.
