A round pavilion standing out in the sea on a long pier, built so that summer visitors could take the air without getting their feet wet.

From the seafront at seh nee gal lee ah, walk out along the pier, eighty metres of it, and stop as you reach the ro ton da. The building sits on a platform about three metres above the water, with a central hall around sixteen metres across and a flat roofed colonnade running round it.

There's been something here since the middle of the eighteen hundreds. The architect vin chen tso ghee nel lee designed a timber platform on piles in eighteen fifty, and again in eighteen sixty five, a gathering place for holidaymakers with a whiff of the health cure about it, inspired by the fashion for sea water therapy. In nineteen ten it was enlarged and linked to the hotel ban yee.

The building you see now went up in nineteen thirty two and nineteen thirty three, to a design by an engineer called kar del lee, chosen, the records say, because it was cheaper and suited the taste of the time.

It has had a hard life for a holiday building. It was badly damaged in the war, repaired, reshaped, weakened by storms and earthquakes in the nineteen seventies, briefly a discotheque in the nineteen eighties, and then badly burned in nineteen ninety two.

And yet the town kept coming back to it. In two thousand and six, after a restoration costing more than two and a half million euros, it reopened, and now it holds exhibitions and conferences in the summer.

I think it's the most cheerful kind of landmark, a building whose only job was to let people enjoy the sea. seh nee gal lee ah's beach has long been called the velvet beach, for its fine sand, and the ro ton da is its emblem.

Before you walk back, stand at the far side of the ro ton da, with the town behind you and nothing but the ay dree at ik in front. That's the feeling the whole thing was built to sell.
