# Cattedrale di Troia

*337 words, about 135 seconds at a natural pace.*

## Clean script

Eleven columns, eleven segments, and a rose window so finely cut that it's been called embroidery in stone.

From the street in front of the cathedral in Troia, look up at the facade, nineteen metres wide and nearly thirty high. Halfway up is the rose window. Below it, in the centre, the great bronze door.

The cathedral was built between ten ninety-three and eleven twenty-five, in the Romanesque style of Puglia, on top of an older Byzantine church, using stones taken from the Roman town that stood here. Bishop Guglielmo the Second paid for much of it, and you'll find his name carved inside.

Now the rose window. Eleven little columns radiate from the centre at equal angles, dividing it into eleven segments, each one filled with carved openwork as fine as lace. The odd number makes it slightly asymmetrical, which is part of its charm. The usual reading is that the eleven columns are the apostles without Peter, the circle at the centre is Christ, and the carved pattern round the edge is eternity.

Then look at the bronze door, made in eleven nineteen by Oderisio of Benevento. It has twenty-eight panels, and among the figures he included himself and the bishop who paid him. Two dragons in the fourth row guard the treasure of the church. Round the side is a second, simpler bronze door, by the same man, from eleven twenty-seven, made when money was tighter.

[beat]

Bronze doors are rare in Italian churches of this age. Troia has two, by the same artist, eight years apart, and you can see the budget shrink between them.

The rose window has been through two earthquakes, in seventeen thirty-one and in two thousand and two, and was carefully restored afterwards. It's so admired that it once appeared on an Italian banknote, the old five thousand lire.

Before you go in, read the inscription above the central door. It says, roughly, may this material door give us a spiritual way in. It's a good thought to carry inside.
