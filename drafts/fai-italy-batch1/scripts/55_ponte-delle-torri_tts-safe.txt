Two hundred and thirty metres long, eighty metres high, and gur ta said it looked like a second nature.

Stand at the end of the bridge near the rock ka, the great fortress on the hill above spo leh toh, and look across. Nine tall stone arches stride over the gorge to the slopes of mon teh loo ko, the wooded mountain opposite. A wall runs along the top on the side you're walking, about twelve metres high.

Nobody is quite sure when it was built. It probably took its present form at the end of the thirteen hundreds, and it may stand on the remains of something Roman, though nothing Roman obviously survives. It's often linked to the great building works that began after thirteen sixty three, when Cardinal al bor noth started work on the fortress behind you.

Here's the gift: it isn't really a road bridge at all. It was an aqueduct. A channel along the top carried water from springs on mon teh loo ko into the town, to the fortress and to the fountains of spo leh toh. The high wall was built to protect it.

At the far end is a smaller fort, the for tee lee tsyo day moo lee nee, built to guard the crossing, and it worked as a mill until the eighteen hundreds. A couple of the piers rising from the valley floor are hollow, with rooms for guards inside.

In October seventeen eighty six, gur ta came this way and was enchanted. He wrote that it was the third work of the ancients he had seen that had the same grand spirit, a second nature working for human purposes. There's a plaque recording his visit.

After the earthquakes of twenty sixteen, the bridge was closed for eight years, and it reopened in December twenty twenty four.

When you've crossed, keep going. At the far end, near the Fortilizio, a path called the jee ro day kon dot tee follows the line of an old aqueduct along the slopes of mon teh loo ko, past ancient hermitages, with the whole bridge and town opening up behind you.
