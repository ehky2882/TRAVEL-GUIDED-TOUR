villa gam beh ry ah, set teen yah no

The New York Times once put this garden on its list of twenty five you should see in your lifetime. It hardly raises its voice.

You're on the hillside at set teen yah no, above Florence. Find the long grass walk that runs the length of the garden, with the villa on one side. At one end, the walk opens out to the view: the city of Florence and the valley of the ar no, laid out below you.

The name may come from gam beh ree, the freshwater crayfish once raised in ponds round here. In the fourteen and fifteen hundreds the land belonged to the gam beh rel lee, the family of the sculptors an toe nee oh and bair nar doh ros sel lee no. But the villa itself was built by tsa no bee lah pee, whose name and the date sixteen ten are recorded in an inscription.

Then came the kap po nee family, in the early seventeen hundreds, and they gave the garden much of its shape. That long grass walk was once a bowling green. Off it are small, separate garden rooms. Look for the grotto garden, the nim fee um, with its sculpture in relief, still fed by water from the springs on the hill above. And look for the lemon house, which goes back to the very beginning.

The most famous thing here, though, came much later. In eighteen ninety six, a Romanian princess, zhan ghee ka, bought the run down villa, and around nineteen hundred she swept away an old vegetable patch in front of the house and replaced it with a water parterre: long, still pools, mirrored side by side, edged with box and cypress. ee dith wor ton didn't care for it. Almost everyone else since has disagreed with her.

The house burned in August nineteen forty four, set alight by retreating German soldiers, and it was rescued ten years later by mar chel lo mar kee, who restored it. The gardens opened to the public in nineteen fifty six. The villa is still a private home, but the garden can be visited by ticket, so check the calendar and book before you come.

Before you leave, stand at the end of the water parterre and look back at the house reflected in the pools. It's the picture everybody takes, and for once, everybody's right.
