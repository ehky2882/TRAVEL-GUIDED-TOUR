One of the greatest poets ever to write in Italian came to this hillside to grow old. These days the whole village carries his name.

You're on vee ah val leh sel leh in ar kwah peh trar ka, in the yoo gay nee an Hills. Find the gate of the house, a stone building from the twelve hundreds that still keeps much of its fourteenth century character, set on the slope above the village.

fran ches ko peh trar ka, pet rark to us, came here in thirteen sixty nine. The house may have been a gift from fran ches ko da kar rah ra, the lord of pad yoo ah, and pet rark spent his last five years here, reading, writing and gardening. He died here, in his little study, on the night of the eighteenth to the nineteenth of July, thirteen seventy four. Tradition says he was found with his head resting on his books.

Inside, the rooms were painted in the fifteen hundreds with scenes from his own poems, from the kan tso nyeh reh, the songs for Laura, and from his Latin epic Africa. In the central room, look for the scenes from his song of metamorphoses, where love keeps changing the poet into something new.

Then find the cat. In a niche is a mummified cat, and for centuries visitors were told it was pet rark's own. It's a lovely story. It also seems to have been invented in the sixteen hundreds by one of the later owners of the house, a man called jee ro la mo ga bree el lee, who knew exactly what his visitors wanted.

And I rather like that. Even the fake bits of this house are about love for the poet.

When you leave, walk down into the village to the parish church of santa maria as soon ta. In front of it stands pet rark's tomb, a sarcophagus of red Verona marble raised six years after his death by his son in law. The Latin on it begins: this stone covers the cold bones of fran ches ko peh trar ka. It's a very quiet end for such a famous man.
