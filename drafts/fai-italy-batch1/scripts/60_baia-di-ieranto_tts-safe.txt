According to the Roman writer plin ee the Elder, this is where yoo liss eez met the Sirens. It's also where a steelworks once quarried limestone.

You've walked down from neh rah no, and here's the bay: a deep green inlet at the very end of the sor ren toe peninsula, the only one of its kind on this tip of land, and directly across the water, the great sea stacks of kah pree, the fa ral yo nee.

Look up to your left, to the headland of mon tal toh, and you'll see an old stone watchtower. It's one of the defensive towers built along this coast in the fifteen hundreds, when sa ra sen pirates were a real and regular danger. Another stands out on poon ta kam pa nel la, the very tip of the peninsula.

The quieter history is the quarry. From the early nineteen hundreds, limestone was dug out of these cliffs, and from nineteen eighteen the quarry belonged to the steel company eel va, part of what later became ee tal see der. Workers came all the way from Sardinia to cut the rock. It closed for good in nineteen fifty two.

And then the company did something unusual. Rather than sell the land for villas, it gave the whole bay to the fy, in the late nineteen eighties, so it couldn't be built on. That's why it looks like this. The water belongs to the poon ta kam pa nel la marine reserve, and the land has been left to the olives, the scrub and the walkers.

There are no bars and no bins down here. Bring water, and take everything home with you.

If you've the energy for one more climb, go up to the tor reh dee mon tal toh. From there you see the whole story at once: the quarry scars healing over, the bay below, and kah pree sitting out in the haze, exactly where the Sirens would have been singing.
