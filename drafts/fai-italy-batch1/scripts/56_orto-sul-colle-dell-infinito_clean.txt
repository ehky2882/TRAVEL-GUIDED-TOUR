Orto sul Colle dell'Infinito, Recanati

Two hundred years ago a young man sat in this garden, looked past a hedge, and wrote fifteen lines that every Italian knows by heart.

You're at the edge of the old town of Recanati, in the garden beside the sixteenth-century monastery of Santo Stefano. Walk to the edge and look out. On a clear day the gentle lines of the Sibillini mountains, and in the other direction, the sea.

For centuries this was the nuns' enclosed garden, a hortus conclusus, planted with flowers, vegetables and fruit trees. A few steps away is the house where Giacomo Leopardi was born, and in eighteen nineteen, aged about twenty-one, he came up to this hill and conceived his poem L'infinito, The Infinite.

The poem is short, and it's built on a trick of the eye. Leopardi says this solitary hill was always dear to him, and so was the hedge that shut out most of the far horizon. Because he couldn't see it, he imagined it: endless spaces, superhuman silences, the deepest quiet. And in the end, he says, it's sweet to drown in that sea.

[beat]

So the point isn't the view. It's the bit of the view he couldn't see. The FAI puts it beautifully: here infinity isn't a landscape outside you, it's a place inside you.

The garden came to the FAI in twenty seventeen, entrusted by the town of Recanati and Leopardi's study centres, and it's been restored and opened to everyone. The visit now includes a short journey through the poem itself, with projections and words from Leopardi's notebooks.

Before you leave, step into the Centro Nazionale di Studi Leopardiani next door, a clean modern building from nineteen thirty-seven. Its library holds more than twelve thousand volumes, and among them, the first printed edition of L'infinito.
