Villa Della Porta Bozzolo

Most villa gardens march straight out from the front door. This one runs off sideways, up the hill, and that's rather the point of it.

From the parterre in front of the villa, turn to face the slope. A stone staircase climbs through four terraces, each with its balustrade and its statues, all the way up to a lawn framed by cypresses near the top of the hill. That lawn is called the theatre, and you're standing in the stalls.

The house began in the sixteenth century as a modest country place for a notary called Giroldino Della Porta, the founder of the family. Two hundred years later, his descendant Gian Angelo the Third married Isabella Giulini, in seventeen fourteen, and the couple decided the old place needed remaking. The terraces date from then. So does the fishpond up at the theatre, from seventeen twenty-three.

Inside, the walls were painted between seventeen twenty-four and seventeen twenty-six. Have a look for the ballroom, where painted landscapes open up the walls as if the room had no walls at all, and the gallery upstairs, full of sirens, nymphs and tritons. Then find the billiard room. It holds a rare example of a game called biribissi, an eighteenth-century gamble that's been described as the ancestor of roulette.

And here's the thing. This was never just a pleasure house. Downstairs and round the back are the working buildings: an ice house, a spinning mill for silk, a cellar of old barrels, and a huge wooden wine press from the sixteenth century. A villa of delight, yes, but one that still had to pay its way.

In the late eighteen hundreds the house went to Senator Camillo Bozzolo, a doctor who'd fought with Garibaldi, and in nineteen eighty-nine his heirs gave it to the FAI.

When you've done the rooms, climb all the way up to the theatre and keep going. A path leads on into the woods to a viewpoint over the Valcuvia, with the little fifteenth-century church of San Bernardino close by.
