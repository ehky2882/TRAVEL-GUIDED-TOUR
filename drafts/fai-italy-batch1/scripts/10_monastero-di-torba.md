# Monastero di Torba

*342 words, about 137 seconds at a natural pace.*

## Clean script

This tower was built to keep the barbarians out. Then the barbarians moved in, and after them, the nuns.

Stand in the meadow where you can see the whole group: the tall stone tower, the low church of Santa Maria beside it, and the old monastery buildings with their portico. Behind you is the wooded valley of the Olona.

The tower is the oldest thing here, part of a Roman fort built in the late fourth or fifth century, when this valley was a frontier. It's one of the rare pieces of late Roman military building to survive in the north of Italy. After the Romans came the Goths, the Byzantines and then the Lombards, and each of them used the fort in turn.

In the eighth century, Benedictine nuns arrived and turned the tower into a chapel. Climb to the second floor and you'll find what they painted: Christ blessing, with angels, the Virgin and Child, and along one wall a procession of nuns in prayer. One of the women buried in the tower has a name that's unmistakably Lombard, Aliberga. The church next door has an eighth-century crypt beneath it.

Here's the thing about Torba. It's a place that kept changing its mind about what it was for. Fort, convent, and then, after the nuns left for good in the fourteen eighties and Napoleon closed what remained, a farm. The church became a store for carts. The frescoes vanished under fresh plaster.

[beat]

It was rescued in the nineteen seventies by Giulia Maria Crespi, one of the founders of the FAI, who bought it and gave it to the foundation. Since twenty eleven it's been part of the UNESCO site for the Lombards in Italy, together with Castelseprio up the hill.

Before you go, step into the church to see its newest addition, an altar designed and given by the architect Mario Botta in twenty twenty-five. Then, if you've the time, take the path up through the woods to the ruins of Castelseprio, which is the other half of the story.

## Fact-check notes

- Acquisition: the FAI body text says 1976, the FAI headline and Wikipedia say 1977. Script says 'the nineteen seventies'.
- The tower date is 'fifth to sixth century' in Wikipedia, while the castrum is fourth to fifth. The script ties the date to the fort.
- 'Aliberga' is Wikipedia's spelling, citing FAI.
- The nuns left for Tradate in 1482 (Wikipedia); FAI's 'over seven centuries' was not used.
- The recent dedication of the monastery to Crespi was not found in these sources, so it isn't mentioned.
- The Botta altar (2025) is from the FAI page.
