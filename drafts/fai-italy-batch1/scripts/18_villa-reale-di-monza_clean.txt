Villa Reale di Monza

Around seven hundred rooms, and the architect was given three years to finish them.

Stand in the great courtyard in front of the main facade. The palace wraps round you in a long U, pale and calm, with the town of Monza behind you and the park beyond the building.

In seventeen seventy-seven the commission went to the imperial architect Giuseppe Piermarini. The client was the Archduke Ferdinand of Habsburg, son of the Empress Maria Theresa and governor of Milan, who wanted a summer residence in the country. Piermarini delivered it in about three years.

Then the regimes changed and the palace changed with them. Napoleon's stepson, Eugène de Beauharnais, made it his residence as Viceroy of Italy, and that's when it became the Villa Reale, the Royal Villa. The vast park behind it was founded by decree of Napoleon himself in eighteen oh five. It's seven hundred and twenty hectares, one of the largest walled parks in Europe, and somewhere in it is the Formula One circuit.

After unification it became the favourite house of King Umberto the First. And this is where the story turns. On the twenty-ninth of July, nineteen hundred, Umberto was shot dead in Monza by the anarchist Gaetano Bresci.

[beat]

His son never forgave the place. Victor Emmanuel the Third closed the villa, and much of its furniture went off to Rome. It stayed shut, more or less, for a century, until the restoration that reopened it in twenty fourteen.

Inside, look for the royal apartments: the King's armoury and study, and Queen Margherita's sitting room and boudoir, and the billiard room with its tables by the cabinetmaker Giuseppe Maggiolini.

When you come out, walk round to the rose garden, with about a thousand varieties, and then keep going into the park. On a weekday morning you can walk for an hour and hardly see another soul.
