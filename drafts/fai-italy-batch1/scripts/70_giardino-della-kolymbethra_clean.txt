Giardino della Kolymbethra, Agrigento

In the middle of the Valley of the Temples there's a green hollow full of oranges, and two and a half thousand years ago it was a reservoir.

From the edge of the valley near the Temple of the Dioscuri, look down. Below the temples' golden stone is a deep, sheltered cleft filled with citrus trees, olives and almonds. That's the Kolymbethra, and you go down into it.

According to the Greek historian Diodorus, in four eighty BC Theron, the tyrant of Akragas, as Agrigento was then called, had a network of underground galleries dug to bring water to the city. They ended at the foot of the town in a great pool, the kolymbethra. Later it became a fish pond, and later still, when the land passed to the Church, citrus trees were planted in the hollow. The water that the Greeks brought here has been growing oranges ever since.

Look for the entrances to the hypogea, the ancient underground channels. Their passages run for a hundred and eighty-five metres, and they're a remarkable bit of ancient hydraulic engineering, still feeding the garden.

Then simply walk. There's the citrus grove, the almond and olive terraces, some of the olives centuries old, and the wild plants of Sicily in between: myrtle, terebinth, lentisk and broom.

By the late twentieth century the garden had been abandoned. In nineteen ninety-nine the Region of Sicily handed it over to be rescued, and the old irrigation channels were restored and the garden brought back. It's five hectares, and it feels like a secret kept from the busy path above.

Remember you'll need a ticket for the Valley of the Temples as well as for the garden.

Before you climb back up, find a spot under the olives where you can see the columns of the temple above the orange trees. Greeks, monks and farmers all left their mark on this one small valley, and from down here you can see all three.
