# Ippodromo San Siro and Leonardo's Horse

*324 words, about 130 seconds at a natural pace.*

## Clean script

Leonardo da Vinci spent years on a horse that was never cast. Five hundred years later, somebody finished the job.

Piazzale dello Sport, outside the racecourse at San Siro. In front of you, on its plinth, is a bronze horse a little over seven metres tall, caught mid-stride.

In fourteen eighty-two Ludovico Sforza, Il Moro, Duke of Milan, asked Leonardo to make the largest equestrian statue in the world, in honour of his father, Francesco Sforza. Leonardo worked on it for years, and in fourteen ninety-three a full-size clay model went on show in Milan. The bronze was set aside to cast it. Then war came. The metal went to make cannon, and when the French took the city in fourteen ninety-nine, their soldiers used the clay horse for target practice until there was nothing left.

That might have been the end of it. But in nineteen seventy-seven an American called Charles Dent set out to make it. He spent years raising money and died in nineteen ninety-four before it was done. The supermarket owner Frederik Meijer took on the cost, on one condition: there would be two horses, one for Milan and one for his gardens in Michigan. The sculptor Nina Akamu shaped it, and the horse was unveiled here in September nineteen ninety-nine.

[beat]

So it's not quite Leonardo's horse, and it never could be. It's a promise kept by strangers, five centuries late, and I think that's rather lovely.

The racecourse behind it opened in nineteen twenty, designed by Paolo Vietti-Violi, and its buildings are full of Liberty-style detail. The area around the horse, and the small gallery about the racecourse, are free to visit every day.

If you've the time, follow the self-guided trail round the grounds. There are seven markers to find, and a botanical park with more than seventy species of trees. Look out for the Palazzina del Peso, the old weighing house, one of the original Liberty buildings.
