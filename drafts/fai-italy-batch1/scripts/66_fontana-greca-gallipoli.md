# Fontana Greca, Gallipoli

*352 words, about 141 seconds at a natural pace.*

## Clean script

It's called the Greek Fountain. It isn't Greek, and it's been moved twice, but it's still one of the most beautiful things in Gallipoli.

At the foot of the bridge to the old town of Gallipoli, stop in front of the tall carved stone front of the fountain, about five metres high. Four carved figures hold up the structure and divide it into three panels of relief, with a trough for animals below.

For a long time people believed it was ancient Greek, from the third century BC. These days most scholars think it's from the Renaissance, in the fifteen hundreds. It started out at a spring by the old baths outside the town, was moved in fifteen forty-eight, and in fifteen sixty it was taken down and rebuilt here, with new Latin inscriptions added.

The three panels each tell a myth about a woman turned into water. Have a look at them in turn. Dirce, tied to a bull. Salmacis, whose body was fused with the young man she loved into a single spring. And Byblis, who loved her own brother, wept for him when he rejected her, and was turned into a fountain of tears.

[beat]

So it's a rather sombre fountain, when you read it properly. The inscriptions under the scenes are moral warnings: about desire, about envy, about going too far.

Walk round the back and you'll find a second face, added in seventeen sixty-five to hold the whole thing up, with the arms of Gallipoli and the insignia of the Bourbon king Charles the Third. Well within living memory, in the nineteen fifties, people still filled small barrels with water here and sold it by donkey cart to houses without running water.

The fountain needs looking after. The old front has cracks running through it, and in twenty twenty-five it was recognised in a national vote for places worth saving, which should help pay for its restoration. Take a good look at the carving while you can. Then cross the bridge into the old town, which is what everyone who drank here was on their way to do.

## Fact-check notes

- The back face (1765) carries the arms of Charles III (Carlo III di Borbone), not Ferdinand IV.
- Byblis was turned into a fountain: it.wiki says by the gods, salento.info says by the muses.
- The FAI recognition in June 2025 and the cracks are from it.wiki.
- The 1950s water selling is from it.wiki.
