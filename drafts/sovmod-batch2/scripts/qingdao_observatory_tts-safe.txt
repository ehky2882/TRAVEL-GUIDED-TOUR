Chingdow Observatory

A shipwreck in a summer storm is the reason this hilltop has a German stone observatory on it.

On the summit of gwanshyang Hill, in the old centre of chingdow, the observatory faces you, a solid stone building with a tower, sitting among trees at the highest point of the hill, with the red roofs of the former German quarter spread out below.

In July eighteen ninety six a German navy gunboat, the iltis, was caught by a storm and sank off the Shandong coast, and the navy decided that one of the causes was the lack of reliable weather forecasts. Two years later, after Germany had taken this stretch of coast as a leased territory, it set up a meteorological and astronomical station in chingdow.

The station moved up to this hill in nineteen oh five, and the main building you see was begun in June nineteen ten to designs by the architect hine rikh shoobart and completed in January nineteen twelve. By then it was observing weather, earthquakes, magnetism, tides, sunspots and time, and it counted among the three great observatories of the Far East.

Look at how heavy the building is, because it was built to look permanent, a piece of imperial confidence in stone, and yet the German lease ended in nineteen fourteen, only two years after it opened.

What it became is the real story, because the observatory carried on working through every change of ruler that followed, and it played a real part in developing modern meteorology and marine science in China, so a building born from colonial ambition ended up serving the science of the country it was built in.

The complex now belongs partly to the navy and partly to the Purple Mountain Observatory, so you may not get inside. Walk instead around the park on the hilltop, which has a long covered walkway marked with the twenty four solar terms of the Chinese calendar, and look out over the old town towards the sea.
