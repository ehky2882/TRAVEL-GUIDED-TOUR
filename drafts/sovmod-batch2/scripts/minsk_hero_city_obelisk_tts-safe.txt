Minsk Hero City Obelisk

Minsk had to wait thirty years after the war to be officially named a hero city, and the reason is a fairly uncomfortable story.

On a low hill where Victors Avenue meets masherow Avenue, the obelisk rises in front of you, forty five metres of pale stone narrowing to a gold star wreathed in laurel, with a bronze female figure at its foot raising long trumpets, and behind it the glass dome of the war museum.

It opened on the eighth of May nineteen eighty five, for the fortieth anniversary of victory, designed by the sculptor valentin zankovich with the architects Viktor kramarenko, who also co designed the Moskva cinema further down this avenue, together with Yevseev and Romanenko. The female figure represents the Motherland, sounding a fanfare over the city.

The title itself, Hero City, was granted on the twenty sixth of June nineteen seventy four, to honour the city's endurance during eleven hundred days of German occupation. That delay is the gift detail, because for years Moscow had treated the Minsk underground resistance with suspicion, as possibly compromised, and it was only formally cleared in nineteen fifty nine.

Read the engraved text at the base, which sets out the decree granting the title. It's an unusual thing to carve into a monument, an official document rather than a dedication, and it says a lot about how badly the city wanted the recognition on the record.

Today the obelisk is the backdrop for the Independence Day parade and for fireworks on Victory Day, and newly married couples come here to leave flowers and have their photographs taken. Walk round behind it to the museum, which opened in its new building in twenty fourteen, and look back from the terrace, where the obelisk lines up perfectly with the long sweep of the avenue towards the centre.
