The Flying Saucer, keev

The architect of this building hid its supports on purpose, so that the saucer on the corner would look as if it were hovering just above the pavement.

On libidska Square, at the end of antonovicha Street, look for the round, disc shaped hall resting at the foot of a tall slab block, and you're looking at the building keev simply calls the Flying Saucer.

It was finished in nineteen seventy one, designed by the architects florian yooryev and lev novikov for the Institute of Scientific and Technical Information, and the complex combines three parts, a long two storey building, a sixteen storey tower, and the saucer itself, which holds a lecture and cinema hall.

The design grew out of the nineteen sixties, when space flight and science fiction made the future feel close enough to touch, and yooryev had hoped the saucer would house a light and music theatre. The hall has remarkable acoustics, able to carry the whole range of the human voice, though it ended up as the library's lecture hall rather than a concert venue.

By the nineteen nineties the building was decaying, and in twenty fifteen plans for a vast shopping centre next door threatened to swallow it, until a campaign to save keev's modernist buildings took up its cause, with yooryev himself writing an open letter.

The happy part is that the campaign worked, and in October twenty twenty the ministry of culture added the Flying Saucer to the national register of architectural monuments, so a building designed to look like it had landed from the future has been formally given a future of its own. Walk round the disc to find the angle where its supports disappear, and then follow antonovicha Street north towards the city centre, where more of keev's post war modernism lines the route.
