Musa Dagh Memorial, Musaler
Hilltop north-east of Musaler village, Armavir Province, Armenia · 40.170378, 44.372951

In the summer of 1915, the people of six Armenian villages on a mountain by the Mediterranean refused to be deported and held out on its slopes for fifty-three days.

That mountain was Musa Dagh, in what is now Turkey's Hatay province, and the monument in front of you, on a low rise at the north-eastern edge of the village of Musaler, was built to remember it, a stern stone mass with sharp, angled walls that looks less like a statue than a small fortress dropped onto the hill.

The village around you was largely settled in 1946 by families returning from Lebanon, Syria and Egypt, many of them descendants of those same six villages, and the memorial was built with their money and their own labour, together with Musa Dagh families abroad. The architect was Rafael Israelyan, and the sculpture is by Ara Harutyunyan.

The story it marks ended at sea, when French warships spotted the defenders just as their food and ammunition were running out, and evacuated around four thousand people to Port Said in Egypt, an episode that later inspired Franz Werfel's novel, The Forty Days of Musa Dagh.

The fortress shape is the point here, because rather than a mourning figure or a grieving mother, the community chose an image of a stronghold that didn't fall, which is a very particular way for survivors and their grandchildren to tell their own history.

At the foot of the hill, next to the monument, you'll find the graves of men from Musaler who died in the Karabakh war, so the memorial now holds two generations of the same families. On a clear day, turn south from the top of the rise, because Mount Ararat fills the horizon, and the mountain the villagers left behind lies far beyond it.
