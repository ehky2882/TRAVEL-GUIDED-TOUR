salaspils Memorial

The first thing you hear here is a steady ticking, like a heartbeat, and it carries right across the field.

From the car park, follow the path through the pine and birch woods until a vast, slanting concrete wall blocks your way, with a narrow passage cut through it, and once you pass through, a wide field opens out, with huge rough concrete figures standing directly on the grass.

This was the site of a Nazi camp that operated in the forest here from nineteen forty one to nineteen forty four, and the memorial was opened on the thirty first of October nineteen sixty seven, designed by the architects goonars asaris, ivars strowtmanis, olyerts ostenbergs and olegs zakamenniys with the sculptors lev bookovsky, olegs skarainis and yahnis zarinsh. The words on the wall come from the poet eyzhens vayveris, himself a prisoner here, and they say that behind this gate the earth groans.

The figures each have a name, the Unbroken, the Humiliated, Protest, the Oath, Red Front, Solidarity and the Mother. They're cast in rough pebbly concrete, angular and heavy, and they have no plinths at all, so they share the ground with you rather than towering over it.

Around the edges of the field, low concrete blocks mark the sites of the barracks, and at the place where children were held, visitors still leave small toys, while the ticking you heard is a metronome beating out the rhythm of a heart.

The design team won a Lenin Prize in nineteen seventy, and the memorial is now part of Latvia's cultural canon, as one of the strongest meetings of architecture and sculpture of its era, its message carried by scale and silence rather than by any written explanation.

Take the circular path around the field, which the designers called the road of life, and walk it slowly, letting the figures come into view one by one before you return through the gate.
