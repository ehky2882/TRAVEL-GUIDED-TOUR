Tsitsernakaberd, Armenian Genocide Memorial
Tsitsernakaberd hill, Yerevan, Armenia · 40.185821, 44.488114

The name of this hill means the swallows' fortress, and legend says the swallows carried messages between two ancient Armenian gods.

Follow the main approach across the hilltop above the Hrazdan gorge, and a long wall of grey stone runs beside you, while ahead a tall, narrow spike of stone rises next to a ring of large slabs leaning in towards one another, forming a closed circle with a gap to walk through.

It was completed in 1967, two years after demonstrations in Yerevan marked the fiftieth anniversary of the genocide of 1915, and it was designed by the architects Arthur Tarkhanyan and Sashur Kalashyan. Every year on the twenty-fourth of April, people walk up this hill in their thousands and lay fresh flowers around the flame.

The wall beside the path is about a hundred metres long, carved with the names of towns and villages where massacres and deportations took place. Inside the ring, twelve slabs bow towards an eternal flame that burns about a metre and a half below the ground, and the forty-four-metre spike beside it is split from top to bottom into two parts, often read as one nation divided between Armenia and its diaspora.

Nothing here tells you how to feel, and that restraint is the memorial's strength. There are no figures, no heroics and no explanation carved into the stone, only names, a flame and a crack running through the column, and the hill does the rest.

This is a place of mourning, so keep your voice low inside the ring. When you come out, follow the path towards the museum built into the hillside, and look for the alley of trees nearby, each one planted by a visiting head of state or public figure, which has become a quiet record of who has come here to pay respects.
