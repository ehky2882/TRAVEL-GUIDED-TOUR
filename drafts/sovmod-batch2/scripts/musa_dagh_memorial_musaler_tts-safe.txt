Moossa Dahg Memorial, Moossaler

In the summer of nineteen fifteen, the people of six Armenian villages on a mountain by the Mediterranean refused to be deported and held out on its slopes for fifty three days.

That mountain was moossa dahg, in what is now Turkey's hatai province, and the monument in front of you, on a low rise at the north eastern edge of the village of moossaler, was built to remember it, a stern stone mass with sharp, angled walls that looks less like a statue than a small fortress dropped onto the hill.

The village around you was largely settled in nineteen forty six by families returning from Lebanon, Syria and Egypt, many of them descendants of those same six villages, and the memorial was built with their money and their own labour, together with moossa dahg families abroad. The architect was rafa el izrayelyan, and the sculpture is by ahra harootyoonyan.

The story it marks ended at sea, when French warships spotted the defenders just as their food and ammunition were running out, and evacuated around four thousand people to port sigh eed in Egypt, an episode that later inspired frants verfel's novel, The Forty Days of moossa dahg.

The fortress shape is the point here, because rather than a mourning figure or a grieving mother, the community chose an image of a stronghold that didn't fall, which is a very particular way for survivors and their grandchildren to tell their own history.

At the foot of the hill, next to the monument, you'll find the graves of men from moossaler who died in the Karabakh war, so the memorial now holds two generations of the same families. On a clear day, turn south from the top of the rise, because Mount ararat fills the horizon, and the mountain the villagers left behind lies far beyond it.
