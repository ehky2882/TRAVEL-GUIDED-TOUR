Bash Aparan Battle Memorial
Hill above the Aparan to Spitak highway, Aparan, Armenia · 40.599787, 44.352137

In May 1918, about a thousand Armenian riflemen stopped an advancing army in the hills around this town, and this memorial was built to make sure nobody forgot it.

A kilometre and a half outside Aparan, on the left of the road towards Spitak, a paved terrace on a rise leads you towards three tall columns of dark tuff stone rise against the sky, with the long slopes of Mount Aragats behind them on a clear day.

The architect was Rafael Israelyan, who worked on it with his son Areg between 1974 and 1979, and the three columns each carry a separate dedication. One remembers the victims of the genocide of 1915, one, shaped like the blade of a sword, honours the battle fought here, and the third lists local men who died in the Second World War.

The battle itself lasted several days, from the twenty-first of May, and it was one of three victories that spring, alongside Sardarapat and Karakilisa, which halted the Ottoman advance and allowed the short-lived First Republic of Armenia to exist at all.

Look for the small basalt pyramid nearby, which is the grave of the commander at Aparan, Drastamat Kanayan, known simply as Dro, reburied here in May 2000. His later career remains debated, so the grave is a reminder that memorials often hold more complicated people than their inscriptions suggest.

What gives this hilltop its weight is the combination, because it gathers a genocide, a victory and a world war onto a single terrace, as if the town wanted one place where all of its twentieth century could be mourned together. Before you leave, walk round to the far side of the sword column, where the land drops away and the ground the battle was fought over spreads out below you.
