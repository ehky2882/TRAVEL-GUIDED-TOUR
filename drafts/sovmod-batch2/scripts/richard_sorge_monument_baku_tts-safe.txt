Monument to Richard Sorgeh, Baku

This is one of very few monuments to a spy that actually looks like what it is about, watching.

In the small park named after him, richard sorgeh's monument stands among pines, plane trees and mulberries, and facing it you should see a long, curved sheet of bronze on a granite base, bent like a radar dish, with part of a man's face emerging from the middle of it.

sorgeh was born in eighteen ninety five in saboonchoo, an oil suburb of Baku, to a German engineer and a Russian mother, and he grew up to become a Soviet intelligence officer working undercover as a German journalist in Tokyo, under the codename ramzi. In nineteen forty one he warned Moscow about Hitler's plans to invade, and later that year he reported that Japan would not attack the Soviet Union, before he was arrested and, in nineteen forty four, executed in Japan.

Look closely at the face, because the sculptor, vladimir tsigal, showed only the central band of it, the brow, eyes and nose, and the eyes are cut right through the bronze, so you can see the trees and the sky behind them, and from most angles it feels as though the gaze is going straight through you.

The monument was unveiled in May nineteen eighty one, on the eve of Victory Day, after tsigal had travelled to Japan to talk with people who knew sorgeh.

The design is cleverer than it first appears, since rather than a hero striding forward with a flag, it's a portrait of a man you can never see clearly, which is a fairly honest way to remember someone whose whole career depended on not being looked at.

On your way out, step behind the bronze and look through the eyes from the other side. Then search the planting nearby for a cherry tree, put in at the opening in soil brought from Japan, which is a quietly generous gesture to the country where his story ended.
