Pyramid of Tirana

Few buildings in Europe have been a dictator's museum, a conference hall, a NATO base, a television studio and a coding school, all inside forty years.

The Pyramid sits just off the main boulevard, a low, splayed concrete form whose sloping sides run down almost to the pavement in front of you, now stepped with broad stairs that climb straight up its face, with brightly coloured boxes now tucked into the structure.

It opened in October nineteen eighty eight as the Enver hodja Museum, dedicated to the communist leader who had died three years earlier, and it was designed by a team that included his own daughter, pranvaira hodja, and her husband, Klement kolanetchi. When it went up, it was said to be the most expensive single building ever constructed in Albania.

Within three years the regime was gone and the museum with it. The building became a conference centre, served as a NATO base during the Kosovo war in nineteen ninety nine, housed a television channel from two thousand and one, and slowly fell apart around it, its marble cladding stripped away and stored outside the city.

There were serious plans to demolish it and put a new parliament here, and a petition against demolition gathered thousands of signatures. In twenty seventeen the decision went the other way, and the Dutch practice em vee ar dee vee reworked the shell into a technology centre for young people, which reopened in October twenty twenty three.

What happened here is rare enough to notice, because the city didn't erase the monument or restore it as a shrine, it simply handed it to the next generation and let them climb all over it, which is arguably the most complete answer a building like this could get. Take the new steps right to the top, then look back down the boulevard towards Skanderbeg Square, where the view runs straight along the city's main ceremonial boulevard.
