Sava Tsentar, Belgrade

The architect of this building was given just one month to come up with the whole concept, and he spent much of it on aeroplanes.

On milenteeya popovicha Street, in New Belgrade's Blok nineteen, the long, low congress building in front of you is sava tsentar, spread out horizontally beneath its broad roof, with the hotel tower of the Crowne Plaza rising just to the north.

In nineteen seventy five, after the Helsinki conference on security and co operation in Europe, teeto agreed that Belgrade would host the next meeting, but the city had nowhere big enough. In March nineteen seventy six the task went straight from teeto's office to stoyan maksimovich, chief designer of the city's construction office, who toured congress halls in Paris, The Hague, Copenhagen and Helsinki before producing his design.

Building took eleven months, and teeto opened the first stage on the fourteenth of May nineteen seventy seven, just in time for the conference that began that June. The great hall followed, built for the congress of the League of Communists, and it remains the largest auditorium in the former Yugoslavia, seating around four thousand people.

Here's the detail that's easy to miss, the plans called for a revolving stage, but it was never installed, and a large fixed stage went in instead.

sava tsentar was built for diplomacy and stayed on as the place where Belgrade goes to concerts, festivals and conferences, and in twenty twenty one the state recognised that by declaring it a cultural monument, before a major reconstruction that reopened it in twenty twenty four. If the foyer is open, step inside and take in how generous the approaches to the hall are, and then walk north past the Crowne Plaza, which began life as the InterContinental, built alongside the centre for a World Bank meeting in nineteen seventy nine.
