Lenin Monument, Supreme Council, tiraspol

A red granite Lenin still strides out in front of the parliament here, in a territory whose statehood almost no other country recognises.

On Twenty Fifth of October Street, the main avenue of tiraspol, stand facing the House of Soviets, and the red granite figure on a tall plinth in front of the building is the Lenin monument, with the parliament of the breakaway region of Transnistria behind it.

The statue was unveiled in nineteen eighty seven and is the work of nikolai tomskee, one of the Soviet Union's best known sculptors of official portraits, and it closely follows his much larger Lenin made for lenin plats in East Berlin, which was dismantled in nineteen ninety one and nineteen ninety two.

Have a look at the stance, with the figure leaning into a forward stride, because tomskee's Lenins were nearly always shown on the move, as a leader heading somewhere rather than a man posing for posterity.

The setting is just as telling, because this stretch of the neester's left bank broke away from Moldova after a short war in nineteen ninety two, and it is internationally recognised as part of Moldova, while its own authorities run it with their own parliament, currency and Soviet era symbols.

Here's the twist worth carrying away, the Berlin original, made for a capital of the Soviet bloc, is long gone, while its smaller sibling, made only four years before the Soviet Union ended, still stands guard in a place that kept the old symbols on purpose. Whether you read that as continuity or as a political statement probably depends on where you're from. From here, walk east along Twenty Fifth of October Street, where the Memorial of Glory and its T thirty four tank are only a few minutes away.
