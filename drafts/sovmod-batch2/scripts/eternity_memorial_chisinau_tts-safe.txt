Eternity Memorial Complex, Keesheenow

Five stone shapes the height of an eight storey building lean together over an eternal flame here, and they're meant to be rifles, one for each year of the war.

Along Pantelimon haleepa Street, the memorial rises ahead, five tall pointed blades of stone, about twenty five metres each, gathered into a pyramid, with a five pointed star at their base and a flame burning in its centre.

It was inaugurated on Victory Day in nineteen seventy five, three decades after the war ended, in memory of the Soviet soldiers who died in the yash to keesheenow offensive of August nineteen forty four. The architect was meenayev, and the reliefs were carved by the sculptors maiko and ponyatovsky. In Soviet times it was simply the Victory Memorial, and it was renamed Eternity after independence.

Walk down the avenue leading to the flame, where six large reliefs show the main stages of the war, from invasion to victory, and look for the soldiers of the Moldovan army standing guard, who usually change every hour.

The complex has gathered new meanings since nineteen ninety one, and close by stands a memorial to those who died in nineteen ninety two defending Moldova's independence and territorial integrity, so the site now honours two very different wars, fought under two different flags.

It has had its share of neglect too, restored in two thousand and six before ownership passed in twenty twenty four to the city of keesheenow, whose mayor described it as rundown and promised renovation.

This is a Soviet victory monument that a small independent country has decided to keep, guard and extend rather than remove, which is not the choice every neighbour has made. Take the path around the back of the pyramid, and then walk the length of the relief avenue again in the other direction, because the story reads differently when you follow it from victory back to invasion.
