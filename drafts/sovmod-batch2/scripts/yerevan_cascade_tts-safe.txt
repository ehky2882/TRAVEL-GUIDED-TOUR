The Cascade, Yerevan

The staircase in front of you took about ninety years to get from a sketch to the shape it has today, and the top of it still isn't finished.

At the foot of the Cascade, where tamanyan Street opens into a garden full of sculpture, you should see a vast white stairway climbing the hillside ahead of you in terraces, with fountains and planted beds at every level, and a statue of a man leaning over a drawing table right at the bottom.

That man is alexander tamanyan, the architect who drew up Yerevan's master plan in the nineteen twenties and first imagined a cascade of terraces and waterfalls linking the centre to the hills above. His idea was shelved as too ambitious, and it was only in the early seventies that jim torossyan and his colleagues redesigned it, with exhibition halls tucked inside the terraces and escalators hidden in the hill.

Building started in nineteen eighty and stopped at the end of that decade, after the earthquake, the collapse of the Soviet Union and the war over Karabakh, leaving a half built ruin for years. In two thousand and two the Armenian American collector jerard kafesjian took it on, and the museum of contemporary art that bears his name opened inside it in two thousand and nine.

Look at the garden around you, which is part of that museum, and you'll find a fat bronze cat and a reclining smoker by Fernando botairo, alongside works by Jaume Plensa and others. Then look at the stone of the stairs, a milky tuff carved with reliefs borrowed from Armenian cross stones, vines and pomegranates.

What's charming here is that a Soviet planning axis became the city's favourite place to sit, so the Cascade works less as a monument than as a living room. Climb all five hundred and seventy two steps, or take the escalators inside, and at the top you reach the obelisk to Revived Armenia, with Mount Ararat filling the view on a clear morning.
