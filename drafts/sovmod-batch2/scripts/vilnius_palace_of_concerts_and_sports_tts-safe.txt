Palace of Concerts and Sports, Vilnius

This building has hosted basketball, rock concerts, the founding of Lithuania's independence movement and a state funeral, and it stands in the middle of a cemetery.

On the north bank of the neris, in the shnipishkes district, the palace stretches along the street, a long hall faced in pale dolomite tiles under a dramatic roof that swoops up at one end like the prow of a ship or a breaking wave, now closed and fenced.

It opened on the twenty second of December nineteen seventy one with a women's basketball match, designed by the architects edwardas khlomowskas, zigmantas lyandzbergis and yonas kryookelis, and the swooping roof is the work of the engineer henrikas karvelis, hung on steel cables, which is what lets it curve so freely.

In October nineteen eighty eight the founding congress of sahyoodis, the movement that led Lithuania to independence, was held inside, and in January nineteen ninety one the palace hosted the public funeral of the thirteen people killed by Soviet troops at the Vilnius television tower.

The ground beneath it is the reason it is so hard to decide its future. This was the old Jewish cemetery of Vilnius, used from the late Middle Ages, which the Soviet authorities destroyed around nineteen fifty when they built the sports complex here, and burials remain beneath the site.

The palace closed as unsafe in two thousand and four, and proposals for a congress centre on the site have met fierce opposition. In twenty twenty six the arguments were still running, with Lithuania's president describing the building as architectural heritage, and the Jewish community asking, in effect, that the cemetery simply be left as a cemetery.

So you're standing at a place where architecture, independence and a very old grief all overlap, and there's no tidy answer to give you. Please be respectful of the ground here, and then walk along the river towards the old town, where the Jewish history of Vilnius is commemorated in many quieter places.
