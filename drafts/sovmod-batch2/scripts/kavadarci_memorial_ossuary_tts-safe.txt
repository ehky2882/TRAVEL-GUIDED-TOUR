Memorial Ossuary, Kavadartsee

Most war memorials borrow the shape of a tower or a flame, but the architect of this one chose to build a house.

In the park on the eastern edge of kavadartsee, on a rise about three hundred and five metres above sea level, you're looking at a pale concrete structure about twelve metres high and two storeys tall, with a walled enclosure wrapped around it, and behind you the town spreads across the valley.

It was opened on the seventh of September nineteen seventy six and designed by petar mooleechkovskee, a professor at the faculty of architecture in Skopje, who took as his starting point the traditional Macedonian house, with its tall upper floor and its courtyard sheltered behind high walls.

Look closely at those surrounding walls, which play the part of the courtyard in his scheme, so that as you walk through the enclosure you're moving through a stylised family yard rather than a formal parade ground.

Inside, the ossuary holds the remains of local partisans and civilian victims of the Second World War from kavadartsee and the surrounding tikvesh region, and their names, more than three hundred of them, are carved into granite slabs on the walls.

That's the quiet idea at the heart of the design, because by giving the dead a house rather than a monument, mooleechkovskee brought them home, placing them back inside the most familiar building of the place they came from. It feels less like a statement to the outside world and more like a family keeping its own. Before you go down, walk round to the edge of the platform and take in the view over the town and the vineyards of the tikvesh plain, which are a large part of why this valley is known across the country for its wine.
