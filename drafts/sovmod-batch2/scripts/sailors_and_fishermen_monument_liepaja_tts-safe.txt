Monument to Sailors and Fishermen Lost at Sea, Leeyepaya

Locals call this memorial the Crocodile, which seems an odd name for a grieving woman until you see its silhouette.

At the far end of koormahyas Avenue, where leeyepaya's seaside park meets the dunes, the monument stands on a raised sandbank in front of you, a tall V shaped pedestal faced in pale grey stone, about eleven metres high, with a bronze woman at the top, one hand raised to shade her eyes as she looks out to sea.

It was built in nineteen seventy seven, designed by goonars asaris, one of the architects of the salaspils memorial outside Riga, with the sculptor alberts terpilovskis, and it was paid for by the local ocean fishing fleet and a fishermen's collective farm. The stone on the pedestal is dolomite from the Estonian island of sahremah.

The nickname comes from the shape of the pedestal, which, seen from the side, looks a little like the open jaws of gena the Crocodile, a much loved character from Soviet children's cartoons. The gap between the two arms of the V has become a favourite spot for photographs, and people climb in and pose inside it.

Look at the plaques at the base, where one carries lines from a Latvian folk song about the winds singing softly and the loved ones lying at the bottom of the sea, and since the year two thousand a second plaque has remembered the crew of an American aircraft shot down over the Baltic, west of leeyepaya, in nineteen fifty.

That second plaque changes the monument's meaning, because a Soviet memorial to local fishermen now also honours American airmen brought down by Soviet forces, so the woman on the pedestal keeps watch over all of the sea's dead, whichever flag they sailed under.

Walk past the monument and over the dune to the beach, where the long, wide sands of leeyepaya stretch away in both directions, and then look back at her silhouette against the sky.
