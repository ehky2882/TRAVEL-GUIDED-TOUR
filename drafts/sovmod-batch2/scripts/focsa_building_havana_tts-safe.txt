Focksa Building, Havana

For almost seventy years, the tallest building in Cuba was a block of flats.

Between Calles seventeen and nineteen in vedahdo, the focksa Building fills your view, a huge concrete tower about a hundred and twenty one metres high, its plan shaped like a letter Y, with long balconied wings spreading out from the centre and a broad podium of shops at street level.

It was built between February nineteen fifty four and June nineteen fifty six, designed by the architects ernesto gomez sampera, his wife mersaydes deeas and the Spaniard marteen domeengez, with the engineer loo ees sigh enz dooplass in charge of the structure. The name comes from the construction company, Fomento de Obras y Construcciones, and the original idea was to house employees of the see em cue radio and television network.

When it was finished, it was the second largest residential concrete building in the world, after the martinelli Building in Sao Paulo, and the engineers had calculated that in hurricane winds of two hundred and forty kilometres an hour, the top would sway by only about ten centimetres.

It was planned as a city within a city, with more than three hundred and seventy flats, garages, a school, a supermarket, shops and restaurants, which owes a good deal to Le Corbusier's ideas about self contained vertical neighbourhoods.

The building then lived through every chapter of Cuba's twentieth century. In the early sixties, after the revolution, the private flats were nationalised, and in the seventies and eighties it housed Soviet and Eastern bloc specialists, with a ground floor supermarket open only to foreigners, so a symbol of fifties prosperity became a residence for socialist advisers.

It only lost its title as the country's tallest building in twenty twenty five. Walk round the base to see the Y open out from different sides, and then head two blocks north to the malekon, from where you can look back and see how completely it dominates the skyline of vedahdo.
