Hotel InterContinental, Prague, now fairmont Golden Prague

Communist Czechoslovakia's first international five star hotel was built partly to give Pan Am passengers somewhere to sleep.

From the little square of nahmnyestee kooree ovikh, beside the river at the northern end of parzheezhska Street, the hotel stretches out ahead, a big building of raw concrete and long ribbons of windows, but broken up into a cluster of separate vertical blocks of different heights, with ceramic panels set into the facade.

It was built between nineteen sixty eight and nineteen seventy four by a team led by the architect karel filsak, after an agreement between the Czechoslovak government and Pan Am, whose InterContinental chain was meant to house the airline's guests in Prague. Part of the site was a gap left by a bomb in the Second World War, and the rest was cleared by demolishing several houses.

Look at how the bulk has been chopped up. filsak deliberately split the facade into smaller vertical pieces so that a very large modern building would sit more comfortably beside the old town, and the ceramic cladding was designed by the artist zbinyek sekal.

Inside, art was part of the plan from the start, and the sculptor miloslav heynee made a huge set of wooden sculptures with a central fireplace for the restaurant, called the Enchanted Forest, part of which survives on the ground floor.

After nineteen eighty nine the hotel passed through banks and international investors, and its brutalist character was softened by later alterations. Then, between twenty twenty one and twenty twenty five, the architect marek teekhee stripped it back to its concrete frame and restored much of filsak's original design, reopening it in April twenty twenty five as the fairmont Golden Prague.

That turnaround is worth noticing, because a building once seen as an intrusion is now being restored as heritage. Walk round to the riverside to see the hotel's stepped profile against the water, and then stroll down parzheezhska towards the Old Town Square, past the Old New Synagogue, whose setting filsak's plan was careful to leave open.
