linnahall, Tallinn

This vast, low building on Tallinn's waterfront was opened as the Lenin Palace of Culture and Sport, and the city still can't agree what to do with it.

Just beyond the walls of the old town, between the harbour and the sea, linnahall lies in front of you, a long, low terraced form of stone and concrete, so low that from the street it looks more like a landscaped hill than a building, with broad stairs climbing over it towards the water.

It was completed in nineteen eighty, the year Tallinn hosted the sailing events of the Moscow Olympics, designed by the architects rayneh karp and reena altmaeh, and it held an ice rink and a big concert hall, where concerts were major events in the city, while the rink hosted hockey, fashion shows and rock gigs.

The design is what makes it remarkable, because instead of blocking the view between the old town and the sea with a big box, the architects buried most of the building into terraces, so you can walk up and over it towards the water, and the stage was even meant to revolve, although money and time ran out before it ever did.

The ice rink closed in two thousand and nine and the concert hall in twenty ten, and since then the building has been largely shut, protected as heritage but slowly decaying. Plans have come and gone, and in March twenty twenty six the mayor proposed demolishing the whole thing to make way for a new concert hall for the national symphony orchestra, an idea heritage experts are strongly resisting.

Your visit may catch it in its final years, or just before a rescue. If the stairs are open, climb onto the upper terraces and walk out to the edge facing the sea, because from there you can look back at the spires of the old town on one side and the ferries on the other, which is exactly the view linnahall was designed to protect.
