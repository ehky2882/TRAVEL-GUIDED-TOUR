National History Museum, Tirana

The most interesting thing about the mosaic above this museum is what somebody quietly took out of it in the early nineteen nineties.

skanderbeg Square opens out behind you, and the long, low building facing you on its northern edge is the National History Museum, with a vast mosaic spread across the wall above the entrance, roughly forty metres wide and eleven metres high, crowded with figures striding across it.

Start with the young woman in the middle, holding a rifle high above her head. She is Mother Albania, the anchor of the whole composition, which five artists finished in nineteen eighty, a year before the museum opened its doors. To one side the procession runs back through time to an illirian warrior and the fighters of skanderbeg's age, and on the other side come the partisans of the Second World War, men and women marching together.

Now find the worker standing beside her, on the left as you look. He originally carried a small red book in his hand, and after the communist government fell, that book was swapped for an empty sack, while a large golden star that hung above the woman's head was removed altogether.

That edit is the reason to stand here for a few minutes. The country could have chipped the whole thing off the wall, and instead it kept the story it still liked, with the warriors, the costumes and the mother with her rifle, and simply retiled the parts it had stopped believing in, so the mosaic now carries two versions of history at once.

The museum itself is closed for a long renovation, scheduled to run until twenty twenty eight, so the facade is the main event. Walk across the square towards the ethem bay Mosque and the clock tower on the far side, then turn round when you reach them, because from that distance the mosaic finally reads as one picture, and the missing star becomes surprisingly easy to imagine.
