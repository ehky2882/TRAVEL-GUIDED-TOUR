Pyramid of Tirana
Bulevardi Dëshmorët e Kombit, Tirana, Albania · 41.323022, 19.821567

Few buildings in Europe have been a dictator's museum, a conference hall, a NATO base, a television studio and a coding school, all inside forty years.

The Pyramid sits just off the main boulevard, a low, splayed concrete form whose sloping sides run down almost to the pavement in front of you, now stepped with broad stairs that climb straight up its face, with brightly coloured boxes now tucked into the structure.

It opened in October 1988 as the Enver Hoxha Museum, dedicated to the communist leader who had died three years earlier, and it was designed by a team that included his own daughter, Pranvera Hoxha, and her husband, Klement Kolaneci. When it went up, it was said to be the most expensive single building ever constructed in Albania.

Within three years the regime was gone and the museum with it. The building became a conference centre, served as a NATO base during the Kosovo war in 1999, housed a television channel from 2001, and slowly fell apart around it, its marble cladding stripped away and stored outside the city.

There were serious plans to demolish it and put a new parliament here, and a petition against demolition gathered thousands of signatures. In 2017 the decision went the other way, and the Dutch practice MVRDV reworked the shell into a technology centre for young people, which reopened in October 2023.

[beat]

What happened here is rare enough to notice, because the city didn't erase the monument or restore it as a shrine; it simply handed it to the next generation and let them climb all over it, which is arguably the most complete answer a building like this could get. Take the new steps right to the top, then look back down the boulevard towards Skanderbeg Square, where the view runs straight along the city's main ceremonial boulevard.
