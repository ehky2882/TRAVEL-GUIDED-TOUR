Villa del la por ta bot so lo

Most villa gardens march straight out from the front door. This one runs off sideways, up the hill, and that's rather the point of it.

From the parterre in front of the villa, turn to face the slope. A stone staircase climbs through four terraces, each with its balustrade and its statues, all the way up to a lawn framed by cypresses near the top of the hill. That lawn is called the theatre, and you're standing in the stalls.

The house began in the sixteenth century as a modest country place for a notary called jee rol dee no del la por ta, the founder of the family. Two hundred years later, his descendant jan an jeh lo the Third married isabella joo lee nee, in seventeen fourteen, and the couple decided the old place needed remaking. The terraces date from then. So does the fishpond up at the theatre, from seventeen twenty three.

Inside, the walls were painted between seventeen twenty four and seventeen twenty six. Have a look for the ballroom, where painted landscapes open up the walls as if the room had no walls at all, and the gallery upstairs, full of sirens, nymphs and tritons. Then find the billiard room. It holds a rare example of a game called bee ree bee see, an eighteenth century gamble that's been described as the ancestor of roulette.

And here's the thing. This was never just a pleasure house. Downstairs and round the back are the working buildings: an ice house, a spinning mill for silk, a cellar of old barrels, and a huge wooden wine press from the sixteenth century. A villa of delight, yes, but one that still had to pay its way.

In the late eighteen hundreds the house went to Senator ka mee lo bot so lo, a doctor who'd fought with gah ree bal dee, and in nineteen eighty nine his heirs gave it away, so that it could be opened to the public.

When you've done the rooms, climb all the way up to the theatre and keep going. A path leads on into the woods to a viewpoint over the val koo vee ah, with the little fifteenth century church of san bair nar dee no close by.
