# Helsinki Central Station

Four men have been holding the lights up outside this building for more than a hundred years. They're made of granite, and they've never once put them down.

On kyvo kahtoo, across the road from the station's main entrance, facing it. You're in the right place if you can see a tall arched doorway set into a wall of reddish stone, two pairs of enormous granite figures standing either side of it, each clutching a round lamp, and, off to your right, the clock tower.

Start with the figures. They're the Lantern Carriers, carved in nineteen fourteen by the sculptor ehmeel vickstrom. Have a look at where they end. From the waist down they stop being men and turn into columns, so they're holding up the building as well as the lights. These days the national railway uses them as its mascots. The Stone Men, they call them. In twenty twenty they were even fitted with face masks.

Now the tower. Forty eight and a half metres, with a clock facing each point of the compass. When the station was finished, it was the tallest tower in Helsinki.

Here's the curious thing. The building you're looking at is the architect's second answer. ellyel sahrinen won the competition in nineteen oh four with a design steeped in national romance. A tall tower, and eight bears. Two younger architects then spent nearly a month taking it apart in the newspapers. A railway station, they argued, was the most modern thing a city could build, so why make it look like something out of a legend? sahrinen toured the stations of Europe and redrew it. The bears went. The lantern men came in.

And the timing is the real story. When sahrinen drew it, Finland was a Grand Duchy of the Russian Empire. During the First World War the half finished building served as a Russian military hospital. By the time it opened, in March nineteen nineteen, Finland was an independent country. It was designed as the front door of one place, and it opened as the front door of another.

Something like two hundred thousand people come through here on an ordinary day, which makes it Finland's most visited building. If you've a few minutes, walk right, round the foot of the tower, into rowta tee en tory, the big square on the station's east side. On that side there's a separate, more formal doorway. It leads to the presidential lounge, a private waiting room for the head of state, and it's very easy to walk past without realising.
