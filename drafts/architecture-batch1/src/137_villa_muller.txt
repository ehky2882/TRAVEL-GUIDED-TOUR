From outside, it's a plain white box. Adolf Loos wrote an essay called Ornament and Crime, and this is what he meant. But step inside, and it's one of the richest interiors of the twentieth century.

Stand on the street below and look up at the house. You should see a crisp, white, cubic villa on the hillside, with a flat roof, square windows set seemingly at random, and almost no decoration at all.

Start with those windows. They don't line up in neat rows, because inside there aren't neat floors. Loos designed the house using what he called the Raumplan, a plan of spaces. Each room sits at its own height, according to how important it is, linked by short flights of steps, so the inside is a sort of three-dimensional puzzle. Loos said his architecture was conceived not in plans but in spaces.

Now imagine going in, on a tour. The rooms are finished in marble, fine wood and silk. Up a few steps, there's a raised ladies' sitting area that looks down into the living room like a box at the theatre. The richness inside is the whole point of the plainness outside.

It was finished in nineteen thirty for František Müller, a building contractor, and his wife Milada, with the help of the architect Karel Lhota, as Loos was unwell. In nineteen forty-eight the Communists seized it. After nineteen eighty-nine, the Müllers' daughter sold it to the city. It was restored and opened as a museum in two thousand, and after a recent renovation it has reopened, with six rooms shown for the first time, including the servants' quarters and the wine cellar.

[beat]

What I love is the argument the house makes. Keep the street front quiet and modest, and save your richness for the people who live inside.

Before you go, book a tour if you can. And walk round the garden side, where the terraces step down the hill.
