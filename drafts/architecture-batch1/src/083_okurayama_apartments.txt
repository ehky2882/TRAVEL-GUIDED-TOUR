Here's a block of nine flats where no two are the same, and where you have to walk through a garden, and through the walls, to reach your front door.

These are people's homes, so please stay on the street. You should see a low, three-storey building, with curving glass and curving walls, and gaps cut right through it, with plants growing in the openings.

Start with the curves. The architect is Kazuyo Sejima, one half of the Pritzker-winning practice SANAA. Inside, the walls between the flats curve, so each home gets its own shape and its own private views. But each room also keeps two straight walls, so you can still fit a bookcase and a bed. Sejima called it her most complex shape so far.

Now look at the openings at ground level. She punched holes through the curved walls to make a shared courtyard, and every resident walks through it to reach their own door. The leftover nooks became small, half-private gardens for the ground-floor flats. Those higher up get terraces on the second and third floors instead.

One resident said the curves in the glass make the boundary between the garden and the room ambiguous. That's exactly the effect. You're never quite sure where indoors stops.

It was finished in two thousand and eight, on a small plot of under five hundred square metres, with just nine flats of about fifty square metres each. The architects could have squeezed in more, and chose not to.

[beat]

Sejima said she didn't want people surrounded by a clearly fenced boundary. They should make some effort to claim their own territory. It's a gentle, slightly radical idea of how neighbours might live: not walled off, but woven together.

Before you go, walk round the corner and look at the building from another side. The cuts and curves line up differently from every angle, and you'll glimpse gardens you couldn't see from the front.
