In the eighteen nineties, some swimmers from this city went to Britain, found indoor pools that stayed open all year round, and came home determined to have one. This is what they built.

Stand on Teatralna Street and look at the building. You should see a big, handsome, yellow bathhouse from the eighteen nineties, the sort of public building that doesn't really get made any more. Then step inside to the lobby, where you'll find old design drawings on the walls.

Start with the story. A doctor, Eugen Kobierske, gave a lecture on water cures and swimming in eighteen ninety-three, and set up a committee. A company was formed, a competition held, and the architect Wilhelm Werdelmann won it. The baths opened in eighteen ninety-seven, with one pool, bathing rooms, tubs and a restaurant. It was meant to be a smart place to meet, and a cleaner city.

Now look at the interior, if you go through. There are sandstone carvings, stained glass, decorative tiles and painted vaulted ceilings, with arcades and columns that borrow from the hot baths of ancient Rome. A women's pool followed about ten years after the first, and pools for boys and girls came in the twenties and thirties.

Look too for a plaque in the lobby. In nineteen fifty-three, a swimmer called Marek Petrusewicz set a world record here.

[beat]

What's remarkable is that it simply kept going. The building came through the Second World War barely damaged, and it's still a working pool, used by schools, students and older swimmers doing gentle exercise, with saunas and a spa in the historic rooms. About half a million people come every year. It's the opposite of a museum.

If you've time, bring a towel and go for a swim. And film fans might recognise the rooms. They've appeared in several films, including The Silent Twins.
