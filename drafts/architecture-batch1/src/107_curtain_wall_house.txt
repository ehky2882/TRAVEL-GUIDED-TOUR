Mies van der Rohe gave the world the glass curtain wall. Shigeru Ban took the phrase literally and gave a house an actual curtain.

This is someone's home, so please stay on the street. Look up at the house. You should see a raised, open box of a house, two floors of living space under a sharp, cantilevered roof, and around the upper floors, instead of walls, huge white fabric curtains, two storeys high, that can be drawn open or closed.

Start with the curtains. When they're open, the living room on the second floor is completely open to the street and the sky, with terraces to the east and south. When they're drawn, the house is wrapped in a soft white veil, like a tent.

Now think about where the idea comes from. Traditional Japanese houses didn't have solid outside walls. They had sliding paper screens called shoji, hanging bamboo blinds called sudare, and sliding doors called fusuma, so you could open a house up to the garden in summer and close it in winter. Ban wanted that same openness in a modern Tokyo house. The curtain replaces all of them at once.

And in winter, when the city gets cold, a set of glass doors closes behind the curtains, so the house can be sealed for warmth and privacy.

It was finished in nineteen ninety-five, early in the career of an architect who later won the Pritzker Prize, and who is just as well known for emergency shelters made of paper tubes after disasters.

[beat]

I love the cheek of the title. Ban said Mies invented glass curtains instead of walls, and he had left only the curtains. It's a joke, but it's also a serious point about how thin the line between inside and outside can be.

Before you go, look at the houses around you, with their solid walls and small windows. Then look back at this one, and imagine living with your front wall made of cloth.
