Saudi Arabia's oil was found in the rock beneath your feet. This building was designed to look like the rock.

Stand back from the complex, far enough to take in the whole thing. You should see a cluster of smooth, rounded, silvery forms, like pebbles piled together, with one tall tower rising out of the middle to around ninety metres. In bright sun they gleam. Up close, you'll see why.

The skin isn't sheet metal. It's made of thousands of stainless steel pipes, each one bent to its own curve and laid side by side in bands, so the surfaces read as stripes from a distance and as tubing when you're close. The Norwegian architects, Snøhetta, who won the competition in two thousand and seven, took the idea from Saudi Arabia's geology: smooth, worn stones, and the layered rock that holds the oil.

That's not a loose metaphor. Ithra was built by Saudi Aramco, the national oil company, here in Dhahran, the city where oil was first found in the country, and the centre sits close to that first successful well, now known as the Prosperity Well.

Now think about what's inside those pebbles. A theatre with about nine hundred and thirty seats for opera, concerts and lectures. A cinema. A library of around two hundred thousand books. Museum galleries, an archive, and a great hall for touring exhibitions. Around a hundred thousand square metres in all, built round a central atrium full of daylight.

[beat]

So the irony is rather neat. A company that made its fortune from what lay under the ground built a place for books, music and ideas, and dressed it to look like the ground itself.

Before you go, walk right up to one of the curving walls and look along it. You'll see each pipe separately, catching the light at its own angle. Then step back again and watch them turn back into stone.
