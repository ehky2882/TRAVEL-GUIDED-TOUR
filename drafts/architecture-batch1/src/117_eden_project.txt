For more than a hundred and sixty years, this was a hole in the ground, dug out for china clay. Now it's full of rainforest.

Stand on the viewpoint at the rim of the pit and look down. You should see a deep, sheltered bowl, and nestled in the bottom, a string of giant bubbles, domes made of hexagons, like a cluster of soap bubbles that have settled against the side of the quarry.

Start with the domes. They're called biomes. Their frames are geodesic steel, a lattice of triangles, and the cells are filled not with glass but with ETFE, a tough, transparent plastic film, inflated into cushions. It's far lighter than glass, which is why the domes could be so big. The architects, Grimshaw, shaped them to sit snugly into the uneven sides of the pit.

Now look at the biggest one. The Rainforest Biome is fifty-five metres high, a hundred metres wide and two hundred metres long, one of the largest indoor rainforests in the world. Inside it's hot and steamy, with tropical plants, and even a waterfall. The smaller Mediterranean Biome next to it is full of olives, vines and citrus.

Then find the Core, the education building, with a timber roof whose spiral pattern copies the way seeds are arranged in a sunflower. Inside it is Seed, a sculpture by Peter Randall-Page, carved from a single block of granite weighing about seventy tonnes.

It was the idea of Tim Smit, who also brought back the Lost Gardens of Heligan, and it opened in March two thousand and one. In twenty nineteen, it had more than a million visitors.

[beat]

What I find most moving is the place itself. A quarry is a wound in a landscape. Eden is a way of saying wounds can heal, and even become the best thing about a place.

Before you go, walk down the zig-zag paths through the outdoor gardens rather than taking the shortcut. It's the best way to feel how deep the old pit really is.
