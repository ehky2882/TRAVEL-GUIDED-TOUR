A mattress shop is not usually where you go for architecture. This one was designed to change your mind.

Stand at the entrance and look at the shopfront. The brand's name sits on a plaque modelled on the one outside Tréca's Paris flagship, and the designers call it a seal of time. Across the front runs the idea of a train, with deep window openings set between the columns, each one framing a little scene of French life.

The train isn't random. Tréca began in Reichshoffen, in Alsace, in nineteen thirty-five, when Victor Moritz turned his family's steel wire business to making spring mattresses, and the firm later supplied springs for the seats of Pullman railway carriages. So the shopfront is a company history you can walk past.

Go inside and the spring is everywhere, abstracted into a spiral that bends the walls and steers you round. The designers, X+Living, led by Li Xiang, whose bookshops in China are famous for making people get their phones out, have divided the floor into ten separate rooms off one main corridor. Each is half-enclosed by curving walls, like a chapter in a book, and each has its own furniture, mood and materials.

Have a look at the colours as you go. Burgundy is the deepest note, with vintage green, a hazy blue and a dusty rose behind it, and then brass, mirrors, hand-painted botanical wallpapers and crystal chandeliers.

[beat]

The trick is that nobody hangs the brand's history on one wall. It unwinds along the route, so by the end you've walked through it. For a shop that sells sleep, that's an oddly wakeful thing to do.

Before you leave, walk the central corridor again slowly, end to end. Its surfaces never stop curving, and the designers compare it to a strip of film unspooling. Second time round, you'll notice how little of it is flat.
