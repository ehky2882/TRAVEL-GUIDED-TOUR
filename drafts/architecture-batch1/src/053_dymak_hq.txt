A building that bends round into a ring, with a roof that tilts one way and then the other, and walls insulated with seaweed.

Stand outside and look at the headquarters. You should see a round, low building wrapped in a grid of timber and glass, its roof line rising towards the forest on one side and dipping down on the other. Inside the ring, if you can catch a glimpse of it, there's a green courtyard stepping down like an amphitheatre.

Start with the façade. It's a regular grid of wooden fins and glass. Notice that the fins are packed more closely on the south side, where they need to block the sun, and spaced more widely on the north, where the light is gentle. It's an old Danish idea, half-timbering, done in a new way.

Now look at the roof. It's covered in about eight hundred and eighty solar panels, each one shaped to fit. The roof climbs up to the north, so the offices look out at the trees, and falls away to the south, so the panels face the sun. Bjarke Ingels, of BIG, compares the twisting form to a Möbius strip, the loop with only one side.

Then think about what it's made of. Mass timber, clay, cork, ceramic tiles, ceilings of recycled paper fibre, and insulation made from eelgrass, a seaweed that's been used for roofs in Denmark for centuries. Dymak supplies materials, and this building is a kind of living catalogue of what they sell.

[beat]

There's something rather Danish about that. Not a glass tower announcing the company. A low ring of wood and grass round a garden, quietly showing you the stock.

Before you go, walk round towards Glisholm Lake behind the building. From that side, you can see the roof rising out of the trees, and how carefully the building sits between the factories on one side and the woods on the other.
