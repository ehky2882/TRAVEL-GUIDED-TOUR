Behind these walls, students are practising music in rows of tiny soundproofed rooms. And how many of those rooms there are decides what the lessons cost.

Stand on the street and look up at the building. You should see a compact block of concrete and glass in the lanes of Donggyo-dong, close to the Hongdae university area, a neighbourhood full of music, clubs and practice studios.

Start with the brief, because it's the whole story. This is a music preparatory academy, the kind of school where young musicians drill for auditions. That means one thing above all: a great many small, soundproof practice rooms, packed as tightly as possible, because every extra room means more students, and more students keep the fees down.

Most architects would fight that. They'd want bigger, nicer rooms, more light, more air. The architects here, FHHH friends, a Seoul practice led by Han Seung Jae, Han Yang Kyu and Yoon Han Jin, did the opposite. They say the project begins by accepting that reality, rather than denying it.

So look at the building as a kind of three-dimensional puzzle. The trick is to take a stack of identical little boxes, each needing to be sealed against sound, and make them into something that's still a pleasure to walk through and to look at from the street. That's what the name points to: a building thought through in three dimensions, not just floor by floor.

It was completed in the mid twenty-twenties, with about one thousand two hundred and seventy square metres inside.

[beat]

I rather admire that honesty. Architecture often pretends money doesn't matter. Here the economics are the design brief, and the architects treated them as a puzzle worth solving well.

If you've time, walk on into the streets of Hongdae. You'll hear music coming out of basements and windows on every block, which is exactly why a building like this belongs here.
