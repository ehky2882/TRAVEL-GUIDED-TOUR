It's a perfectly sensible glass apartment block, until someone appears to have taken a canyon out of the middle of it.

Stand across Blake Street and look up at the sixteen-storey tower. Most of it is a crisp glass box. But somewhere around the middle, the façade breaks open into a rough, rocky gash, several floors deep, full of plants, with terraces and water, as though the building has been worn away by a river.

That's the Canyon, and it's the whole idea. MAD, the Beijing practice led by Ma Yansong, carved a four-storey space out of the building, inspired by slot canyons, the narrow ravines that water cuts through rock in the American West. It runs from the sixth floor to the ninth, and residents can wander up through it on a kind of trail.

Look for the planting. It's based on the plant life of the Colorado foothills and canyons, chosen to cope with the climate. Altogether there are more than thirteen thousand square feet of terraces, and from up there they look as if they're hanging in the air.

Then listen. There's running water in the Canyon, and on a quiet day you can hear it cascading from the pavement below.

The Canyon doesn't stop at the ninth floor. It climbs across the façade to a rooftop terrace with a pool and a garden, sixteen storeys up, looking out at the Rocky Mountains.

[beat]

There are a hundred and eighty-seven flats here, and a shop on the ground floor. It's a rental building, not a museum. But it brings a bit of the landscape that Denver sits in back into the city, and it lets you look at the mountains from inside a mountain.

Before you go, walk a block or two along Blake Street and look back. The cut reads differently from every angle. And the RiNo district around you is full of murals, so the walk is worth it on its own.
