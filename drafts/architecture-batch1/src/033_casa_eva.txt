A concrete frame, open to the air, with a straw hat on top. That's more or less the whole idea, and it's a good one.

These are private homes, so this is one to enjoy from the street. Look for a three-storey building on a slope running down towards the sea: a grid of bare grey concrete columns and floors, almost no walls, some timber screens, and on the very top, a big thatched roof.

Start with the frame. It's a simple grid of columns, beams and slabs, cast on site, twelve metres by fifteen, with columns every three metres. Most floors are two metres eighty-five high. The ground floor gets a bit more, just over four. There's hardly any enclosure at all. Railings mark the edges, and the frame itself is the outline of the building.

Now find the wooden boxes. Each bedroom sits inside a lattice of timber, see-through enough to let the breeze and the light in, solid enough to give some privacy. In a hot coastal town, that matters more than glass.

And then the roof. It's a palapa, the traditional thatched roof of the Mexican coast, and it's the one thing here that isn't concrete, steel or structural timber. The studio, S-AR, from Monterrey, led on this project by Cesar Guerrero and Ana Cecilia Garza, put the oldest local way of keeping the sun off right on top of the most modern part of the job.

There are three flats, each about a hundred and eighty square metres, each with its own small plunge pool. Because the site slopes, they all see something different. The lower one sits among the tree tops. The upper one looks out to the mountains and the sea.

[beat]

What I like is how little it tries. It's a shelter, a shade, and a view, which is really all you need on this coast.

Before you go, look along the street for other palapas. Once you've spotted one, you'll see them everywhere in Puerto Escondido, on beach bars, on houses, on hotels.
