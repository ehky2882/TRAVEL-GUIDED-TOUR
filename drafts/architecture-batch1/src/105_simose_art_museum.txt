Most museums rearrange the art. This one rearranges the building. Its galleries float, and they can be moved about like boats.

Stand at the edge of the water basin and look out. You should see a broad, shallow sheet of water, and sitting on it, a group of square, glass-clad boxes in different soft colours, with the Seto Inland Sea and its islands beyond.

Start with the boxes. There are eight of them, each one a ten-metre cube, clad in coloured glass. The colours were inspired by the islands of the Inland Sea. The architect, Shigeru Ban, calls them movable galleries, and the museum says it's the first in the world to have them.

Now here's how it works. The boxes were built on floating bases, using techniques from Hiroshima's shipyards. When the museum wants a new layout, it raises the water in the basin, the galleries float, and they're pushed into a new arrangement, side by side or linked with bridges, without a crane. There are seven planned formations to choose from, depending on the exhibition.

Inside is a collection of about five hundred works, gathered over half a century by Yumiko Shimose, the head of a local company: Japanese dolls for the girls' festival, art nouveau glass, especially by Émile Gallé, and paintings by Matisse, Chagall and Kaii Higashiyama. The garden is planted with flowers that Gallé loved.

It opened in twenty twenty-three, along with ten villas by Ban and a French restaurant, and won a Prix Versailles, a prize for the world's most beautiful museums.

[beat]

What I love is the idea that a building doesn't have to be finished. These galleries can drift into a new shape for every show, like islands rearranging themselves in a calm sea.

Before you go, go up to the roof. From there you can look down on the floating galleries, and then out across the real islands of the Seto Inland Sea that inspired them.
