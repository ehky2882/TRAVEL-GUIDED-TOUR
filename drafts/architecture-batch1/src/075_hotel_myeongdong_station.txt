Most buildings start with the street and work inwards. This one started with a bed, and worked outwards.

Look up at the hotel's front. You should see a grid of soft, rounded white frames, stacked one on top of another, each with a window set into it, like a wall of bubbles or a stack of capsules, quite unlike the flat shopfronts and signs around it.

Start with those frames. Each one is a curved panel of ABS plastic. There are just two shapes, turned and repeated to make the rhythm you see. The architect, Yong Ju Lee, had each panel made in a mould cut by computer, vacuum-formed, treated against fire, and fixed to an aluminium frame.

Now the idea behind it. The architects say the form came from the inside, not from the city around it. Each room is a compact module that combines a bed, storage, a table and washing, for sleeping, resting and working, and there are twenty-nine of them, along a central corridor. The front is simply what you get when you stack those rooms up. It isn't decoration at all.

Look closely at the windows, if you can. Inside, the panel at the head of each bed is patterned with traditional motifs, and it controls the light and the view.

There's a modern twist in the design process too. The architects experimented with artificial intelligence to try out shapes, but only as a sketching tool. The real design came from practical questions: where the light falls, which way you sleep, where you put your bag. It was finished in twenty twenty-five.

[beat]

I like how honest that is. In one of the most crowded shopping streets in Seoul, it doesn't shout with signs. It just shows you its rooms.

Before you go, plunge into the lanes of Myeongdong around you. Come back after dark, when the street food stalls are out, and look up at the hotel, its rows of rounded windows glowing above the crowds.
