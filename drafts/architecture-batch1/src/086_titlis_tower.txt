For forty years this was just an antenna on a glacier. Now it's a restaurant, a viewing deck and, by some accounts, the highest Rolex shop in the world.

Stand on the snow outside the summit station and look up. You should see a tall, open steel mast, the old telecoms tower, and wrapped around it, two long, glazed boxes stacked across it, with smaller towers of stairs and lifts rising alongside.

Start with the mast. It's fifty-six metres of steel, built in the mid nineteen eighties to carry telecommunications equipment, at more than three thousand metres above sea level. The architects, Herzog and de Meuron, kept it. They didn't hide it either. You can still see the original structure running right through the new building.

Now look at the glass boxes. They hold a restaurant and shops, with views straight out over the glacier and the peaks. Inside, there's steel and concrete, and plenty of wood, to make a room at three thousand metres feel warm.

Then look right at the top. There's a public viewing platform up there, with a view right round three hundred and sixty degrees, over the glacier and across the Alps. You reach the building from the mountain station through an old tunnel, kept and built into the new design.

It opened on the twenty-ninth of May, twenty twenty-six, as part of a long-term plan to rebuild the whole summit, by the same Basel practice that turned a London power station into Tate Modern.

[beat]

I like that they kept the antenna. They could easily have pulled down a dull piece of engineering and built something showy in its place. Instead they've let a working structure become a place for people, which is a very Swiss sort of thrift.

If you've got a head for heights, go out onto the top platform. And when you come down, cross the Titlis Cliff Walk nearby, said to be Europe's highest suspension bridge, for the view back up at the tower.
