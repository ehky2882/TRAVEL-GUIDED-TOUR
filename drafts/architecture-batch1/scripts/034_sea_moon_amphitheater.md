# Sea-Moon Amphitheater, Hangzhou Bay

**Place:** Port Lake Park, Hangzhou Bay New Town, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China  
**Coordinate (WGS-84):** 30.35804, 121.20985 (from the owner)  
**Length:** 370 words (about 148 seconds)

## Script (clean)

Here's a theatre where the stage is a wave, the seats are smaller waves, and the actors come on through the moon.

You'll find it on the shore of the lake in Port Lake Park. Look for a low mass of pale stone and concrete that swells up out of the lawn in curves, like water frozen mid-roll. At the back it rises into one great wave, tall enough to block the view, and through the middle of that wave, a round opening.

Start with the waves. There's one big one and three small ones, all rolling the same way, east to west, the same direction as the tides in Hangzhou Bay. The big wave is the stage. The small ones in front are the audience, their crests shaped into seats. And two timber platforms sticking out to the sides, shaped like boats, are the best seats in the house.

Now the round opening. It's a moon gate, the circular doorway you find in old Chinese gardens. Here it's the stage door. The architects imagined performers stepping out of the bright moon over the sea, while the audience lie back along the wave tops to watch.

Behind it there's an island of pine trees and a pond, and steps that cut through the waves. Notice too how low it all starts. The middle sits only a step or two above the grass, so you can simply wander in.

It's by Gardening Architects, led by Wang Xin and Sun Yu, made for a public art exhibition, on the half-finished foundations of an abandoned pier. Wang was the only architect among twenty invited artists. The idea goes back to a curved roof on the China Academy of Art's Xiangshan campus, which felt, he said, like stepping onto towering waves.

[beat]

It's finished, but it was never meant to feel still. The point is that the audience and the players sit in the same tide.

Before you go, climb up to the moon gate and look back through it. And if you can, come back on a clear night with a moon on the water. That's when it makes the most sense.
