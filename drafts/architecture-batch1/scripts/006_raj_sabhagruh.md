# Raj Sabhagruh, Dharampur

**Place:** Shrimad Rajchandra Ashram, Dharampur, Valsad district, Gujarat, India  
**Coordinate (WGS-84):** 20.53607, 73.17409 (area only: Dharampur town; the ashram is on a hill outside it. Get a map pin before use)  
**Length:** 343 words (about 137 seconds)

## Script (clean)

Eight hundred thousand pieces of marble, every one of them a leftover, and every one of them carved by hand.

Find a place on the approach where you can take in the whole building. It's tall, about forty metres, and it seems to twist as it rises: stacked halls that turn forty-five degrees against each other, so the corners never quite line up. Then walk closer and look at the surface. That fine, pale, perforated skin is made of those marble offcuts.

This is the centrepiece of the Shrimad Rajchandra Ashram, on the Mohangadh hillock just outside Dharampur, dedicated to the teachings of Shrimad Rajchandra, a Jain philosopher who died in nineteen oh one, aged thirty-three, and whom Gandhi counted among his teachers. Rajchandra spent thirty-five days on this very land in nineteen hundred. The building was designed by the London practice Serie Architects and finished in twenty twenty-four.

Its shape comes from Jain tradition. The model is the samavasarana, a mythical temple stepped in rising tiers, where the teaching is given from the top and everyone gathers below. Here, the turning layers are meant to read as an ascent, from learning at the bottom to introspection at the top.

Inside, at the heart of it, is an auditorium for five thousand people, wrapped in four curving walls and reached through a lobby with eight entrances, one for each point of the compass and each point in between. Above it sits a meditation hall twenty metres high. There's a museum, a library and classrooms too.

[beat]

The ashram describes the building as a lantern, a symbol of the light of knowledge. What I find moving is the material. A building about non-violence and restraint, dressed in stone that someone else threw away. It practises what it's built to teach.

If the ashram is open to visitors when you're here, look for the entrance that faces you, and count the others round the lobby. Eight doors, and no back door. Everyone comes in the same way.
