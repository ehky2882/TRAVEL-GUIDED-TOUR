LEGO House, Billund

It's a building made of LEGO bricks. Not literally, but almost: twenty-one giant white blocks stacked on top of each other, crowned with a brick that has eight studs on top.

Stand in the square and look up. You should see a stack of big white boxes piled at different angles, their roofs painted in bright red, blue, green and yellow, and right at the very top, a single white block with eight round bumps, just like a two-by-four LEGO brick.

Start with that top block. It's called the Keystone, and it's the gallery that crowns the whole stack. The building's proportions are all based on the classic two-by-four brick. The architect, the Danish practice BIG, led by byarkeh ingels, made the whole thing a giant model of the toy.

Now look at the roofs. Those coloured terraces are public, and free. You can climb up and walk across them, and there are nine playgrounds up there, among the colours. Down here, the square around you is free too, a public space for the town.

Then go inside, if you have a ticket. The building is split into four coloured zones, each meant to exercise a different kind of play: blue for problem-solving, green for social play, red for creativity, yellow for emotion. In the middle stands the Tree of Creativity, a tree more than fifteen metres tall, made of about six point three million LEGO bricks.

It opened on the twenty-eighth of September, twenty seventeen, here in billoon, the small Danish town where LEGO was invented, right next to the company's headquarters.

What I love is that the building does what the toy does. It's simple pieces, stacked in an unexpected way, and it invites you to climb all over it. That's a very generous sort of architecture.

Before you go, climb right up to the highest terrace and look down. You'll see the coloured roofs laid out below you, like a box of bricks just tipped out on the floor.
