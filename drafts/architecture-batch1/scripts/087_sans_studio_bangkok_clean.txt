Sans Studio, Bangkok

A photography studio has one job: control the light. So it's a nice irony that this one is designed around letting light in.

Stand on the street and look at the building. You should see a compact, three-storey block, with smooth curves carved out of its mass, round windows punched into its walls, and a terrace up on the roof.

Start with the curves. The architects, the Bangkok practice PHTAA Living Design, took their cue from Bauhaus-era art and design, with its love of simple geometry, and the play between solid and empty space. They carved curved hollows out of the block so daylight falls into the building, and the shapes of light inside change with the hour and the season.

Now find the round windows. They're part of the same game: pure circles cut into plain walls, the most basic geometric shape there is.

Inside, if you ever get the chance, each floor does something different. On the ground floor is a reception hall, with dark wood panels hiding a bright blue kitchen. In the first studio, a green industrial staircase doubles as a piece of sculpture and a backdrop. The second studio, upstairs, is fully enclosed, polished concrete and white, with walls of coloured lighting panels that can turn the whole room any colour or project graphics, and a hidden skylight. And on the roof is an open terrace with a stainless steel bar, for shoots and parties.

It's about four hundred square metres, designed in twenty twenty, and it's rented out for photo shoots, product launches, seminars and even weddings.

[beat]

I like the idea of a building made for making images that's also worth taking a picture of. It's a studio that's its own best advertisement.

If you're passing in the late afternoon, wait for the sun to drop. That's when the curves and the round windows throw their sharpest shadows, and you can see exactly what the architects were playing with.
