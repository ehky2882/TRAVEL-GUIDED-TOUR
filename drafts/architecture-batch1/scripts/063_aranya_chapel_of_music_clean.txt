Chapel of Music, Aranya

Imagine a concert hall that seats forty-eight people, can open its roof to the sky, and pipes the music down through brass tubes to a dark room below.

Stand in the middle of the community plaza. You should see a compact, three-sided building of bare grey concrete, its walls curving gently inwards, like a hollowed-out stone, with very few openings.

Start with those curves. Each of the three walls bends inward to face one of the three groups of buildings around the plaza, so the chapel answers its neighbours on every side. The architects, Vector Architects, led by Gong Dong, said they imagined it as a fine musical instrument that had landed on the square.

Now think about what's inside. There are two rooms, one stacked on the other. Upstairs is the music hall, where forty-eight listeners sit on two tiers around a sunken stage. Over their heads is a big round skylight, a retractable roof that can be opened to let the air and the sky in during a performance.

Downstairs is the Meditation Rotunda, nine metres tall, with polished concrete seats. It's lit only by thin slots of light at the corners, where the walls don't quite meet. And this is the clever bit: nine brass tubes carry the sound of the hall down into the rotunda, so you can sit in the dim below and listen to the music from above.

It was finished in September twenty twenty-three, the fourth building Vector Architects have made here at Aranya. As you leave, a large east-facing window gives you a view out to the horizon.

[beat]

I find it rather lovely, a building that lets you choose how to hear a concert. In the light, upstairs with the players. Or down in the dark, with the music coming through the walls.

If you've time, walk from here down to the beach. The architects' two earlier buildings, the Seashore Library and the Seashore Chapel, are both on the sand, and the three together make a fine short walk.
