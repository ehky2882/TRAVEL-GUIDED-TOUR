Akwasun Leedo, Pahcheh vill

In nineteen eighty-three, Malta's leading architect built a swimming pool that looks like a stage set for a dream.

Stand by the pool and look around. You should see the water surrounded by freestanding walls and odd little structures, arches and openings that frame nothing in particular, walls that stand on their own, all in pale, sunlit surfaces with deep shadows.

Start with those walls. They don't hold anything up. They're follies, pieces of architecture made purely to be looked at and walked through, and they create what one writer called a mirage-like effect. England himself has described the lido as a pool like a theatre stage. In the nineteen eighties, his work was full of these surreal compositions: volumes, flat planes and arched, square and rectangular openings, in Maltese stone and concrete, often in soft pastel colours.

Now walk through them. Look at how the openings frame the sky and the water, and how the shadows move round the pool through the day. It's architecture as a game of light and shade, played out in the Maltese sun.

The architect, richard england, born in nineteen thirty-seven, trained in Malta and Milan, and worked for jo ponti before coming home. He's best known for churches and quiet, spiritual spaces. This is the playful side of him, and it's part of what is now the St. George's Park Hotel in pahcheh vill, the busy nightlife quarter named after Dr joozeppeh pahcheh, who built some of the first seaside houses here.

What I love is that it treats a hotel pool, the most ordinary of holiday places, as a work of art. You come for a swim, and you find yourself walking through a painting.

Before you go, stand at the far end of the pool and look back through the walls and arches. Then wander down to St George's Bay, and see how pahcheh vill has grown up around this little dream of the eighties.
