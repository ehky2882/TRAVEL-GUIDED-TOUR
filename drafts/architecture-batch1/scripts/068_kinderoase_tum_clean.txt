Kinderoase at TUM, Munich

The first permanent building in Europe by one of the world's most celebrated architects is a kindergarten, for sixty children. And he says he designed it from their point of view.

Stand on Gabelsbergerstraße and look at the building. You should see a narrow, five-storey block, its front covered in rows of folded, rust-brown steel louvres, angled this way and that, like a giant set of half-open blinds.

Start with the louvres. There are two thousand and eighty-five of them, in weathering steel, which rusts to that warm brown and then stops. They shade the rooms, filter the light, and soften the noise of the street. The architect is Francis Kéré, from Burkina Faso, who won the Pritzker Prize in twenty twenty-two and teaches here at the Technical University of Munich.

Now look between the louvres, up the front. Behind them runs a vertical playground, with slides linking the floors, so a child can go down a level the fun way. Each age group has its own floor, with shared play, sport and dining spaces higher up. On top, partly covered, is a roof terrace they call the Himmelswiese, the sky meadow.

The building is almost all timber, mainly spruce, with ash for the floors and the stairs. Only the emergency stairwell at the back is concrete. This plot had sat empty since the Second World War. It was paid for and given to the university by a businesswoman, Ingeborg Pohl, to help university staff, especially women, combine careers and families, and it was handed over in July twenty twenty-six.

[beat]

I find it rather wonderful that Kéré's first permanent European building is for small children rather than a museum or a bank. He said he designed it entirely from the perspective of the children. Look at those slides and you can believe it.

Before you go, walk a few minutes into the museum quarter. The Pinakothek galleries are just round the corner, so you can go from the newest building on the street to some of the oldest paintings in Munich.
