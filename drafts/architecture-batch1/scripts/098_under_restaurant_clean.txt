Under, Lindesnes

At the very southern tip of Norway, a concrete tube runs off the rocks and down into the sea, like a periscope pointed the wrong way. At the bottom, there's a restaurant.

Stand on the rocks by the shore and look down. You should see a long, rough concrete box, a bit like a sunken container, sloping off the shoreline into the water and disappearing beneath the waves, its end resting on the sea bed.

Start with the concrete. It's thick and deliberately rough, built to take the pounding of the sea. And the rough surface has a job. Mussels and seaweed are meant to attach themselves, so over time the building turns into an artificial reef.

Now imagine the inside, because this is the point. You walk down through the building to a dining room five and a half metres below sea level. One whole end is a window, eleven metres wide and over three metres high, looking straight out into the sea, at the fish, the kelp and the changing light.

It's by Snøhetta, the same Norwegian architects who made the Oslo Opera House, and it opened in March twenty nineteen, as Europe's first underwater restaurant. It seats about forty, for a long tasting menu of fish and produce from the coast of southern Norway, and it won a Michelin star in its first year. The building doubles as a marine research station too.

[beat]

What I love is how honest it is about the sea. You don't get a perfect turquoise tank. You get what's out there that evening: murky or clear, crowded or empty. The restaurant says the sea decides the menu and the view.

Before you go, walk along the rocks to look back at the building from the side. You'll see just how much of it has slipped beneath the water. And if you can, come back at dusk, when the lights of the dining room glow under the waves.
