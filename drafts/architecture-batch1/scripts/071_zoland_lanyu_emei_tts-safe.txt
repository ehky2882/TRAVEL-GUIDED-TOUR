Zoland Lahn yoo, Mount Uh may

The hotel's Chinese name, lahn yoo, borrows a character that means the mist that rises through mountain trees. On Mount uh may, you'll see plenty of that.

Look for a cluster of low buildings stepped up the wooded hillside, with steep pitched roofs and deep eaves, grey tiles, and walls of rough stone and concrete, arranged less like a hotel than a small mountain village.

Start with the entrance. The front wall is concrete, but it's been chiselled by hand to give it a rough, stony texture, so it sits with the rocks of the mountain rather than against them. The architects, Studio J. Bridgland, led by justin bridgland and jaycee chway, rebuilt a group of run-down old buildings here, and they describe their approach as architectural quietude.

Now find the courtyard at the heart of it. In the middle stands an ancient jun nahn tree, a precious nanmu, said to be the oldest and tallest in the valley, in a shallow reflecting pool. The whole resort is organised round it.

Then look at the materials, which are almost all local. The dining hall has a roof of grey tiles from nearby luh shahn, a cedar ceiling, and walls of schist from Mount uh may itself. The guest rooms sit on the highest ridge, in washed stone, partly hidden under a green roof, with deep red local stone in the bathrooms. There's an outdoor bathing pool built into the slope.

It opened in twenty twenty-five, with twenty-nine rooms, steps from the ching yin Pavilion, one of the loveliest spots on the mountain.

Mount uh may is one of China's four sacred Buddhist mountains, and it's been full of monasteries and guest houses for pilgrims for over a thousand years. In a way, this is simply the latest of them: a quiet place to stop on the way up.

If you've got the time, walk down to ching yin Pavilion itself, where two mountain streams meet under a pair of old bridges. The sound of the water is exactly what this hotel was built to listen to.
