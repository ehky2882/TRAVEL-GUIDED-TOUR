# Chenhu Migratory Bird Observation Tower, Wuhan

**Place:** Xianglushan, Chenhu Wetland, Tonghu Town, Caidian District, Wuhan, Hubei, China  
**Coordinate (WGS-84):** 30.31194, 113.84102 (area only: Chenhu lake point. Move onto the Xianglushan promontory before use)  
**Length:** 331 words (about 132 seconds)

## Script (clean)

Every winter, more than a hundred thousand migratory birds come down on this marsh. This tower is built for watching them, and the climb is half the point.

Stand at the foot of the tower on Xianglushan, the small rise that pushes out into the wetland. You should be looking up at a slim, silver tower about twenty metres tall, with no solid core at all. Instead you can see straight up through the middle, past layer after layer of stairs.

That's because it's two staircases, twisted round each other like a strand of DNA. One takes you up, the other brings you down, so people never have to squeeze past each other. And the stairs aren't hung from a structure; they are the structure. Steel braces running the opposite way to the stairs stop the whole thing from twisting, which leaves a clear void about three metres wide right through the centre.

Now count the landings as you climb. There are twenty-four of them, short platforms staggered up the tower, and each one turns you to face a different part of the wetland, so the view changes all the way up.

The tower was assembled from twenty-four standard pieces, made in a factory and bolted together here, so there was as little building work as possible in the marsh. Its matte silver skin changes with the weather: paler under grey skies, cooler in bright sun, warm at sunset.

[beat]

The architects, Xiang Architects, said they didn't want the tower to be just a route to a viewpoint. They wanted the climbing itself to be part of the landscape. I think they got it. You arrive at the top having already seen the place from twenty-four angles.

The birds come between October and March. If you're here then, stop on a landing halfway up, keep very still, and let your eyes adjust to the reeds. That's usually when you start to see them.
