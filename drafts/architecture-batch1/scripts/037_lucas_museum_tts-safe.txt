Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, Los Angeles

It cost about a billion dollars, it's paid for by the man who made Star Wars, and it's very firmly not a Star Wars museum.

Walk into the plaza underneath the building. You should be standing in shade, with a huge, smooth, pale shape curving overhead, more like a spaceship that's come to rest in a park than a museum. Look straight up and you'll see a great oval opening, an oculus, four storeys above you, framing the sky.

Start with that shell. It's clad in more than twelve hundred curved panels of fibreglass-reinforced polymer, and every one of them is a different shape. The architect, mah yen song of the Beijing practice MAD, talks about emotional architecture: spaces that flow, with no hard edges. The whole thing is lifted up on a central arch that spans a hundred and eighty-five feet, so the ground floor stays open, a public room under the building for anyone walking by.

Then look around you at the park. Until recently this was an asphalt car park. Now it's thirteen acres of gardens by the landscape architect mia lairer, with more than three hundred new trees, a meadow, a hanging garden and a fountain.

Inside, it's about storytelling in pictures, of every kind. Norman Rockwell, Van Gogh, Diego Rivera, comic art by Frank frazetta, a library with more than thirty thousand comic books, and George Lucas's own film archive, from nineteen seventy-one to twenty twelve. There are two cinemas, named not after directors but after a documentary maker and a master of montage.

I rather like the argument the museum is making. That the cover of a magazine, a mural and a film still all belong in the same building, because they're all doing the same job: telling you a story.

If you've got time, have lunch in the restaurant, called, inevitably, the Skywalker Grill. It looks out over Los Angeles. And it's closed on Tuesdays, so plan around that.
