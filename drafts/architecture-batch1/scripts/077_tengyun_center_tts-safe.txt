Most corporate headquarters keep you out. This one lifts itself up off the ground and hands the land underneath to the public.

Stand underneath the building. You should be in a wide, shaded open space by the bay, with paths, lawns and planted slopes around you, and overhead, three huge, smooth, white, cloud-like masses floating about eight and a half metres up, joined together by bridges.

Start with the lift. The whole office block is raised on ten structural cores, and that frees up about two storeys' worth of space beneath it as a public park by the sea. You don't have to go into Tencent to be here. Anyone can walk in, sit in the shade and look at the water.

Now look up at the clouds. There are three of them, in a line running north to south. The southern one holds exhibitions and events. The middle and northern ones are offices, arranged round open atriums. They're linked by steel-truss bridges, which double as places to stop and look out over the bay.

Then look for the northern building's roof. It has a big shell-shaped skylight made of e t f e, a kind of very light plastic film, that lets in daylight while cutting down the harsh coastal sun.

It's by MAD, the Beijing practice led by mah yen song, with dahng chwin and yoskeh hayahno, and it opened in twenty twenty-six, for around fourteen thousand Tencent staff. Ma says floating, for him, is a spatial idea, not just a visual trick.

What I think is worth noticing is the deal it strikes. One of the biggest technology companies in the world gets its headquarters, and the city gets its coastline back, in the shade. That's a rare bargain.

Before you go, walk out to the water's edge and look back. From the bay, you can see the whole set of clouds at once, hovering above the people walking beneath them.
