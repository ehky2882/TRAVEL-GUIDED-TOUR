Seashore Chapel, Aranya

The architects imagined this as an old boat, left behind on the beach when the sea went out. When the tide comes in, the sea comes back underneath it.

Stand on the beach in front of the chapel. You should see a tall, white, angular building with a pitched roof, raised up off the sand on concrete supports, with grand stairs leading up to it, and, between the stairs, a narrow gap.

Start with that gap. It's sixty centimetres wide, and it runs up the middle of the staircases, so as you approach along the thirty-metre path, you catch a slim glimpse of the space hanging above. Then you climb, go through a gate, turn round a screen wall, and the whole ocean opens up in front of you.

Now look under the building. The lower, covered space is a shady place to rest on the beach. But when the tide rises, it floods, and then the chapel really does look like a boat afloat.

Inside, if you can go in, it's a single tall room, about ten metres high, finished in textured white plaster and timber. There's only one big window, facing east, out to sea. A triangular opening lights the cross. And a thin gap between the north wall and the roof lets a blade of sunlight in at midday in spring, summer and autumn, raking across the plaster. On the north side there's a tiny meditation nook, just big enough for one person, its curved wall turning your eyes to the ocean.

It's by Vector Architects, led by Gong Dong, built between twenty fourteen and twenty fifteen.

What I find moving is how little it needs to do. A white room, a window, the sea. The tide does the rest.

If you can, come back at high tide, when the water runs right under the chapel. And walk on along the beach to the Seashore Library, by the same architects.
