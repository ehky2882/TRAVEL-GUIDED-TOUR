Oh vo Patagonia, El Chalten

Somewhere up on that rock face, people are going to sleep tonight in a glass egg, bolted to a cliff, with nothing below them but Patagonian air.

Look up at the cliffs above the valley of the reeo deh las vweltas. Somewhere up there, high on the rock, you may be able to pick out a few small, pale capsules, stuck to the face like eggs laid by some enormous bird. That's what oh vo means in Spanish: egg.

Start with the height. The capsules hang roughly two hundred and seventy metres above the valley floor. Each one sleeps two, and each has three levels: a bed at the top, a tiny sitting room in the middle with a dry toilet, and at the bottom, a net, where you can lie out over the drop. Every level has a view all the way round.

Now think about how they got there. Each capsule weighs a couple of tonnes, and they were hauled up the mountain on an aerial lift. The idea came from a highliner, esekyel rooehteh, and an architect, looees aparisseeo, and it took about three years to engineer.

And the way up for guests is no lift. You walk through native lenga forest for half a day, then clip on a harness and helmet and climb a veea ferrahta, the iron path of cables and rungs, with a mountain guide alongside you. The power up there comes from the sun. And if the wind gusts over a hundred kilometres an hour, which happens here, the night is called off.

What I find rather wonderful is the honesty of it. Most hotels try to keep the weather out. This one hands you the weather, the stars and a dawn view of fits roy, and asks only that you climb for it.

If you'd rather keep your feet on the ground, head back towards el chalten and take the trail up to Laguna de los Tres. You'll get the same mountain, at sunrise, from a rock you didn't have to sleep on.
