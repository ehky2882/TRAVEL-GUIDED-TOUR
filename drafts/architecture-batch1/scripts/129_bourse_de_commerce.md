## Script (clean)

It's been a queen's palace, a grain market, and a stock exchange. Now, inside its old dome, a Japanese architect has dropped a ring of bare concrete.

Stand outside by the tall stone column next to the building. You should see a great round building, crowned by a big glass-and-iron dome, and right beside it, a lonely stone column, taller than the roof.

Start with the column. It's the oldest thing here, the Medici column, left from a palace built for Catherine de' Medici in the sixteenth century. It's said she used it for watching the stars, and her astrologers to read them.

Now the building. It was built in the seventeen-sixties as the Halle aux blés, the city's grain market, built in a ring around a central courtyard. The courtyard was covered by a wooden dome, which burned in eighteen oh two and was replaced with iron. In the eighteen eighties, the building was remodelled as a commodities exchange, and traders worked here until nineteen ninety-eight.

Then go inside. Under the dome, the Japanese architect Tadao Ando has placed a perfect circle of smooth concrete, about nine metres high, inside the old round hall: a building within a building. Walk round the top of it and look up at the painted murals running round the base of the dome, protected as a historic monument since nineteen seventy-five. It opened in May twenty twenty-one as a museum for François Pinault's collection of contemporary art.

[beat]

What I love is that Ando didn't fight the old building. He just drew a circle inside a circle, and let four hundred years of Paris history sit around it, in the light from the dome.

Before you go, walk out the back into the gardens of Les Halles. Turn round and you'll see the dome rising above the trees, the column at its side, the way Parisians have seen it for two and a half centuries.
