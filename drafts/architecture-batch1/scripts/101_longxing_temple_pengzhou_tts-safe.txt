Long shing Temple Quarter, Pung joe

Most old temples in China's cities end up isolated, a precious relic on an island of traffic. Here, the planners tried the opposite: to weave the temple back into everyday life.

Stand in the open forecourt on the south side of long shing Temple. You should see the temple buildings in front of you, and around them a new quarter of low, three-storey buildings with sloping roofs, water channels and walkways running between them, and bigger buildings with upturned eaves rising behind.

Start with the layout. The temple sits at the centre, and everything else is arranged around it. To the north, a new museum rises just above the temple roofs. To the south is this forecourt. To the east, an old factory site has become a park. And to the west, a stream of water ties the whole area together.

Now look at the roofs. The designers, b i a d's ASA Studio, took the sloping roofs of the pung joe they remembered from the nineteen nineties, and made them into public spaces, linked by walkways in the air. The larger cultural buildings have upturned eaves meant to recall old bell towers, so the whole area has what they call a skyline in memory.

Then look down at the old street. The Qing Dynasty long shing Street has been kept on its original line and at its original scale, sunk slightly below the new ground level. South of the temple, osmanthus Street has become a food street. And look at the details: the structure follows the timber column-and-tie tradition of western sitch wahn, and where bamboo would once have been used, there's aluminium in the same colour, to meet fire rules.

What I like is the ambition: not a theme park around a temple, but a neighbourhood, with a library, a gallery, shops and benches by the water for older residents.

Before you go, look for the temple's pagoda. Local accounts tell a remarkable story about it: an earthquake split it in the eighteenth century, and half of it later fell, leaving the rest leaning but standing, a local wonder for generations.
