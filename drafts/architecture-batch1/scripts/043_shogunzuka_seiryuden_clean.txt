Shogunzuka Seiryuden, Kyoto (former site of KOU-AN)

Kyoto has a guardian buried under it, so the story goes. A clay general in full armour, about two and a half metres tall, with a sword, and a bow and arrows, lies in a mound on this hilltop, watching over the city.

Walk out onto the great wooden stage in front of the hall. You should be standing on a broad deck of timber high on the Higashiyama ridge, with the whole of Kyoto spread out below you, more than two hundred metres down, and the mountains on the far side of the valley.

Start with the mound. Shogunzuka means the general's mound. When Emperor Kanmu moved the capital here, in seven ninety-four, he is said to have had that clay general buried on this hill to protect his new city, and legend has it the mound rumbles when Kyoto is in danger.

Now turn round and look at the hall. It's newer than it looks. Seiryuden was opened in twenty fourteen by the temple of Shoren-in, but the building itself is an old martial arts hall from early last century, taken down elsewhere in the city and rebuilt up here. It was moved to house a national treasure, an ancient painting of the Blue Fudo, a fierce guardian figure, surrounded by flames.

[beat]

For two years, from twenty fifteen, this stage held something very different: KOU-AN, a tea house made entirely of glass by the designer Tokujin Yoshioka. No scroll, no flowers, just light, with a prism that threw rainbows across the floor in the afternoon. It's gone now, on to museums elsewhere, but I think it chose the right spot. A tea house is about stillness, and there's nowhere stiller than up here, above the noise.

If you've got the legs for it, take the Higashiyama path back down on foot. It's about half an hour through the woods to Shoren-in, the temple that runs this place. And if you're here in spring or autumn, come back after dark, when the city lights come on below.
