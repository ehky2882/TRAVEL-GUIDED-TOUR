# National Theatre, London

**Coordinate (WGS-84):** 51.50704, -0.11453 (exact, OpenStreetMap)

## Script (clean)

In nineteen eighty-eight, the future King Charles described this building as a clever way of putting a nuclear power station in the middle of London without anyone objecting. People have been arguing about it ever since, and that's rather the fun of it.

Stand on Waterloo Bridge, on the side facing downstream, and look over at the South Bank. The National Theatre is the great grey building just east of the bridge, stacked in horizontal layers of terraces, with two blunt towers rising out of the top.

Start with the layers. Those are terraces, open to the public, piled up and overlapping, and the architect, Denys Lasdun, meant them as a kind of landscape facing the river. Walk along them and you'll find the city opens up at every level: St Paul's downstream, Somerset House across the water.

Then the towers. They're fly towers, the tall boxes above each main stage where scenery is hoisted out of sight. The bigger one sits over the Olivier, the largest theatre here, with eleven hundred and sixty seats round an open stage. The Lyttelton has eight hundred and ninety, with a traditional picture-frame stage, and tucked underneath is the Dorfman, about four hundred, which used to be called the Cottesloe.

Now get close to the walls. The concrete was cast in rough timber boards, and the grain of the wood is still printed in it, inside and out. Put your hand flat against it if you like.

[beat]

It opened in stages in nineteen seventy-six and seventy-seven, and it's been listed Grade Two Star since nineteen ninety-four. So the building once compared to a power station is now officially protected. Concrete has a way of winning people round, slowly.

Come back after dark if you can. The fly towers are lit up at night, and from the middle of Waterloo Bridge the whole thing glows over the water. Then go in. The foyers are open to anyone, ticket or not.
