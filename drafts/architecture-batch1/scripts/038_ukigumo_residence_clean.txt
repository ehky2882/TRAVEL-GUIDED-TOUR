Ukigumo, Floating Clouds Residence, Tokyo

Before there was a house here, there was a gap. A patch of open sky between the neighbours' roofs, with clouds drifting across it. That's what the architect decided to build.

This is someone's home, so please stay on the street. It sits back on a flag-shaped plot, the kind that's common in Tokyo, reached down a narrow strip from the road. Look over the low ground floor and you should see the upper storey: a big, soft, white volume sitting on top of the house.

That white volume is the cloud. The architect, Ukei Shimada, designed this as his own house, and the name, Ukigumo, means floating clouds. Upstairs, inside the cloud, is the living room, the dining room and the kitchen, the part of the house where the day happens, lifted up towards the bit of sky that started it all.

Now drop your eyes to the ground floor. Down here are the bedrooms, storage, and an earth-floored room, the old Japanese idea of a space that's half indoors and half out. Big sliding wooden doors open it onto the little front garden, so on a warm day the house opens up to the street and the plants.

It's small, about a hundred and twelve square metres, and it was finished in twenty twenty-five.

The lovely thing about the word ukigumo is that, in Japanese, it doesn't only mean a cloud. It also suggests a life in drift, never quite settled. There's something rather brave about calling your own house that. It's an admission that a home isn't fixed, it just happens to be where you've floated to.

Before you go, have a look up at the gaps between the houses all along this street. In a city as dense as Tokyo, every one of them is a little piece of sky that somebody hasn't built on yet.
