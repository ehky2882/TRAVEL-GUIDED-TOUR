Rostiger Nagel, Lusatian Lake District

Locals call it the Rusty Nail. Officially it's a landmark. Either way, it's a thirty-metre steel tower standing on land that, not long ago, was a vast open-cast coal mine.

Stand on the shore where the canal runs into the lake. From the land side, you should see a tall, rust-brown tower of folded steel plates, with staircases zig-zagging up through it like a sculpture. Walk round to the water side and it changes into a single, sheer, narrow column.

Start with the rust. It's weathering steel, which forms a stable coat of rust that protects the metal underneath. The whole tower is made of steel sheet just six millimetres thick, welded into hollow sections stiffened inside with ribs, so every panel is part of the structure. It weighs a hundred and eleven tonnes and stands on a triangular plan.

Now look at the shape. It's meant to recall the huge steel machines of the lignite mines that worked this whole region, and the climbing stairs stand for people reshaping the landscape. A hundred and sixty-two steps take you to the top.

It was designed by two young Munich architects, Stefan Giers and Susanne Gabriel, who won an international competition in two thousand and five. It opened at the end of October two thousand and eight, as part of a big regional building exhibition aimed at turning a ruined mining area into a lake district.

[beat]

That's the real story here. Every lake you can see from the top, the Sedlitzer See, the Geierswalder See, the Partwitzer See, is a flooded mine. The water is new. The Rusty Nail is a monument to a landscape that was used up and then given a second life.

So do climb it. From the platform you can see the lakes, and on the horizon, the power stations at Jänschwalde and Schwarze Pumpe, still standing, reminding you where all of this began.
