Casa Nido

The planning rules here would have let the owners knock the old house down and start again. They kept it, and built a second house inside it.

This is a private home, so stay on the pavement and look at the corner building. You're after old solid brick, laid with lime mortar, and a crisp, simple volume rising out of it. Where windows and doors were no longer needed, the openings have been bricked in, but their outlines are left showing, like scars the house is allowed to keep.

Torrefiel is a working-class district on Valencia's northern edge, filled in during the middle of the last century as people came in from the countryside. Not much of that early fabric survives, and the architects, Iterare, with Rubén Gutiérrez and Pedro Ponce, treated this shell as a thing worth protecting. They stripped the plaster, repaired the brick and the mortar, and used the old walls as a nest. Hence the name: Casa Nido, the nest house.

Those walls earn their keep, too. Lime mortar lets the brick breathe and flex a little, and that thick masonry soaks up the day's heat and gives it back after dark, which in a Valencian summer is rather the point.

Inside, out of sight, there's a courtyard carved into the back of the plot, a nod to the old Iberian patio house, lit by a window nearly seven metres tall. A narrow limestone stair runs up the middle.

[beat]

And that's the quiet argument this corner makes. You don't need to demolish an ordinary building to make a modern one. Sometimes the old house is the best part of the new one.

Before you go, wander a few streets in any direction and look for other low brick houses like this one, the survivors of old Torrefiel. Once you've seen what was done here, you'll spot the ones still waiting their turn.
