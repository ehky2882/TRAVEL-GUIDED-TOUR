Chentro Eedriko dee Veenya Moorahta, Rome

Rome is a city that has been building for water for two thousand years. This is one of its newer answers, and it doesn't look anything like an aqueduct.

From Via del kazahlay solahro, look up at the tall steel structure rising over the trees. You should make out a slim shaft carrying two great rings, a narrower one high up and a much wider one lower down, clad in vertical stainless steel panels that catch the light.

Those rings are the point. They're water tanks, shaped like doughnuts. The upper one is about seven metres across and the lower one about thirty-four. Underneath the shiny cladding the structure is weathering steel, the kind that rusts to a deliberate dark brown, but here it's been dressed in stainless panels running vertically, which suit the curves.

This is a piezometric tower, which simply means a tank set high enough that gravity does the work of pushing water through the pipes. It was designed by Francesco palpa chelly for ah chay ah, Rome's water company, and built between nineteen eighty-five and nineteen eighty-nine.

And it's no ornament. From here, water goes out to around four hundred thousand people, across ay oor and lowren teeno, along the ostee enzay, and on into parts of testahcho, Ripa and san sahba, right into the old city.

What I like is that nobody tried to hide it. Most cities tuck their tanks away behind a wall. This one was given the full attention of an architect and allowed to stand up and shine, which seems right for something that keeps half a district in water.

If you've got time, walk on into the veenya moorahta housing estate next door, built in the nineteen seventies on a curving plan with stepped terraces, a bit like a hill town. Look back towards the tower from its streets and you'll see how it acts as the neighbourhood's lighthouse.
