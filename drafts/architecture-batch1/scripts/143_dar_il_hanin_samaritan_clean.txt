Dar il-Ħanin Samaritan, Santa Venera

The architect Richard England said he wanted to make a theatre for the soul, rather than a stage for the body. This is it: a garden of quiet spaces, in the middle of a busy suburb.

Stand at the entrance and look in. You should see a group of pale stone walls, courtyards and water channels, with sculpture and planting, a walled garden of calm set apart from the streets around it.

Start with the idea. The complex was built for the Society of Christian Doctrine, founded in Malta by Saint George Preca, as a conference centre and meditation garden. England designed it as a sequence of silent spaces, to walk through slowly. One writer called it an oasis of peace, with an ambition that's almost like Piranesi, the great engraver of imaginary architecture.

Now find the water chapel. It's lit entirely by light reflected off the surrounding water channel, so the walls shimmer with moving light. The project quotes the Swedish writer Axel Munthe: the soul needs more space than the body.

Then look at the sculpture and the glass. There's a Risen Christ by John Grima, and in the newer garden, glass artworks by England's son, Marc. The complex grew over many years, with the main garden and chapel built between two thousand and nine and two thousand and fourteen by a local master builder, Lolly Vella, and later additions since.

What I like is that it's made almost entirely of things you can't see in a photograph: silence, reflected light, the sound of water. You have to stand in it to understand it.

Before you go, sit for a few minutes in the water chapel and watch the reflections move across the walls. That's the whole building, really, in a single moment.
