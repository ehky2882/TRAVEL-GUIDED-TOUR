Jorge Juan 55, Madrid

Here's a shop that was designed, built, finished, and then never opened. For more than thirty years it sat behind its front, perfectly preserved, like something out of a fairy tale.

Stand on Calle de Jorge Juan at number fifty-five. You should see an ordinary, handsome apartment block from about nineteen ten. Look at the shopfront on the right-hand side at street level. That's it.

Start with the story. In nineteen eighty-six and seven, the architect Francisco Alonso de Santos, Paco Alonso to most people, rebuilt this space as a luxury shoe shop for a footwear businessman, Manuel Losada. It's only about two hundred square metres. And then, for reasons no one seems quite sure of, it never opened. It stayed closed, almost untouched, from nineteen eighty-seven onwards.

Now look inside, if you can. Writers describe it as a tectonic puzzle: imposing blocks of marble and heavy solid wood fitted together, with mirrors and contrasting textures. The style is often called brutalist, but it's brutalism made like jewellery. One newspaper called it a work of goldsmithery. Even the furniture is treated as part of the architecture, including three round tables, so you can't take the room apart without losing it.

From twenty seventeen, the Toledo school of architecture used it for exhibitions. Then the building was sold, works started, and architects raised the alarm. In December twenty twenty-three, the city moved to protect the façade, the interior, the furniture, the stairs and the patio.

[beat]

And here's the twist. It finally has a tenant: an estate agency. I rather like that. A shop designed to sell the finest shoes in Madrid ended up selling flats in Madrid instead, and the marble survived.

Before you go, walk a block or two along Jorge Juan. This is one of Madrid's smartest shopping streets, and once you've seen number fifty-five, every other shopfront looks a little flimsy.
