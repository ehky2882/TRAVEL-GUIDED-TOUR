# Suzhou Museum of Contemporary Art

**Place:** Shore of Jinji Lake, Suzhou Industrial Park, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China  
**Coordinate (WGS-84):** 31.31703, 120.70897 (exact)  
**Note:** Opened 30 August 2026  
**Length:** 339 words (about 136 seconds)

## Script (clean)

Suzhou is the city of classical gardens. So when a Danish architect was asked to build its new art museum, he built a garden, with a roof like a ribbon of silk.

Stand in the gardens by the water and look back at the museum. You should see a cluster of low pavilions, twelve of them, gathered like a little village, all tied together by one continuous roof that rises and dips and folds over them like a ribbon, with Jinji Lake behind you.

Start with that roof. Its gentle undulations echo the curved, tiled eaves of old Suzhou buildings. Bjarke Ingels, of BIG, says the roof is effectively the building's true fifth façade.

Now walk in under it. The idea comes from the lang, the covered walkway that winds through every classical Suzhou garden, taking you from view to view. Here it becomes the spine of the museum, a continuous route with smaller paths branching off to the galleries. There are bridges and tunnels too, linking spaces above and below ground. Four of the pavilions hold the main exhibition halls. The others hold a theatre, a hall, a restaurant and the entrance.

It's big, sixty thousand square metres, and it opened on the thirtieth of August, twenty twenty-six, with a Picasso exhibition, a large survey of contemporary Chinese art, and a show on BIG's own work. It's the practice's first completed art museum.

Ingels calls it a sort of Scandinavian reinterpretation of the Chinese garden. And he wanted it to join the city to the lake rather than block it.

[beat]

What I find interesting is the cheek of it: a Danish firm borrowing Suzhou's own most famous idea and handing it back. But the garden has always been about walking and pausing and looking, which is exactly what a gallery is for.

Before you go, walk out to the lake shore. The gardens and paths stay open after the museum closes, so it's a fine place for an evening stroll.
