Umbral Plazoleta Negra

On a road known for its hairpin bends, this spot is among the few places that ask you to stop and, if you like, to take your shoes off.

Pull in safely at curve twenty-two on the road up to Farellones, in Lo Barnechea, and you'll find a semicircular stone platform on the mountainside, set on a wall laid in the traditional way, with a wooden deck reaching out over the valley. Mind the traffic, which can be heavy in the ski season, and keep well back from the edge.

This is the Umbral of Plazoleta Negra, umbral being Spanish for threshold, and it belongs to a series of ritual spaces conceived by Cazú Zegers, the same architect who restored the Hotel Magnolia downtown; this one dates from 2018 and 2019, and the municipality credits the artist Vicente Gajardo with the work.

The wall was built by stonemasons from Farellones itself, using techniques handed down over generations, and the space is meant as a ceremonial gateway into the mountains, where visitors are invited to remove their shoes and leave a small offering to the apus, the guardian spirits of the Andean peaks.

Zegers describes these thresholds as places for leaving something behind without abandoning your history, and that's a useful frame for a road that is usually just the quickest way to the ski slopes.

From the deck you look down the valley towards the city and up towards the glaciers, and the idea is simply to arrive at the mountain with a little attention, rather than at speed.

[beat]

It asks very little of you, and gives back a surprising amount.

Before you drive on, walk the length of the platform slowly and look up to the snowline; and if this sort of place appeals, another of Zegers' thresholds, the Agora of the Ancestral Woman, sits on the northern slope of the Parque Metropolitano back in the city.
