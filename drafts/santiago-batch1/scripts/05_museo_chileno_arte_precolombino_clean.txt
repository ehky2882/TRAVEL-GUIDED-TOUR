Chilean Museum of Pre-Columbian Art

Chilean history, as most of us learn it, begins in 1541 with a Spanish captain and a new city, yet the building in front of you tells a story thousands of years older.

The long, pale colonial building at the corner of Bandera and Compañía, one block west of the Plaza de Armas and just behind the cathedral, is the old Royal Customs House, built between 1805 and 1807, and the museum's entrance is on Bandera, at number three hundred and sixty-one.

Sergio Larraín García-Moreno, the man behind it, was an architect who spent close to fifty years collecting pre-Columbian objects, and in December 1981 he opened this museum to give his collection a permanent public home; it now holds more than three thousand pieces from around a hundred cultures, from Mesoamerica to the southern Andes.

Then head downstairs, into space that the architect Smiljan Radić created beneath the old building in a renovation completed in 2014, which enlarged the museum by roughly seventy per cent. Down there is the permanent display called Chile Before Chile, and the title is the point.

Among its most moving pieces are those of the Chinchorro people of the northern coast, who were preparing their dead with great care some seven thousand years ago, among the earliest such practices known anywhere and older than Egypt's; nearby stand chemamüll, tall carved wooden figures from Mapuche funerary tradition.

The curators' argument is gentle but firm, namely that the country's story didn't begin with a founding date on a plaque, and that the diversity of everything that came before is something to be proud of rather than tidied away.

It's a museum that quietly moves the start of the story back by several thousand years.

On your way out, look for the Inca quipu, the knotted cords used to keep records without writing, then walk round to the Plaza de Armas, where the official version of the founding sits in bronze, on horseback.
