Tahyair Rohka Naygra

High in the Kahohn del Myepo, with the river below and mountains on every side, one of Chile's great sculptors chose to build a world of concrete.

The road up the Myepo valley, in the commune of San Hosay deh Myepo, brings you to Rohka Naygra, with the artist's house on one side and, across the road, the workshop and museum: a double height studio behind a portico of red pigmented concrete, and terraces of sculpture stepping up the slope. This is a private home and archive, so visits are by arrangement, and unless you've been invited in, please enjoy it from the roadside.

The sculptor is Fedaireeko Assler, born in nineteen twenty nine, a winner of Chile's National Prize for Visual Arts and three times of the Altasor award, and one of the generation of the nineteen fifties who pushed Chilean sculpture towards abstraction.

His material is concrete, which he has worked since the nineteen seventies using moulds cut from expanded polystyrene, which lets enormous weights take on soft, organic forms; he has said that his sculptures are made for people, to be touched and felt and even walked into.

With his partner, Franseeska Daylahno, he settled here in nineteen ninety three, turned a former inn into a house museum and built eleven terraces into the landscape, and the archive now runs to more than a thousand pieces, along with some two hundred numbered sketchbooks, each a record of how a work began.

The lovely part came in April twenty twenty six, when Chile declared the whole ensemble a national monument, and the decree protects not only the buildings and terraces but the relationship between them, the valley and the mountains, which means the law now recognises a view.

Few artists get to see their working life turned into a landscape worth protecting.

If you can't visit, look for Assler's concrete works back in Santiago, in the Parkay deh lahs Eskooltooras in Proveedensia beside the Mapohcho, where you'll recognise the same instinct to make heavy things feel light.
