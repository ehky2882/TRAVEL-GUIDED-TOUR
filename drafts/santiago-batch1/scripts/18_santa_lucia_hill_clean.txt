Santa Lucía Hill

Before there was a city here, there was this rocky outcrop, which the Mapuche knew as Huelén.

Facing you from the Alameda is the hill's grandest entrance, the Neptune Terrace, a theatrical sweep of twin staircases and balustrades around a great fountain, painted in the yellow and white you'll see all the way up to the summit.

The Spanish conqueror Pedro de Valdivia took the hill on the thirteenth of December 1540, the feast day of Saint Lucy, which is how it got its present name, and two months later the new city was founded at its foot.

Over the following centuries it served as a lookout and a fortress, with the Hidalgo fort completed in 1820, and, rather unexpectedly, as an observatory, set up in 1849 by an American astronomer, James Gilliss, whose instruments went on to form the nucleus of Chile's national observatory.

What you're looking at, though, is mostly the work of one restless man, the city's intendant Benjamín Vicuña Mackenna, who from 1872 turned a bare rock into a romantic public park, with winding paths, fountains, a chapel lit by gas and these yellow and white façades.

It's worth sitting with that for a moment, because a hill first known by its Mapuche name, then a conqueror's foothold and a fort, ended up as a pleasure garden for the whole city, and it still is.

It keeps time, too, since a cannon on the hill traditionally fires at noon, so if you hear a bang at midday, nothing is wrong.

Climb on up through the terraces to the summit lookout, where the final steps get narrow and steep, and the view runs from the Andes to the towers of Providencia; on the way down, the cafés of the Lastarria neighbourhood are just across the road.
