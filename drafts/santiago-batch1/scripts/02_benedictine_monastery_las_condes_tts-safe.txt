Benedictine Monastery of the Holy Trinity of Lass Kondess

There's no stained glass in this church, no gilding and no painted saints, and that absence is precisely the design.

Follow the path up the hillside on Montekasseeno, high on the eastern edge of Lass Kondess, until the monastery church sits just above you: a cluster of plain white volumes stepped into the slope, with the city falling away behind you to the west. If the doors are open and you go in, keep your voice low, because this is a working Benedictine monastery and the monks still pray here through the day.

The architects, Marteen Korrayah and Gabreeyel Gwarda, were, rather unusually, both monks of this community, and they designed the church between nineteen sixty two and nineteen sixty four; Gwarda went on to become one of Chile's most respected historians, winning the National History Prize in nineteen eighty four, and the monastery remained his home.

Then look at the shape, which is cleverer than it first appears: the church is essentially two cubes joined at their corners, one for the altar and one for the congregation, so the space turns on a diagonal and you're never quite squared up to a flat wall.

The materials are almost nothing at all, just concrete and lime with no ornament anywhere. The interesting bit is this: the light was always meant to be the protagonist, entering indirectly so that you seldom see its source, washing across the white surfaces and shifting as the hours pass, which means the interior is never quite the same twice.

Chile declared it a national monument in nineteen eighty one, and it later became the first Chilean building to receive a Getty Foundation conservation grant, which puts a small hillside church in the same company as the Sydney Opera House.

Before you leave, find a place at the edge of the hill and look east to the Andes, because on a clear winter morning, with fresh snow on the peaks, you'll understand why anyone would choose to pray up here.
