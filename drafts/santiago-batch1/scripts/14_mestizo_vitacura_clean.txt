Mestizo

Those great grey boulders are not decoration, because they are holding up the roof.

Along the northern edge of Parque Bicentenario, on Avenida Bicentenario, sits the restaurant: a long, low pavilion partly sunk into the ground, its roof resting on a row of enormous rounded stones, with glass between them looking out over the park.

The architect is Smiljan Radić, who won the Pritzker Prize, architecture's highest honour, in 2026, and whose international reputation took off with his Serpentine Pavilion in London in 2014; this restaurant is one of the buildings that made his name at home.

The stones are a granite the quarrymen call ala de mosca, or fly's wing, brought from Pirque, south of the city, and some of them weigh around ten tonnes. Their tops and bottoms were cut flat so they could carry the roof beams, while the rest was left looking much like the natural rocks you might find in the park.

The engineers made sure each stone carries only a small fraction of what granite can bear, and the back of the building takes the earthquake loads, so the effect of rocks casually propping up a roof is really careful engineering dressed as nonchalance.

I always think this is Radić's great trick, taking something primitive, a stone or a simple shelter, and making it feel both ancient and quite new. Critics have compared the boulders to the carved maidens that hold up the roofs of Greek temples, except that here nobody has carved them into anything at all.

The food, appropriately, is upmarket Chilean cooking, eaten in the shade of rocks rather older than the country.

Once you've had a good look, walk into the park itself, past the lagoons, and then turn round: from a distance the restaurant almost sinks into the slope, which is rather the point of it.
