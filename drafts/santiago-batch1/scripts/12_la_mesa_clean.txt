La Mesa

Olive trees and a kitchen garden greet you at the door here, which tells you most of what you need to know about the cooking inside.

Walk along Alonso de Córdova until you reach number two thousand seven hundred and sixty-seven, and look for the entrance framed by greenery: an urban garden, olive trees, and beyond them a dining room with plants spilling out onto an outdoor area.

The chef, Álvaro Romero, describes his approach as simple but refined cooking built on local produce, and the name is the manifesto, since La Mesa just means the table, the place where you want guests to feel at home and to come back.

The surprise is in the kitchen, which runs entirely on electricity as part of a broader commitment to sustainability that extends to buying from small producers on fair terms. Try the sea urchin ceviche, a very Chilean pleasure, or the grilled scallops, and if vegetables are more your thing, there's a vegetable tartare as well.

The wine list follows the same instinct, with a house wine called Leonera from the Itata Valley in the south, one of Chile's oldest wine regions, and a newer wine bar alongside, Masal, which concentrates on small producers from Chile and further afield.

Put another way, this is a restaurant that has built an argument out of modesty, with no theatre and no grand gestures, simply good things from nearby, cooked carefully, in a room that feels like someone's home.

[beat]

That's harder to pull off than it sounds, and it has earned La Mesa a place on the 50 Best Discovery list.

Take a proper look at the planting by the door, then drift along Alonso de Córdova and its side streets, where small galleries open their doors most afternoons and the walk is as good as any gallery itself.
