Fookasahwa

This restaurant is named after a teacher, which is not something you can say about many places.

Head for the Kahsa Kostanaira complex on Aveneeda Nwayva Kostanaira and take the lift right to the top, because Fookasahwa occupies the fourth floor rooftop, a space the building simply calls Roof, with a terrace that looks east towards the Andes.

The chef is Markos Bah aysa, a veteran of Santiago's Japanese kitchens, who opened Fookasahwa in December twenty twenty two and cooks here alongside his twin sons, Marcus and Lucas. The name honours Nowkee Fookasahwa, the Japanese chef who mentored Bah aysa when they worked together years ago, so every booking is, in a small way, a thank you note.

The menu brings Japanese technique to the Chilean coast, with touches of Neekay, the cooking of Peru's Japanese community, and a very local larder: Patagonian king crab, lobster, truffles and caviar from Chilean sturgeon farms, alongside gyohza filled with lamb and foie gras.

If you'd rather hand the decisions over entirely, there's an ohmakahsay, the Japanese custom of letting the chef choose for you, which seems especially fitting in a kitchen built on trust between master and pupil.

It's worth noticing how naturally the pairing works, because Chile's long Pacific coastline produces some remarkable seafood, and Japanese cooking is, at heart, about respecting exactly that kind of raw material, so the marriage here feels less like fusion than an old friendship made official.

A teacher's name over the door, and a father and his sons at the counter, make for a restaurant that wears its lineage rather proudly.

If you're up on the roof around sunset, wait until the last light has gone off the mountains before you leave, then stroll a block over to Alonso deh Kordova, where the galleries and design shops make for a pleasant evening walk.
