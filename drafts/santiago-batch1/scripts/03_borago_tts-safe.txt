Borragoh

More than two hundred foragers, fishers and small growers, scattered from the northern desert to Patagonia, supply the restaurant in front of you.

Set back from the long Aveneeda San Hosay Mareea Eskreevah deh Balagair, at number five thousand nine hundred and seventy, Borragoh keeps a quiet frontage here in Veetakoora, with the rounded bulk of Serro Mankayway rising to the north. It only opens in the evening, so by day you may find little sign of life, which rather suits a kitchen that prefers the food to do the talking.

The chef is Rodolfo Goozmahn, who opened Borragoh in two thousand and six with an idea that seemed eccentric at the time: to cook only from Chile's own larder, including plants, seaweeds and fruits that grow nowhere else, many of them barely known even to Chileans.

There's just one menu, called Endaymeeka, which runs to somewhere between twelve and eighteen small courses and changes through the year, and Goozmahn draws heavily on Mapoochay food traditions, with a research centre alongside that catalogues and tests new ingredients. During the long pandemic closure the team even launched Moomahmee, a burger built from native ingredients, which tells you how seriously they take the idea.

Here's what strikes me about the whole project: fine dining has usually borrowed its prestige from elsewhere, from French technique and imported luxuries, whereas Borragoh built its reputation by looking inward, and the world followed, placing it on the World's Fifty Best list every year since twenty fifteen and giving Goozmahn Latin America's Icon Award in twenty twenty five.

A country's wild hillsides and cold coastline, served as a tasting menu, turned out to be the most persuasive case for Chilean cooking that anyone had made.

If you're not eating here tonight, the walking trails on Serro Mankayway begin a short drive north, and the scrubby, scented plants along them are a good introduction to the kind of wild Chile that ends up on these plates.
