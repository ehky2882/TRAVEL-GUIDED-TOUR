Mesteeso Eetahleea

Most architects are hired to make things new, but here a Pritzker Prize winner was asked to make an old warehouse last a little longer.

In the block between Aveneeda Eetahleea and the streets of Mareen and Santa Eesabel, in the heart of Barreeo Eetahleea, the restaurant occupies a converted warehouse, and its entrance patio is marked by a tall green column and an olive tree.

The building is more than forty years old and once housed the design workshop of Artooro Mooroo ah, whose family also owns the original Mesteeso in Parkay Beesentenahreeo; Eegnahseeo Mooroo ah, an engineer and artist, drove this second location, and the architect, once again, is Smeelyan Rahdeetch.

Rahdeetch described the job as a basic repair of a fragile, run down structure, and he meant it. The warehouse was scanned with drones to decide what could be saved, cracks were left on show, and everything new, from the floors to the kitchen, is marked out in green concrete so you can always tell old from new. That six metre green column at the entrance is by the sculptor Marsayla Korrayah, his long time creative partner.

Inside, light filters down through skylights in the old roof, a tall pole carries a round lamp that stands in for the sun, the olive tree is said to be around a century old, and the tables and folding chairs were rescued from antique dealers in the neighbourhood.

The real story, then, is restraint: in a city where old buildings are easily replaced, a Pritzker laureate chose to mend, and to make the mending visible, which feels right in a neighbourhood that has long lived on restoring old furniture.

Some visitors find the cracks unsettling and others find them beautiful, and both reactions are rather the point.

Afterwards, wander along Aveneeda Eetahleea itself, where the antique and restoration workshops that supplied these tables still trade, and the side streets hide courtyards full of small designers and cafes.
