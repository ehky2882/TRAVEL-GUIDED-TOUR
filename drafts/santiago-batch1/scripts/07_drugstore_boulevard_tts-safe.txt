Drugstore Boulevard

Long before Santiago had shopping malls it had arcades, and this is one of the few from that era that still feels properly alive.

That passage opening off Aveneeda Proveedensia in front of you, at number two thousand one hundred and twenty four, near Lohs Layohness metro station, is the Drugstore, a warren of small shops, cafes and bookshops tucked in behind the street frontage.

It opened in the late nineteen sixties, the creation of two families, the Yakohneez and the Santa Kroosez, who had admired the shopping arcades of Europe and wanted something similar for a Proveedensia that was fast becoming the city's fashionable address.

By the nineteen eighties it was one of the places to be seen in Santiago, with record shops such as Fooseeon drawing the young and design shops drawing everybody else, and that thread of design and culture has never really snapped.

Have a wander and you'll find bookshops including Altameera and Tahk, cafes where people settle in to read or work, and dozens of small labels; more than sixty per cent of the units are independent or collaborative ventures, and the management has been championing Chilean design for over twenty years.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that Proveedensia's arcades were a kind of experiment before the big malls arrived, and the municipality now marks them as part of its hidden urban heritage. Most were overtaken by the giants, but the Drugstore survived by doing the opposite of a mall, staying small, local and a little bit slow.

It's the sort of place where you can still meet the person who made the thing you're buying.

When you're done, pick up a book by a Chilean author if one catches your eye, then step back out onto Proveedensia and look east, where the Gran Torray Kostanaira rises above the rooftops, the age of the mega mall in a single silhouette, barely fifteen minutes' walk away.
