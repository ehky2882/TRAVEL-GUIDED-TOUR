Plaza de Armas de Santiago

It's February 1541, and a Spanish expedition led by Pedro de Valdivia is marking out a new city on the banks of the Mapocho, beginning with this square.

Face towards the ornate front of the Central Post Office and the square lays out its history for you: on your left, along the western side, the long stone front of the Metropolitan Cathedral, and ahead of you, along the northern edge, the post office, the old Royal Court building and the town hall.

The grid came first, laid out by Pedro de Gamboa as a chequerboard of blocks with this square at its heart, and the streets around you still follow those colonial lines almost five centuries on.

The cathedral is the fifth church to stand on its site, its predecessors lost to fire and earthquake; along the north side, the old Royal Court now houses the National History Museum, and the town hall has governed from the same side since 1895.

Then look for the statues, which tell two founding stories at once. There's Valdivia on horseback, and not far away a monument to Chile's Indigenous peoples, a great carved stone face, and since how the conquest should be remembered is still debated in Chile, the square holds both readings without settling the argument.

Which brings us to what this square really means. Archaeological evidence suggests that an Inca settlement lay beneath the colonial city, so the square that announced a beginning was, in all likelihood, built on top of an older one.

Every layer of Santiago, from Inca and colonial to republican and modern, meets in this one square.

Spare a minute for the chess players who gather around the square with their boards, then walk round behind the cathedral to Bandera street, where the Pre-Columbian Art Museum tells the much longer story.
