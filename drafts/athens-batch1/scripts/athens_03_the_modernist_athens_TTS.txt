The Modernist Athens

Before this was a hotel, it was a little piece of Canada.

You should be on a quiet street on the lower slopes of kollo nah kee, with a leafy residential square nearby and a clean, symmetrical nineteen fifties building in front of you: flat planes, regular windows, nothing fussy. This was the Canadian Embassy, and the building still has that composed, slightly formal air of a place where letters were stamped and visas were checked.

The conversion came from the Greek hotelier koss tees ka rah dzas, whose first Modernist opened in Thessaloniki, and the architects form related. They didn't try to hide the post war bones. They leaned into them.

Have a look inside the lobby if the door's open. Marble, raw plaster, bronze and black glass: the hard, assertive materials of the period, set against the softer oak floors and leather headboards upstairs.

Then there's the gift detail. The thirty eight rooms are sold in sizes, like clothing: small, medium, large and extra large. No grand names, no Deluxe Superior Premier. Just pick the size that fits, which is rather a good joke for a building that used to process paperwork.

Up top is a planted roof garden with a view across to the Acropolis, and on some evenings there are vinyl sessions in the bar downstairs.

Here's what I find interesting. Most hotels in old buildings borrow their glamour from the eighteen hundreds. This one borrows it from the nineteen fifties, a decade Athens is only now beginning to love. The fifties built half this city, and a building like this makes the case that it was worth keeping.

From here, the hotel's own favourite is a ten minute walk away, across from the National Gallery: The Runner, a tall figure of stacked glass by the sculptor koss tas vah roh tsoss. It's been there since nineteen ninety four. Catch it in low sun, when the light runs right through it.
