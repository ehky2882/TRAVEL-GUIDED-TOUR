There's no lobby to speak of, and no restaurant. Just a house, and a great deal of art.

mahn dzah roo Street is a short pedestrian lane off pah tree ar hoo ee oh ah keem, one block from kollo nah kee Square. No cars come down here, which is half the charm. Number ten is the neoclassical townhouse with the tall windows and the quiet front door. Stand across from it and give the façade a moment.

The house dates from the late nineteen twenties, the tail end of the neoclassical style that dressed Athens for a century. Today it holds just six suites, two to a floor, and they're built so they can be joined together if a family or a group of friends takes a whole level.

The real point of the place, though, is what's on the walls. Shila works more like a gallery you can sleep in. The art and design come mostly from Greek artists and makers, they change on rotation, and nearly everything you see can be bought: the paintings, the ceramics, the furniture. The art director is the Greek photographer ef tee hee ah steh fah nee dee, and the choices feel like one person's eye rather than a hotel group's.

Inside there's a garden courtyard, a library lounge and a roof terrace. What there isn't is a dining room. Breakfast comes to your suite, and for dinner the staff will point you to the neighbourhood.

And that seems right for kollo nah kee. This has always been the part of Athens where people lived among their collections, in houses exactly like this one. Shila simply opened the door and let you stay the night.

If you've got a few minutes, walk the one block to kollo nah kee Square and sit at a café table for a coffee. It's the best way to watch the neighbourhood go about its business, and the people watching is very much part of the architecture.
