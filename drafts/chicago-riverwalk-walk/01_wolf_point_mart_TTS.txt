Two ocean basins nearly touch about six miles southwest of here, and that near-miss is the only reason there's a city on this spot.

Look across the water and slightly left, where the river splits into three. The blunt point of land in the middle, with glass towers standing on it now, is Wolf Point — site of Chicago's first hotel, and of the first bridges anyone threw across this river.

Why here? Because of the gap. Southwest of the city, past a marsh the early settlers called Mud Lake, the land rises a few feet and then everything changes its mind: water on one side drains east toward the Atlantic, water on the other runs south to the Gulf of Mexico. The rise was so slight that in a wet season the two systems joined and you could paddle straight through, and in a dry one you got out and carried the canoe about a mile and a half. That route had been in use for centuries before the first Europeans were shown it in sixteen seventy-three.

Now look right, at the limestone.

The Merchandise Mart, nineteen thirty, by Graham, Anderson, prohbst and White — four million square feet, and the largest building in the world when it opened. Marshall Field's built it to pull thirteen scattered wholesale warehouses under one roof, and the company president who pushed hardest for it was also chairman of the Chicago Plan Commission, so the man who wanted the riverfront to look better was chairing the body in charge of making it look better.

A portage is a place you pass through. Less than a century after a canal replaced the carrying, the largest building on earth was parked where people used to hurry past.

Keep going east, under Wells and under luh sal, to the marker set in near the corner of luh sal and wah ker: the Eastland.
