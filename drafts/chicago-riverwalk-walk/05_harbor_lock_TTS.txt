Which way is the water going?

You're at the east end of the Riverwalk, under the Lake Shore Drive bridge. Look east, past the bridge, toward open water. Set across the mouth of the river there is a low concrete structure with gates in it. You can see it from here; you can't walk out to it.

The Chicago Harbor Lock — six hundred feet long, eighty feet wide, finished in nineteen thirty-eight, and the reason everything behind you works.

The river surface sits somewhere between one and four feet below the level of the lake. Left alone, Lake Michigan would simply pour in. In effect, that's the arrangement: since nineteen hundred this river has run away from the lake, west and inland, because the city dug a canal twenty-eight miles southwest on a downhill grade and let gravity carry the sewage toward the Mississippi instead of toward the water it drank. The lock manages the rest — how much lake water is let in, and how boats get between two surfaces at different heights. It cycles more than ten thousand times in a boating season, ten to fifteen minutes at a time, boats riding up and down inside it like an elevator.

The reversal is not something that happened. It's something being done, right now, by people on shift.

And it isn't absolute. Dozens of times since the mid nineteen eighties, after enormous rain, the gates have been opened to let the river run out into the lake the original way for a few hours, because the alternative is water in basements. The old direction is still there, waiting.

A mile and a quarter behind you, you started at a fork where two ocean basins nearly touch, and where people carried boats over the gap between them. You're standing at the gap now. Somebody put a gate on it.

Stay a while if the boats are moving. The lock is slow on purpose, and nobody has ever worked out how to hurry water. And the whole time you're watching it, the river at your back is running inland, away from the lake, because that is what was decided.
