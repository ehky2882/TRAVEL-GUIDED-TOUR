Each half of the bridge over your head weighs three thousand four hundred tons, and it is lifted by a pair of motors with about as much power as a small car engine.

The trick is counterweights. bas kyool is French for seesaw, and that's the mechanism exactly: the roadway is a lever, a block of concrete and steel sits on the short end, and the whole assembly pivots on a shaft called a truh nyun. Balance it finely enough and a hundred and eight horsepower is plenty. The motors were kept that small deliberately — electricity was expensive in nineteen twenty.

It opened in May nineteen twenty: the first bridge anywhere built as a double-leaf, double-deck bas kyool, with two roadways stacked one above the other. Before it existed, Michigan Avenue simply stopped at the river, and the street on the far side went by a different name: Pine.

The stone tower on this bank is a museum — five floors of one bridgehouse, with the gear room at the bottom, where you can stand next to the gears that do the lifting. Open most days in season.

In the nineteen twenties this bridge went up more than three thousand times a year. Boats had the right of way and the traffic sat and waited for them. It now lifts about forty times a year, spring and fall, mostly for sailboats heading out to the lake and coming back.

The river used to interrupt the street. Now the street tolerates the river a few dozen times a season, on a schedule the river doesn't set.

Keep east. The path runs out from under Michigan Avenue, opens up, and ends where the river does — at the Lake Shore Drive bridge and the lock.
