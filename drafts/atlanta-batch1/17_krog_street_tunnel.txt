There's one rule in here, and nobody wrote it down. Nothing stays.

Stand at one of the two mouths — better, a few steps inside, where the daylight stops and your own footsteps change. This is Krog Street, and it runs under a working rail yard, which is the only reason the tunnel exists: the road needed to cross the tracks, and the tracks weren't moving. Behind you on the west is Cabbagetown; east of you, Reynoldstown and Inman Park.

Walk in before you decide anything about it.

The tunnel was cut just before the First World War, for freight, and has done that dull job ever since. It has also been, for about forty years, a wall the neighborhood repaints constantly. I'll leave it at that, because whatever is on these walls today won't be by the time this recording is a year old, and describing it would be a way of lying.

The impermanence isn't a flaw the tunnel has; it's the arrangement. The walls belong to whoever showed up last, which means they belong to nobody — a difficult thing for a city to leave alone.

In October 2014 somebody tried not to. A private, ticketed party was permitted down here over the local planning unit's objection, and the night before, roughly a hundred people came with rollers and gray paint and covered it end to end. By morning there was nothing to sell tickets to. Within days it had started coming back.

That's the clearest thing anyone has said about this place, and it was said by erasing it.

One thing to carry with you, because it follows this corridor the whole way around. Atlanta BeltLine Incorporated counts four thousand four hundred and twenty-five affordable homes built or preserved against a goal of five thousand six hundred by 2030. Dan Immergluck, who studies housing at Georgia State, counts something different: prices along the corridor rose faster than prices away from it, and they started rising years before most of those homes existed. Both counts are accurate, and what they add up to is still being argued here.

This tunnel floods, and closes without warning for stormwater work. If you find it shut, walk up to the edge of the yard — the view along the tracks explains why the road had to go under. And whatever you did see in here, take the picture. It won't be there long.
