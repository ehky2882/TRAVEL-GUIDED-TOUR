The gold on the dome above you arrived by mule wagon. Twice.

You should be on Washington Street at the Capitol's west front — a long limestone facade behind a portico of columns, a lawn of statues around you, and the gilded dome high above, topped by a copper figure holding a torch and a sword.

Begin with the price tag. When Georgia budgeted a million dollars for a new capitol in eighteen eighty-three, the builders finished the job in eighteen eighty-nine and handed back the change — the winning bid came in at eight hundred sixty-two thousand, seven hundred fifty-six dollars and seventy-five cents. A government building delivered under budget is its own kind of monument.

The walls tell you what the state could and couldn't afford. The outside is Indiana limestone — imported, because it was cheaper. The Georgia marble the state was proud of went indoors, and the interior marble alone cost more than all the limestone wrapping the entire exterior.

Now the gold. In nineteen fifty-eight the town of duh lon uh guh, up in the north Georgia mountains where an early American gold rush ran, decided the dome ought to shine and collected forty-three ounces from its own hills. Seven covered wagons hauled the gold to Atlanta over three days — mules, children, a dog, and two state patrol cars bringing up the rear. The leaf went on in winter, and winter won: the gilding never properly stuck, and within twenty years half of it had weathered away. So in nineteen seventy-nine duh lon uh guh did the whole thing again — another wagon train, sixty ounces this time — and the coat applied in nineteen eighty-one is the one catching the light above you.

Statehouses usually wear their gold as a boast. This one wears a favor from a small mountain town, done twice because the first try peeled. The roof on Georgia's postcards is not a story about wealth — it is a story about neighbors who redid the job by mule until it held.

Before you go, find the newest figure on the grounds: a statue of Martin Luther King Junior, added in twenty seventeen, a short walk from where he was born and preached. The lawn has been argued over for generations, and it keeps changing — which may be the most honest thing about it.

Take one more look up. Somewhere in that shine is the patience of forty-three ounces that didn't stick, and sixty that did.
