They called it the Curb Market because the Black vendors sold at the curb.

That's the whole name. It isn't a nickname or a piece of local color; it's a plain description of an arrangement, and once you know it you can't hear the phrase any other way.

Stand on Edgewood Avenue with the long brick front of the building across from you. Low, wide, more warehouse than civic monument, big square-headed openings, flat roofline. It looks slightly unfinished at the ends, and it is — two towers once bookended the building and they're gone.

Walk the length of the building first, then come back to the middle.

The ground under it was cleared by fire. In 1917 a fire burned through the Old Fourth Ward and put a great many people out of their homes, and in the aftermath, in 1918, a farmers' market opened here in a tent. It worked immediately. The Atlanta Woman's Club raised roughly three hundred thousand dollars for something permanent and fireproof, hired the architect A. Ten Eyck Brown, and the market opened on the first of May, 1924. Atlanta built a whole civic identity out of the fire of 1864. Out of this one, it built a grocery.

Then the arrangement. Inside the building, white merchants. Outside, along the curb, Black merchants — same market, same customers walking between them, one wall deciding who rented a stall and who stood in the weather. Black shoppers could buy on either side of that wall. Black sellers could not cross it.

That is the shape of this whole street. Auburn Avenue is a second version of a city, built on the outside of the first one, by people who were told the first one was full.

In 1973 the city declined to renew the market's lease, and a Save the Market campaign talked it back open.

[beat]

In 2018, for its hundredth birthday, the building went back to its original name: the Municipal Market. Some people use it. Plenty of people still say Curb Market and mean it as an honor. The two names sit on the same building without agreeing, and that argument isn't settled, and probably shouldn't be.
