In 1947, on the holiest day of the Jewish year, the rabbi of this congregation stood up in front of it and said segregation was wrong. Eleven years later, somebody bombed the building.

You're on Peachtree Street, north of the arts center, in front of a wide limestone temple set back behind a lawn — columns across the front, a broad flat face, the whole thing pale and horizontal where everything near it is vertical. Philip Trammell Shutze designed it; it opened in 1931.

Read the front of it.

The congregation is much older than the building. The Hebrew Benevolent Congregation was organized in 1867, which makes it Atlanta's first Jewish institution, and by the early twentieth century it was prosperous, established, and extremely careful. It had reason to be. In 1913 a member of this congregation, Leo Frank, was convicted of the murder of thirteen-year-old Mary Phagan in a trial saturated with antisemitism, and in 1915, after his sentence was commuted, he was taken from custody and lynched. Georgia issued a pardon in 1986 — a procedural one, for the state's failure to protect him. It was not a finding that he was innocent.

After that, this congregation kept its head down for a generation. Then Jacob Rothschild took the pulpit, and didn't.

He preached against segregation from 1947 onward. After Little Rock, he helped write the Ministers' Manifesto, which asked white Atlanta clergy for communication between the races and obedience to the law. On the twelfth of October, 1958, the Temple was bombed. Nobody was inside. Five men were arrested; one was tried twice and acquitted, and no one was ever convicted of it.

[beat]

The response came fast and it came from everywhere — the mayor, business leaders, churches, and a great many people who had never once been inside this building. Other congregations offered their own buildings to a congregation that suddenly didn't have one. Ralph McGill wrote a front-page editorial about it the same morning, and won a Pulitzer the following year. Rothschild kept this pulpit for another fifteen years. What you're standing in front of is a congregation that learned precisely what visibility costs, and then chose it a second time.
