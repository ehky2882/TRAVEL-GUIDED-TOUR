Almost everything built here was designed to be thrown away.

In 1895 Piedmont Park was a world's fair. The Cotton States and International Exposition ran for a hundred days and drew something like eight hundred thousand people, and nearly every hall put up for it — Georgia pine with moss-green roofs, a Woman's Building, a Machinery Hall, a Ferris wheel on the midway — came down again afterward.

You want the path along the north edge of the lake, with the Midtown towers standing up behind the water. If you can see them reflected, you're in the right place.

Head for the water.

Lake Clara Meer isn't a lake. It's about twelve acres of hole that somebody dug for the fair, filled, and lit with a colored electric fountain — electricity being, in 1895, a thing worth buying a ticket to look at. The terraces, the granite steps and the shape of the ground around you are fair leftovers too. The city bought the grounds in 1904 and made them a public park, and the Olmsted firm was brought in about a decade later to shape what came next.

One building at that fair outlasted the rest in argument, if not in lumber. The Negro Building was the first hall at a major American exposition given over entirely to Black exhibits — built by Black contractors, filled with work from Atlanta University, Spelman, Morehouse, Howard and Tuskegee. And on the fair's opening day, Booker T. Washington gave the speech that made him nationally famous, arguing that Black advancement should come through work and skill rather than through demands for social equality.

People have been arguing about that speech ever since. W.E.B. Du Bois, who moved to Atlanta two years later, came to read it as an acceptance of inferiority. Others have read it as the only sayable thing, in that room, in that year, to that audience. The argument has never closed, and this ground is where it opened.

[beat]

The fair lasted a hundred days. The lake is still here. The argument is still here. The building the argument started in is gone — which is a fairly Atlanta set of things to survive.
