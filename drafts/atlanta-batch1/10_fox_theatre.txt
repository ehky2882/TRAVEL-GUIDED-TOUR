Nobody built this to be a theater. The Shriners built it to be a Shriners' temple, and then they ran out of money.

Stand on the west sidewalk of Peachtree, at Ponce de Leon, with the whole front of the building across from you: a city block of buff and cream striping, arched doorways, onion domes and minarets, with a marquee bolted onto the front of it.

Take the minarets first.

The Yaarab Temple Shriners had five thousand members and an appetite for a headquarters, and in the late twenties they commissioned this at a price that ran to about two and three-quarter million dollars — which was more than they had. So they leased the auditorium to a movie man named William Fox and stayed on as tenants in their own building, paying rent here until 1949. Olivier Vinour, of Marye, Alger and Vinour, drew it. It opened on Christmas Day, 1929, two months after the stock market crashed, and by 1932 it had gone bankrupt.

Inside — and you should get inside, on a tour or for a show — the auditorium is built as a Moorish courtyard under an open sky: false parapets, a canvas awning, and a ceiling of small bulbs shining through crystals to make stars. Roughly forty-six hundred seats, and an organ with more than three thousand pipes that can imitate a locomotive whistle.

Now the part that matters. In 1974, Southern Bell offered to buy the Fox and take it down for a parking deck.

[beat]

What happened next is most of the reason Atlanta has a preservation movement at all. A campaign called Save the Fox went public and got loud — benefit concerts, Liberace on one bill and Lynyrd Skynyrd on another, a fundraising drive that reached ordinary people who had never bought a ticket here. The city refused to issue the demolition permit. A deal got cut: the phone company put its tower up next door instead, a nonprofit bought the theater in 1975, and the mortgage was clear by 1978. This city has taken down a great deal of itself, and mostly nobody stopped it. Here, once, somebody did — and this is what winning looks like.
