The bronze figure on the lawn is not Martin Luther King Jr. People assume it is, take the photo, and walk on.

Stand so you have the statue in front of you and the church behind it, both in one view. The sculpture is a man holding an infant straight up over his head, arms locked, the child's body small against the sky. It's called Behold. Patrick Morelli made it, and Coretta Scott King unveiled it on the eleventh of January, 1990.

So.

The gesture comes from an African naming ritual — a father lifting a newborn toward the sky and presenting the child to creation. Morelli built the piece around that moment instead of around a portrait. Which means the monument to a man whose face is known everywhere contains no likeness of him at all. It's someone you don't know, holding someone you don't know, in a posture about beginnings.

Now the church. Ebenezer's congregation dates to 1886, and this sanctuary — the one the Park Service calls the Heritage Sanctuary — was finished in 1922, built in stages while the congregation worshipped in a storefront and paid for the walls as they went up. King's grandfather preached from that pulpit. Then his father. Then, for eight years, he did, as co-pastor alongside him. The Park Service restored the room to the way it looked in the nineteen sixties, down to the woodwork and the light fixtures. It's open daily, and it is still a room people worship in — if a service is happening, this stop can wait.

Here is what the block does to the story. The house where he was born is up the street. This pulpit is here. His tomb is across the road. Those three points sit inside a five-minute walk.

[beat]

Most monumental lives spread out — a birthplace in one state, a career in another, a grave somewhere symbolic. This one folded back on itself. And the statue out front, refusing to be a portrait, is oddly honest about that. What's being marked here isn't one man's face. It's the block that produced him, and got him back.
