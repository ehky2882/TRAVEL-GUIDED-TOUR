ATLANTA — STOP 30
ATLANTA STUDENT MOVEMENT BOULEVARD / CLARK ATLANTA
Clean version

This street has carried the name Atlanta Student Movement Boulevard since 2010. What it's named for happened in 1960 — fifty years between the event and the sign.

Stand at the corner of Atlanta Student Movement Boulevard and Chestnut Street, the Clark Atlanta campus behind you. There's a Georgia Historical Society marker at the curb, dark metal with gold lettering.

Don't read the marker yet.

This corner had a drugstore — Yates and Milton, Black-owned, a meeting room with a soda fountain. In February of 1960, three Morehouse students in it — Lonnie King, Joseph Pierce and Julian Bond — worked out how to reach every campus at once.

What came out of that first wasn't a protest. It was a purchase. On the ninth of March they bought a full page in the city's daily papers and ran a document called An Appeal for Human Rights, drafted mostly by a Spelman senior named Roslyn Pope, which set out what the state was denying them, point by point, and made clear they weren't going to wait. Six days later, some two hundred students sat down at eleven eating places downtown; seventy-seven were arrested. The lunch counters were desegregated the next year.

Here's the part you can only see from this corner. Six institutions sit within a few blocks of it, and they sit here because the rest of the city was closed to them. Nobody planned that density as an organizing tool. But it meant a decision made in a drugstore on a Tuesday could reach every Black college student in Atlanta by Thursday, on foot.

The older argument was still running on these campuses the whole time — whether to build your own institutions or force the doors of everyone else's. It had been going since the 1890s and it never closed. These students didn't settle it; they stopped waiting for it to be settled.

Two years after the Appeal a new mayor took office. On his first day, Ivan Allen Junior had the signs reading white and colored taken down at City Hall. That December, in 1962, his administration put a barricade across Peyton Road to stop Black families buying houses past it. A court ordered it down seventy-two days later.

*[beat]*

Now read the marker. Then watch the crosswalk: students cross between campuses all day, the same few blocks somebody walked in 1960 to get a room full of people to agree.
