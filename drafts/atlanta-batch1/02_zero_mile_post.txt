The stone in front of you is a stand-in. The real one left town in 2018 — and the argument about whether it should have is half the reason to come down here.

You should be under the Central Avenue bridge deck near Wall Street, a level below the downtown you walked in from, with rail lines running past in the dark and an interpretive panel beside a squat stone post. It is dim down here, and quieter than the streets above, which is fitting — you are standing on the original surface of Atlanta.

Look at the stone first. The original was a surveyor's marker set to fix the end of a state railroad, carved with a zero on one face and one hundred thirty-eight on another — zero miles from here, one hundred thirty-eight to Chattanooga. Some American cities began as forts, some as missions, some as harbors. This one began as an arithmetic problem, and the answer was a stake in the ground. When the settlement that gathered around it needed a name, it tried a few before landing on Atlanta.

Now look around, because the floor you're on is the real exhibit. These shadowed lanes were open-air streets until the 1920s, when the city, tired of waiting at rail crossings, bridged over the whole district and lifted its traffic one story into the air. Ground floors became basements, merchants moved their doors up a level, and the original street level was simply left behind — the layer people mean when they say underground Atlanta. Nothing was ever buried. The city just moved upstairs.

As for the stone itself: the original stands in a museum eight miles north, safe indoors beside a Civil War locomotive. Preservationists will tell you it would not have survived down here; others answer that a zero mile post has exactly one job, which is to stand at mile zero. Both sides have a point, and the replica in front of you — set by survey on the precise original spot — is the city's way of splitting the difference.

[beat]

Before you head back up to the daylight, stand by the old grade a moment. Every address, every mile marker, every skyline photograph of this city measures out from a stone that is no longer here — and somehow the spot still holds.
