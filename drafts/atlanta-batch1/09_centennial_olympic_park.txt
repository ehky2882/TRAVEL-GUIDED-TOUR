It's the summer of 1996. The ground you're standing on is not a park — it's a tangle of aging warehouses and vacant lots that most of the city walks around, not through. Then Atlanta talks its way into hosting the Olympic Games, and decides mid-preparation that what the Games really need is a front yard.

You should be at the Fountain of Rings, where jets of water trace the five-ring emblem flush with the plaza floor — children usually mark the spot better than any sign could. The plaza around you is the park the Games left behind, ringed now by museums and towers.

Walk the eastern edge and you'll find five square plazas paved like quilt squares — the Quilt Plazas, each stitched to a different memory of that summer. One holds the story of the bid itself: a local lawyer named Billy Payne talked the world into bringing the Games to Atlanta, an idea his own city initially found improbable. Another, the Quilt of Remembrance, is the hard one — it keeps a light burning for Alice Hawthorne, killed when a bomb went off in this park on a July night in 1996, a night remembered now alongside Richard Jewell, the security guard wrongly suspected in public after he had in fact spotted the bag and moved people away from it.

The park's other legacy is quieter and bigger. To host the world, Atlanta remade whole blocks — and the remaking had an address book. Techwood Homes, the first public housing built in the United States, stood a short walk north of here; it became the Olympic Village, and afterward a mixed-income neighborhood where only seventy-eight of the original residents ever returned. Every host city buys its renovation with somebody's move, and Atlanta signed that deal in plain sight.

So the honest reading of this park is both things at once. The gamble worked — though on a thirty-year delay, since almost everything gleaming around you arrived a decade or more after the flame left. And the bill was real, and specific, and mostly paid by people who never saw a closing ceremony.

[beat]

Stand by the rings a moment. When the fountain runs, the water rises and falls exactly where the world once stood — and the children playing in it now are the one renovation nobody argues about.
