A beauty shop on the first floor, a radio station above it, and above that an office with no window in it — same building, same decade, one address.

You're looking at the Prince Hall muh sonn ik Temple — a tall brick block on Auburn Avenue, stone trim, a muh sonn ik emblem cut above the entrance. It went up between nineteen thirty-seven and nineteen forty, built by Georgia's Black Masons under the leadership of John Wesley Dobbs, whose name is on the street one block north.

Count the floors.

The beauty shop was a Madam see jay Walker salon — the national brand, franchised, Black women's money moving through Black women's businesses. Above it was dubble yoo ee ar dee, which at the end of the nineteen forties became the first radio station in the country owned by an African American, after a professor and bank president named Jesse blay tun bought it and replaced the entire staff. And higher up, from nineteen fifty-seven, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference kept its headquarters, with Martin Luther King junior working out of a small interior office for years.

So: cosmetics, broadcasting, a fraternal order, and a civil rights organization, stacked vertically, paying rent to one another. That isn't a charming coincidence. A movement needs a landlord who will rent to it, a printer who will print for it, and a station that will put it on the air — and on this street all three existed, and two of them were in this building.

It nearly didn't survive. The temple sat empty and deteriorating for years; one preservation group called it among the most significant unprotected buildings in the region. Restoration finished in early twenty twenty-six, and on the eleventh of February, twenty twenty-six, it was formally brought into the Martin Luther King junior National Historical Park. Public programming began that spring. It is the newest piece of the park and the schedule is still settling, so check before you count on getting inside.
