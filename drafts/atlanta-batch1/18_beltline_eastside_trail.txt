Notice what your legs aren't doing.

Be on the Eastside Trail itself, anywhere between Ponce de Leon Avenue and North Avenue, with the cut walls rising on either side and street bridges crossing overhead. Look up at one, then back down at the ground you're on. The streets go up and down. This doesn't.

Everything here used to be one thing.

That flatness isn't landscaping. It's a railroad grade. A loaded freight train can climb almost nothing, so the men who cut this line through the city in the late 1800s took the hills out, and the trail inherited the result. You are walking on somebody's decision about torque.

Which is, more or less, Atlanta's biography. No river worth the name, no harbor, no fort — the place began as a mark on a map where a railroad was meant to end, and has taken its shape from track ever since. Four freight lines, built by four companies for four unrelated reasons, happened to trace a rough ring around the city center. Nobody planned the ring. It was a leftover.

In 1999 a Georgia Tech graduate student named Ryan Gravel wrote a master's thesis proposing the leftover become a loop of transit and parks — twenty-two miles of it — then mailed copies to people who could do something about it. The piece you're standing on, the Eastside Trail, opened in 2012. Close to seventeen of those twenty-two miles are continuous now, with the rest scheduled by 2030.

So Atlanta's newest public space is, for something like the fourth time, a railroad.

One thing to carry with you, because it follows this corridor the whole way around. Atlanta BeltLine Incorporated counts four thousand four hundred and twenty-five affordable homes built or preserved against a goal of five thousand six hundred by 2030. Dan Immergluck, who studies housing at Georgia State, counts something different: prices along the corridor rose faster than prices away from it, and they started rising years before most of those homes existed. Both counts are accurate, and what they add up to is still being argued here.

Keep going whichever way you were headed. The grade stays flat, the walls drop away, and somewhere in the next mile you'll pass over a street you've driven a hundred times and never once seen from above. That's the trade the railroad made with this city, and you've inherited a piece of it.
