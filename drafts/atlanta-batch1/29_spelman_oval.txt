ATLANTA — STOP 29
SPELMAN COLLEGE OVAL
Clean version

The school here opened in 1881. Emancipation was sixteen years earlier. Do the arithmetic, because it tells you who walked in on day one: most of the first students had been born enslaved, and a number of them were grown women who had come to learn to read.

What you want is the Oval, the long green at the center of the Spelman campus, with the older brick buildings around the edge. If the campus is open to visitors, come in from the gate on Spelman Lane and stop where the lawn opens out. If it isn't, the gate does most of this stop's work on its own.

Ignore the newest buildings for a minute.

It began in the basement of Friendship Baptist Church, downtown, run by two women from Massachusetts — Sophia Packard and Harriet Giles — on money collected a few dollars at a time. In 1882 they were introduced to John D. Rockefeller, who gave them two hundred and fifty dollars on the spot, and far more later. Two years after that, the school took a new name in honor of his wife's family. Packard and Giles got buildings on this lawn. The Rockefellers got the name over the whole institution.

Rockefeller Hall was dedicated in 1886, the same year the school began training nurses. Fifteen years later it dedicated a hospital — MacVicar, on the campus. A women's college with a hospital on its own quad, because Black nurses had almost nowhere in this city to train, and Black patients had little care that wasn't segregated, scarce, or both.

The first two college degrees came in 1901, to Jane Anna Granderson and Claudia White. Twenty years, church basement to bachelor's degree.

That stack of dates is describing something specific. When the institutions around you are closed, you don't get to specialize. This school trained teachers, and then had to supply the schools that hired them. It trained nurses, and then had to build the hospital they could train in. Every gap in the city outside showed up on this campus as another building. The Oval isn't a quad in the decorative sense — it's an inventory of what the city declined to provide.

*[beat]*

It's also just a lawn that works: people cutting across it to class, somebody asleep under a tree. Ordinary use of a place that had to be argued into existence, which is what everyone involved was after.
