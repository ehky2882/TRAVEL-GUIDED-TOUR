Segment: 04 of 45 — Woodruff Park and the Phoenix
Position: the south end of the park at Peachtree and Edgewood, at the statue

Look at the bird.

You should be at the south tip of Woodruff Park, where Peachtree meets Edgewood, at a bronze of a woman held aloft by a phoenix — wings spread beneath her, her arms thrown back. The sculpture is called Atlanta from the Ashes, and the streetcar rattles past on one side while the lawn opens north on the other.

The phoenix is official. Atlanta's city seal shows the same bird over a single Latin word, Resurgens — rising again — because this city has burned more than once: in 1864, in war, and again in 1917, by accident, when a fire nobody ever explained took block after block of the east side. Only one of those fires made it onto the seal. Myths get built on the fire you can name an enemy for.

Now the statue's own story, because it is very Atlanta. It was commissioned in the 1960s not by the city but by a department store's foundation — Rich's, the great local emporium — cast in Italy, and dedicated in 1969. It stood near the store for years, and only moved to this corner in 1995, just in time for the Olympics.

And the park it landed in has the same plot. In 1971 a buyer quietly assembled these blocks of expensive downtown land and gave them to the city as a park — anonymously. The buyer turned out to be Robert Woodruff, the man who ran Coca-Cola for decades, and the city only renamed the place for him in 1985, once the secret was out. For fourteen years, Atlantans ate lunch on grass a very rich man had bought them without leaving a name.

So read this corner honestly: a civic myth commissioned by a store, a commons donated in secret by a soda executive. Public spirit in this town has always worn private clothes — and the point isn't the money, it's the modesty. Whatever else you make of it, the bird and the lawn were both gifts.

[beat]

The park has been Atlanta's arguing ground for decades — rallies, marches, encampments, lunch breaks. Sit a minute and watch it work. A city rising again, over and over, is mostly just this: people coming back to the same green patch and starting over.
