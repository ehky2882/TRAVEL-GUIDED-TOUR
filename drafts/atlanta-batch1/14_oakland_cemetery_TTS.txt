ATLANTA — STOP 14
OAKLAND CEMETERY, MEMORIAL DRIVE GATE
TTS-safe version

Twenty-seven of this city's mayors are buried behind this wall. So are six governors of Georgia, and a fair number of the people Atlanta named things after.

Stand at the Memorial Drive gate, with the brick wall running off in both directions and the downtown towers over your shoulder. The wall and the gates date to eighteen ninety-six. The tower just inside, with the steep roof, went up three years later and was the sexton's house before it was an office.

Give this a minute.

Oakland opened in eighteen fifty as six acres called Atlanta Cemetery and grew to forty-eight. Something like seventy thousand people are in here. By eighteen eighty-four every ordinary plot had been sold, which is why the city stopped burying most of its dead here well over a century ago. It was laid out as a garden cemetery, meant to be walked in, at a time when Atlanta had almost nothing else of the kind.

Inside, toward the south, are the African American Grounds. People were buried there under segregation, in a section set apart, and many of the markers were wood, so many of the graves are unmarked today. Carrie Steele Logan is in that ground. She founded Georgia's first orphanage for Black children, and then ran it.

Then there's what isn't here. From eighteen ninety-four until a few years ago, a marble lion lay on the grass in the Confederate section, carved by a sculptor named Brady for the Ladies Memorial Association, marking the graves of unknown Confederate dead. After repeated vandalism, the city removed it in August of twenty twenty-one. One argument held that a marker over unknown graves belongs with the graves. Another held that a Confederate monument in a public cemetery honors a cause rather than the men under it. The city took it out; it has not been on public display since; the ground where it lay is sod. The sixty-five-foot obelisk a short walk away, raised in eighteen seventy-four, is still standing.

That's the city's habit, and once you've noticed it you'll see it everywhere. Atlanta rarely keeps a thing where it is and rarely destroys it outright. It moves it — and sometimes doesn't say where.

The gate is open through the day and you can walk in. People run in here, which sounds wrong for about ten seconds, until you're inside and realize it's the largest quiet ground for a mile in any direction.
