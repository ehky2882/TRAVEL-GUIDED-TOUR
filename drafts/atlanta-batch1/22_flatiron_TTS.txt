The building in front of you is eleven stories of triangle, and at the sharp end there is barely room for a desk.

You should be where Peachtree Street meets Broad, looking at a narrow stone wedge that splits the two streets like a ship's bow. Step back far enough and you can see both long sides at once, tapering to the prow.

The shape is not a flourish — it's a scar. Downtown Atlanta is what happens when a railroad's curving corridors and a farm town's wagon roads refuse to agree, and where the two systems collide they leave lots like this one: triangular leftovers that only an ambitious architect would touch. In the late eighteen nineties one did. Bradford Gilbert, a New Yorker who specialized in early steel-frame towers, raised eleven stories on this scrap of land and called it the English-American Building.

Hold the date loosely and the sequence firmly. New York's famous Flatiron — the one on the postcards, the one this building forever gets compared to — went up in nineteen oh two. Atlanta's was already standing, watching. New York got the name and the fame; Atlanta quietly got there first.

But the better story is survival. This is the oldest skyscraper Atlanta has left — and in this particular city, age is not a passive achievement. Atlanta clears its own landmarks the way other cities dust theirs, and nearly everything downtown from this building's generation is gone, replaced once or twice over. A tower from the eighteen nineties still standing on Peachtree Street is not a leftover; it is a survivor of a demolition habit that would have taken it at almost any point in the last century, if the wrong owner had held it at the wrong time.

So give the little wedge its due. It watched the buildings around it come down and the towers behind you go up; it has outlasted the fame it never got. And the crooked lot lines that made it — the railroad arguing with the wagon roads — are still down there under the asphalt, still refusing to agree, still deciding the shape of everything built on top of them.
