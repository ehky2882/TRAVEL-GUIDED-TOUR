Inside — and you should get inside, on a tour or for a show — the auditorium is built as a Moorish courtyard under an open sky: false parapets, a canvas awning, and a ceiling of small bulbs shining through crystals to make stars. Roughly forty-six hundred seats, and an organ with more than three thousand pipes that can imitate a locomotive whistle.

Now the part that matters. In nineteen seventy-four, Southern Bell offered to buy the Fox and take it down for a parking deck.

What happened next is most of the reason Atlanta has a preservation movement at all. A campaign called Save the Fox went public and got loud — benefit concerts, lib uh rah chee on one bill and lenerd skinerd on another, a fundraising drive that reached ordinary people who had never bought a ticket here. The city refused to issue the demolition permit. A deal got cut: the phone company put its tower up next door instead, a nonprofit bought the theater in nineteen seventy-five, and the mortgage was clear by nineteen seventy-eight. This city has taken down a great deal of itself, and mostly nobody stopped it. Here, once, somebody did — and this is what winning looks like.
