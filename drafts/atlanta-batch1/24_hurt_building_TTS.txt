Thirty feet of this building is missing, and the missing part is the best thing about it.

You should be at the sharp end of a great stone triangle just east of Five Points, where Edgewood Avenue splits from Exchange Place. Look at the point of the wedge: instead of running out to a knife edge, the tower stops thirty feet short, and the corner opens into a curved, columned entrance with air and daylight around it.

The missing footage was a choice, and the man who made it is a name worth keeping: Joel Hurt. An engineer and developer, Hurt had already given the city its first skyscraper — the Equitable Building of eighteen ninety-two, eight stories, the building that taught Atlanta to look up. When he came back to build his masterpiece here in nineteen thirteen, he owned a prime triangle of downtown land, every square foot of it rentable — and he sliced thirty feet off the sharpest, most visible corner and gave it back to the street, so the intersection could breathe and his tower could meet Five Points with a rounded classical bow instead of a blade. Behind those curved columns sits a domed rotunda three stories high, finished with its original chandelier — a hidden formal heart placed exactly where the building gave up its most valuable ground.

Rentable space traded away for civic manners: in any city it would be rare, and in this one it borders on scandalous. Developers here have mostly treated the street as whatever is left over once the floor plate is drawn. Hurt worked the equation backwards, and the proof has stood for over a century.

As for his first tower, the Equitable — the building that started Atlanta's whole skyward habit — the city knocked it down in nineteen seventy-one. The father of the Atlanta skyscraper survives only in his second draft, the one whose best feature is the part he chose not to build.

So stand right at the apex, in the thirty feet that isn't there. You're occupying the gift — a small permanent donation of downtown air, made by a man whose other landmark is gone. In Atlanta, what a builder declines to take can outlast everything he builds.
