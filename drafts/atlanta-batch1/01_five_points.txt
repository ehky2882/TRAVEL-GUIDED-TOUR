Segment: 01 of 45 — Five Points
Position: the plaza where Peachtree, Marietta, Decatur and Edgewood meet
---
This is Five Points. Every street address in Atlanta — every house number on every mailbox in the city — is measured outward from the ground under your feet.

You should be standing in the open plaza where Peachtree Street crosses Marietta, with Edgewood and Decatur Street angling in behind you. Around the plaza, construction fencing wraps the rail station, and there may be crews working above it — the station's concrete canopy is being taken down piece by piece, a job that runs for years, so expect noise and expect the view to keep changing.

Start with the streets themselves. They refuse to meet squarely, and nothing downtown does — the whole grid sits about thirty degrees off true north, because these streets were never drawn on paper first. They followed the rail lines, and the rail lines followed the land, which is why North Peachtree famously points somewhere other than north. And the pattern is older than the rails: two Muscogee footpaths crossed near this spot, the Peachtree Trail and the Sandtown Trail, long before anyone here spoke English.

One more layer down: in 1875 the city sank three wells into this intersection and piped its first public drinking water out through hollowed wooden logs. Atlanta's original waterworks are still somewhere beneath the asphalt you're standing on.

Most American cities keep something ceremonial at their center — a courthouse square, a cathedral, a green. Atlanta keeps a junction. This was never a place that happened to grow a crossroads; it was a crossroads that grew a place, chosen by railroad surveyors because lines could meet here, and everything else — the towers, the streetcar, the name on the map — came afterward.

[beat]

So the demolition around you is not an interruption. It is closer to a tradition: Atlanta has been rebuilding its own front door for as long as it has had one, and not everyone wanted the old concrete roof gone — some people admired it, and said so. The city listened, and took it down anyway.

Stay a moment before you move on. Underneath the fencing, the paving, the wells and the rails, two footpaths are still quietly doing what they have always done here — deciding where everything else goes.
