Segment: 23 of 45 — the Candler Building
Position: 127 Peachtree Street, at the marble entrance
---
Walk up close to the marble — close enough to see the faces.

You should be at the base of a white seventeen-story tower on Peachtree Street, its lower floors carved like a jewelry box: vines, figures, medallions, and portrait heads worked into north Georgia marble. Take your time with the portraits, because some of these faces are the builder's own family.

The builder was Asa Candler, and the money was Coca-Cola. Candler had bought the formula for a headache tonic and turned it into a soft-drink empire sold for a nickel a glass, and when he wanted a monument in 1906, he didn't build a bottling plant. He built the tallest tower the city had yet seen, wrapped its base in marble hauled down from the Amicalola hills, and hired a sculptor named F.B. Miles to spend years carving it — science, art, industry, and, in among the allegories, his own parents rendered in stone. A family album, published in marble, on the city's main street.

Inside went Candler's own bank — the Central Bank and Trust, holding the fortune the tower advertised — along with a grand marble staircase, chandeliers, a pair of Tiffany glass windows, even baths down below — a private economy under one roof: the fortune, the bank that held it, and the family faces guarding the door. A century later the tower became a hotel, the staircase and the Tiffany glass still in place, and the carvings simply switched from advertising a fortune to advertising room rates.

Look closest at how unnecessary all of it is. A steel frame doesn't need vines or portrait medallions; every flourish on this facade is pure announcement. Atlanta's first great fortune wasn't old money and didn't pretend to be — it was syrup and nickels, brand new, and it bought itself the oldest trick in architecture: permanence by decoration. The nickels are long spent. The announcement is still running.

[beat]

Before you walk on, pick one face and really look at it. Somebody's mother, somebody's father, carved five stories above a street that has torn down nearly everything else from their lifetime — still here, still watching Peachtree, still part of the pitch.
