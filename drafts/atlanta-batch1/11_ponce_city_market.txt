For fifty years, nothing that arrived at this building was meant to stay in it.

Stand on the north side of Ponce de Leon Avenue, on the sidewalk, far enough back to take in the whole face of it: brick running most of a city block, a tower at the western end, arched windows at street level, the sign up on the roof. Behind it, out of sight, the BeltLine crosses.

Two things about the front of it.

First, the size is not decoration. When this opened in 1926 it was a Sears, Roebuck mail-order house — about two million square feet of floor, most of it a machine for filling catalog orders across the Southeast. The store on Ponce was the small part. Everything else in here was headed out the back, in a box, to somebody's farm.

Second, what came after. Sears closed the store in 1979 and was gone by 1987. In 1990 the city bought it for twelve million dollars, moved police and fire offices into one corner, and called the whole thing City Hall East. For twenty years a building this size sat mostly as empty rooms with a municipal parking permit taped to the glass. The city sold it in 2011 for twenty-seven million, and the market opened in 2014.

The building itself never changed. What changed is the direction it faces. It was built to send things away from Atlanta, it now exists to bring Atlanta to it, and it turned a hundred years old this month without altering a brick to manage the switch.

One thing to carry with you, because it follows this corridor the whole way around. Atlanta BeltLine Incorporated counts four thousand four hundred and twenty-five affordable homes built or preserved against a goal of five thousand six hundred by 2030. Dan Immergluck, who studies housing at Georgia State, counts something different: prices along the corridor rose faster than prices away from it, and they started rising years before most of those homes existed. Both counts are accurate, and what they add up to is still being argued here.

Go in when you're ready; the food hall is the obvious move, and a good one. But stay out here a minute first. A hundred years of this building's life happened before anybody thought to invite the public in, and the wall in front of you is the same wall.
