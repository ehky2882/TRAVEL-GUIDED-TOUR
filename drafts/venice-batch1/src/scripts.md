So Luigi made a plan. He put books in bathtubs, in old rowing boats and in a full-size gondola that now sits in the middle of the shop. When the water rose, the boats would float, and the books would float with them.

The gift detail is out the back. Old encyclopedias, spoiled by the water and no use to anyone, were stacked up into a staircase. Climb it and you can look out over the canal behind. Luigi admitted he'd never imagined that what he saw as a simple practical solution would become so famous. And keep an eye out for the cats. The shop has long had a few, once strays and now residents.

Here's the thing, though. The floods are real. In November twenty nineteen, during the worst high water in Venice since nineteen sixty-six, the shop lost hundreds of books. The bathtubs look like a joke. They are actually how a bookshop survives in this city.

[beat]

Go early or late, when it is quieter, and spend a few minutes on the real books rather than the props. Then walk back to Campo Santa Maria Formosa. Carlo Scarpa's work at the Querini Stampalia, just across the canal from the square, takes on exactly the same water, in a very different way.

=== 19 | fondazione-querini-stampalia | Visit | Fondazione Querini Stampalia | 45.436458 | 12.341035 | Fondazione Querini Stampalia
Most buildings in Venice spend their lives trying to keep the water out. Carlo Scarpa let it in.

Campiello Querini, just off Campo Santa Maria Formosa in Castello. Stand on the little square and face the canal. Across it, a slim bridge of iron and timber leads to a side door of the palazzo. That bridge is your first piece of Scarpa.

The foundation goes back to eighteen sixty-nine. Count Giovanni Querini Stampalia, the last of his family, left the palazzo, its library and its collections to the city. Nearly a hundred years later, between nineteen sixty-one and nineteen sixty-three, the Venetian architect Carlo Scarpa was asked to rework the ground floor and the garden. They were constantly being flooded.

Have a look at the bridge first. It starts with two big blocks of white Istrian stone as its first steps, then turns into iron and planks of larch. It sits right next to an older bridge built the traditional way, so you can compare the two side by side.

Inside, Scarpa did something no one else would have dared. He opened the old water gates to the canal and let the tide come in, along channels and steps cut beside the walls. His reasoning was simple: the high water would be inside, as it is in the rest of the city. Rather than fight it, he gave it a place to go and made it beautiful.

Then the garden. Water runs the length of it in a narrow channel, past little maze-like carvings in alabaster and stone, into a basin of copper and mosaic. It is a tiny space, and every joint in it has been thought about.

[beat]

This is the place I'd send anyone who wants to understand modern architecture in Venice. It doesn't pretend the city isn't sinking. It accepts that, and it makes something rather lovely of it.

Go in, buy a coffee in the café, and sit by the garden. Then, if you're curious, walk ten minutes to Piazza San Marco and find Scarpa's other small masterpiece, the Olivetti showroom, under the arcades.

=== 20 | negozio-olivetti | Visit | Negozio Olivetti | 45.434153 | 12.337532 | Negozio Olivetti

He wanted, as he put it, a calling card in the most beautiful square in the world. Scarpa finished it in nineteen fifty-eight.

Now have a look at that staircase. Its steps are slabs of pale Aurisina stone, staggered, each one appearing to hang in the air. It is the heart of the room, and the reason you'll remember it.

What's worth knowing is that Scarpa never treated anything as minor. A door handle, a light fitting or the edge of a step each got as much thought as the whole. Even the door is a slab of stone, made to disappear into the wall.

[beat]

The shop is cared for by FAI, the Italian national trust, and it is open to visitors. Inside are typewriters and calculating machines from the nineteen fifties and sixties. === 21 | punta-della-dogana | Visit | Punta della Dogana | 45.430740 | 12.336287 | Punta della Dogana
For three centuries, every ship entering Venice had to declare its cargo here. These days, the cargo is contemporary art.

You're at the very tip of Dorsoduro, where the Grand Canal meets the Giudecca Canal. The great dome of the Salute is behind you. San Marco is across the water to your left, and San Giorgio Maggiore ahead and to your right. The long, low building that forms this point is the old sea customs house, the Dogana da Mar.

It was designed by Giuseppe Benoni and finished in sixteen eighty-two. Look up at the tower on the point. Two kneeling figures hold up a golden globe, and on the globe stands a figure of Fortune. She turns with the wind, a weathervane telling sailors which way the luck was blowing.

The customs house was closed in the nineteen eighties and fell into decay. The city then held a competition to bring it back to life, and in two thousand and seven it went to François Pinault, the French collector. He asked the Japanese architect Tadao Ando to restore it, and it opened as a museum of contemporary art in two thousand and nine.

Ando cleaned up the old brick walls and the timber roof trusses, and largely left them alone. His one big gesture is at the centre: a cube of smooth, reinforced concrete set into a double-height room, in the heart of the old warehouses. A decade later he used the same idea at the Bourse de Commerce in Paris.

The interesting bit is the contrast. Seventeenth-century brick, rough and patched, against the most careful concrete in the world. Neither tries to look like the other, and that is why they get on.

[beat]

Before you go, walk right out to the point, below Fortune. The view takes in more of Venice at once than almost anywhere else on foot. Then turn and look up at the Salute. Its steps are one of the best places in the city to sit and watch the boats.

=== 22 | fondazione-giorgio-cini | Visit | Fondazione Giorgio Cini | 45.429283 | 12.342414 | Fondazione Giorgio Cini
This whole island was rescued by a father in memory of his son.

You're on San Giorgio Maggiore, the island directly across the water from San Marco. Stand on the quay with Palladio's white church to one side and the long monastery buildings behind it. The church is the part everyone sees from the Riva. The monastery, which most visitors never enter, is the Fondazione Giorgio Cini.

For centuries this was a powerful Benedictine monastery. Then came Napoleon. The monks were driven out, and the island was used by the military and left to decay for almost a hundred and fifty years.

In nineteen fifty-one, the industrialist Vittorio Cini set up a foundation to restore the whole complex. He named it after his son Giorgio, who had been killed in a plane crash in nineteen forty-nine. The idea was to bring the island back to life as a centre for scholarship and culture, and it has been one ever since.

Have a look at what was saved. There are two cloisters, one of them by Palladio. There is Palladio's refectory, the monks' great dining hall, for which Paolo Veronese painted his enormous Wedding at Cana. The original now hangs in the Louvre, a souvenir of Napoleon's visit. And there is a library and a grand staircase by Baldassare Longhena, the architect of the Salute.

What's worth knowing is that the foundation's spaces are visited only on guided tours. So the island stays quiet even when San Marco, five minutes across the water, is at its busiest.

[beat]

Book the guided tour if you can. If you have time afterwards, look for Le Stanze del Vetro, the foundation's glass gallery, also on the island. Then take the lift up the church bell tower next door for the best view in Venice.

=== 23 | fondazione-prada | Visit | Fondazione Prada | 45.440632 | 12.332005 | Fondazione Prada

What I find rather lovely is how much the building remembers. A queen, a bank for the poor, an archive, an art gallery. Venice rarely throws anything away. It just finds a new use for it.

=== 24 | giardini-della-biennale | Observe | Giardini della Biennale | 45.429020 | 12.357933 | Giardini della Biennale
Napoleon wanted Venice to have a public park. What it got, eventually, was the world's most famous art exhibition.

The entrance to the Giardini, at the eastern end of Castello, along the waterfront from San Marco. The trees and lawns in front of you are the only large public gardens in the historic city. Inside, scattered among them, are pavilions in every style of the last hundred and twenty years.

Napoleon laid out the gardens in the early nineteenth century, clearing monasteries to do it. In eighteen ninety-five the city held its first international art exhibition here, and it has been held every other year since. The first foreign country to build its own pavilion was Belgium, in nineteen oh seven. Today there are twenty-nine national pavilions in the gardens, each country's statement of what it wanted to look like.

Have a look for three of them. The Austrian pavilion, by Josef Hoffmann, from nineteen thirty-four. The little Venezuelan pavilion, by Carlo Scarpa, from the mid nineteen fifties: a small, careful study in light and concrete. And the Nordic pavilion, shared by Sweden, Norway and Finland, by Sverre Fehn, finished in nineteen sixty-two.

The gift detail is in Fehn's building. He didn't cut down the trees on his site. He built round them, and they grow up through his concrete roof. The roof itself is a lattice of thin concrete beams, which filters the light so it feels like daylight in a forest, even in Venice.

And here's the thing. Most architecture is fixed in one place and one century. Here, a single walk takes you from nineteen oh seven to the present, building by building. It is the closest thing the world has to a library of national architecture.

[beat]

Outside the Biennale season, the gardens are an ordinary park, and you can still see the pavilions from the outside. Walk on east to Sant'Elena, the quiet, leafy end of the city, where Venetians walk their dogs and nobody is in a hurry.

=== 25 | basilica-di-san-giorgio-maggiore | Observe | Basilica di San Giorgio Maggiore | 45.429389 | 12.343279 | Basilica di San Giorgio Maggiore

=== 27 | ponte-della-costituzione | Observe | Ponte della Costituzione | 45.438862 | 12.319459 | Ponte della Costituzione
Venice waited more than seventy years for a new bridge over the Grand Canal. When it finally came, people slipped over on it.

Stand at the foot of the bridge on the Piazzale Roma side, where the buses and cars stop. Across the Grand Canal is the railway station, Santa Lucia. Between them is a low, shallow arch of red steel, with steps of glass and stone.

It is the Ponte della Costituzione, designed by the Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava. The city commissioned him in nineteen ninety-nine. The bridge was carried into place by barge, and it opened on the night of the eleventh of September, two thousand and eight. It was only the fourth bridge across the Grand Canal in the city's history.

Have a look at the arch. It is very shallow, a single span of almost eighty metres between the banks. It is a beautiful line, but a shallow arch means a long, low staircase.

That is where the trouble starts. The steps were largely glass, lit from within by the parapet. Glass is slippery when wet, and in Venice things are often wet. People fell. A strip of stone was added; people still fell. In twenty nineteen a court fined Calatrava for negligence in the design. And at the end of twenty twenty-four, the city began taking out the glass steps and replacing them with stone.

Then there was access. The bridge had no step-free route, so a pod-like cabin was added in twenty ten to carry wheelchair users across. It was slow, and hot inside, and it was scrapped as an expensive failure.

[beat]

And yet. Stand back and look at the line of it against the canal and the station, and it is graceful. Most Venetians cross it every day without a second thought. It is a lesson in what happens when a beautiful idea meets a city that has been dealing with water for fifteen hundred years.

From here, walk down off the bridge and along the canal side towards the Tolentini. In five minutes you'll find Carlo Scarpa's gateway to the architecture school, which deals with the same problem far more quietly.
