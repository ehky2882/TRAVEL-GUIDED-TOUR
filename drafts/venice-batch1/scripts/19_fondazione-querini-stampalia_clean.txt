Fondazione Querini Stampalia

Most buildings in Venice spend their lives trying to keep the water out. Carlo Scarpa let it in.

Campiello Querini, just off Campo Santa Maria Formosa in Castello. Stand on the little square and face the canal. Across it, a slim bridge of iron and timber leads to a side door of the palazzo. That bridge is your first piece of Scarpa.

The foundation goes back to eighteen sixty-nine. Count Giovanni Querini Stampalia, the last of his family, left the palazzo, its library and its collections to the city. Nearly a hundred years later, between nineteen sixty-one and nineteen sixty-three, the Venetian architect Carlo Scarpa was asked to rework the ground floor and the garden. They were constantly being flooded.

Have a look at the bridge first. It starts with two big blocks of white Istrian stone as its first steps, then turns into iron and planks of larch. It sits right next to an older bridge built the traditional way, so you can compare the two side by side.

Inside, Scarpa did something no one else would have dared. He opened the old water gates to the canal and let the tide come in, along channels and steps cut beside the walls. His reasoning was simple: the high water would be inside, as it is in the rest of the city. Rather than fight it, he gave it a place to go and made it beautiful.

Then the garden. Water runs the length of it in a narrow channel, past little maze-like carvings in alabaster and stone, into a basin of copper and mosaic. It is a tiny space, and every joint in it has been thought about.

[beat]

This is the place I'd send anyone who wants to understand modern architecture in Venice. It doesn't pretend the city isn't sinking. It accepts that, and it makes something rather lovely of it.

Go in, buy a coffee in the café, and sit by the garden. Then, if you're curious, walk ten minutes to Piazza San Marco and find Scarpa's other small masterpiece, the Olivetti showroom, under the arcades.
