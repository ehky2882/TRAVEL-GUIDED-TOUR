Most buildings in Venice spend their lives trying to keep the water out. karlo scarpa let it in.

kam pee ello kweh ree nee, just off kampo santa maria for mo za in kass tello. Stand on the little square and face the canal. Across it, a slim bridge of iron and timber leads to a side door of the pa lat so. That bridge is your first piece of Scarpa.

The foundation goes back to eighteen sixty nine. Count jo vah nee kweh ree nee stam pah lee a, the last of his family, left the pa lat so, its library and its collections to the city. Nearly a hundred years later, between nineteen sixty one and nineteen sixty three, the Venetian architect karlo scarpa was asked to rework the ground floor and the garden. They were constantly being flooded.

Have a look at the bridge first. It starts with two big blocks of white istree an stone as its first steps, then turns into iron and planks of larch. It sits right next to an older bridge built the traditional way, so you can compare the two side by side.

Inside, Scarpa did something no one else would have dared. He opened the old water gates to the canal and let the tide come in, along channels and steps cut beside the walls. His reasoning was simple: the high water would be inside, as it is in the rest of the city. Rather than fight it, he gave it a place to go and made it beautiful.

Then the garden. Water runs the length of it in a narrow channel, past little maze like carvings in alabaster and stone, into a basin of copper and mosaic. It is a tiny space, and every joint in it has been thought about.

This is the place I'd send anyone who wants to understand modern architecture in Venice. It doesn't pretend the city isn't sinking. It accepts that, and it makes something rather lovely of it.

Go in, buy a coffee in the café, and sit by the garden. Then, if you're curious, walk ten minutes to Piazza San Marco and find Scarpa's other small masterpiece, the olly vetty showroom, under the arcades.
