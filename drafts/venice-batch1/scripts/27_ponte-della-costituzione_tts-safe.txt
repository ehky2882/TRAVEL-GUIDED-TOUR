Venice waited more than seventy years for a new bridge over the Grand Canal. When it finally came, people slipped over on it.

Stand at the foot of the bridge on the pee at sah lay roma side, where the buses and cars stop. Across the Grand Canal is the railway station, santa loo chee a. Between them is a low, shallow arch of red steel, with steps of glass and stone.

It is the pon tay della kos tee too tsee oh nay, designed by the Spanish architect santee ah go kala trah va. The city commissioned him in nineteen ninety nine. The bridge was carried into place by barge, and it opened on the night of the eleventh of September, two thousand and eight. It was only the fourth bridge across the Grand Canal in the city's history.

Have a look at the arch. It is very shallow, a single span of almost eighty metres between the banks. It is a beautiful line, but a shallow arch means a long, low staircase.

That is where the trouble starts. The steps were largely glass, lit from within by the parapet. Glass is slippery when wet, and in Venice things are often wet. People fell. A strip of stone was added; people still fell. In twenty nineteen a court fined kala trah va for negligence in the design. And at the end of twenty twenty four, the city began taking out the glass steps and replacing them with stone.

Then there was access. The bridge had no step free route, so a pod like cabin was added in twenty ten to carry wheelchair users across. It was slow, and hot inside, and it was scrapped as an expensive failure.

And yet. Stand back and look at the line of it against the canal and the station, and it is graceful. Most Venetians cross it every day without a second thought. It is a lesson in what happens when a beautiful idea meets a city that has been dealing with water for fifteen hundred years.

From here, walk down off the bridge and along the canal side towards the toe len tee nee. In five minutes you'll find karlo scarpa's gateway to the architecture school, which deals with the same problem far more quietly.
