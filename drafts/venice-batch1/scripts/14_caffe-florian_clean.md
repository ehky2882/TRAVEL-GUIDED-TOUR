# Caffè Florian

On the twenty-ninth of December, seventeen twenty, a man called Floriano opened a coffee house on this square. It is still open.

Piazza San Marco, on the south side, under the arcades of the Procuratie Nuove. The basilica is at the far end of the square to your left. The café is the run of arched windows, glowing inside, with an orchestra stand out front and tables in the square.

Floriano Francesconi called it Alla Venezia Trionfante, Venice Triumphant. Everyone simply called it Florian's, after him, and the name stuck. It is generally said to be the oldest café still trading in Italy, and one of the oldest in the world.

Casanova came here, in part because Florian's was one of the few coffee houses in Venice that let women in. The playwright Carlo Goldoni came too. And in the eighteen nineties, the story goes, Venice's mayor, Riccardo Selvatico, and a small circle of painters talked through, at these tables, the idea that became the Biennale.

Now have a look inside. The little rooms you see today were decorated in eighteen fifty-eight by Lodovico Cadorin, with mirrors, painted panels and velvet benches. They are tiny, and that is the point. This was a place built for conversation, not for crowds.

And here's the thing. Florian's is expensive, and there's no getting round that. But you aren't really paying for a coffee. You are paying for three hundred years of other people's conversations, in rooms built for them.

[beat]

My advice is to go inside rather than sit out in the square, where the music adds a charge to the bill. Have one coffee standing at the little bar or sitting in one of the rooms. Then look across the square at its rival, the Caffè Quadri, on the opposite side, which has been here nearly as long.
