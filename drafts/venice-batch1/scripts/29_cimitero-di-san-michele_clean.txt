Cimitero di San Michele

Venice buries its dead on an island of their own. Stravinsky is here, among the cypresses.

San Michele, the walled island in the lagoon between the city and Murano. The water bus stops right at its gate. Stand on the landing and face the island. To one side is a small church in brilliant white stone. All around it, behind high brick walls, the dark spires of cypress trees.

The church, San Michele in Isola, was built between fourteen sixty-nine and fourteen eighty by Mauro Codussi. It is generally held to be the first true Renaissance church in Venice, and it is faced entirely in white Istrian stone. Have a look at the curved tops on its front. That shape spread right across the city.

The cemetery came later. Napoleon banned burials within the city, so a new cemetery had to be found. The monastery on this island was suppressed, many of its buildings were pulled down, and the land became a burial ground. It was later joined to a second island, San Cristoforo, to make room.

Igor Stravinsky is buried here, and so is the poet Ezra Pound.

Now the modern part. Space has always been the problem here, and in nineteen ninety-eight the English architect David Chipperfield began a long project to extend the cemetery. The first part, the Courtyard of the Four Evangelists, opened in two thousand and seven. Its walls are faced in dark basalt and pale Istrian stone, with the words of the four gospels set into them. It is plain and calm and solid, and it is built round courtyards that echo the old cloister next door.

[beat]

What I find rather moving is that Chipperfield didn't try to compete with Codussi. He made something quieter and darker, and let the white church keep the light.

Walk through the cemetery to find the Chipperfield courtyards; there are signs at the entrance. Please keep your voice down. This is a working cemetery, and people are visiting their own. Then take the water bus on to Murano, a few minutes away.
