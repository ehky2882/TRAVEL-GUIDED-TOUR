Seventy one square metres, eighty seats, two tiers of boxes, and a ginness record for being the smallest historic public theatre in the world.

You're in the hamlet of veh tree ah no, high in the hills above look ka. From the outside, you'd hardly guess there was a theatre here at all. That's because it used to be a hayloft.

In eighteen eighty nine a local engineer, veer jee lee oh bya jee nee, gave the village an old hayloft on one condition: that it be turned into a theatre. Twenty two villagers formed a society to do it. Each paid a little every month, and each gave their labour for free. The theatre opened in eighteen ninety one.

Inside, it's like stepping into a jewellery box. The locals call it la bom bon yeh ra, the sweet box. Sixty seats in the stalls, twenty more in two little tiers of balconies, the boxes painted with coats of arms, garlands and masks. And then the curtain, painted in the late eighteen hundreds with the sister arts of painting, music and poetry, and a Latin motto that roughly means: to make progress, and to give honest pleasure.

That motto is the whole place, really. It was built by people with no money and a great deal of determination, so that a mountain village could have plays, music and somewhere to gather.

It was abandoned in the nineteen sixties and declared unsafe. In nineteen ninety seven the bya jee nee heirs gave it away, to be shared with the town, and after a careful restoration it reopened in September two thousand and two. The restorers even had local supporters adopt the straw seated chairs to help pay for them. Since then it's hosted small seasons, concerts, and even performances with la ska la in Milan.

Visits are by booking, so arrange one before you climb up here. And when you're inside, look for the little wooden flowers by the box office. Each one stands for somebody who helped bring this theatre back to life.
