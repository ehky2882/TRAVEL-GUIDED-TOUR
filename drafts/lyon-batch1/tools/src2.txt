=== 07 | l-antiquaire | L'Antiquaire
Lyon's cocktail revival has an address, and you're standing outside it.

Rue Hippolyte Flandrin is a short street in the first arrondissement, between the Saône and Place des Terreaux. Number twenty is easy to miss: a dark frontage, low light behind the glass, and nothing that shouts. That's L'Antiquaire, and the quiet is part of the act.

It has been here since 2010, and it was the first bar of Marc Bonneton, who has since become one of the best-known figures in French cocktail making. The style is what people call a neo-speakeasy: a bar that borrows the look of the American Prohibition years without pretending to be hidden. Inside, dark wood, bottle-green banquettes, dim light, and bartenders in shirtsleeves.

Have a look at the drinks list if you go in. The classics are done precisely, the Dry Martini, the Negroni, the White Russian. Then there are the house creations, which are where the fun is. The Pandan Killer is rum infused with pandan leaf, with house-made coconut cream, pineapple and bitters. And the Dirty Parmesan, which I'd call brave, is vodka infused with parmesan, vermouth flavoured with oregano and a dash of olive brine. A martini that tastes faintly of pizza. It is, I'm told, rather good.

And here's the thing. Lyon has always been a wine city first, a place of Beaujolais and Côtes du Rhône on the bouchon table. A bar like this one helped give it a second habit, and the counter seats now get booked like restaurant tables. Locals will tell you to arrive before eight on a weekend if you'd like to get in at all.

[beat]

If one bar isn't enough, walk across Place des Terreaux and up Rue Royale to number nineteen, Le Fantôme de l'Opéra. Two very different ideas of a cocktail bar, ten minutes apart.

=== 08 | le-fantome-de-l-opera | Le Fantôme de l'Opéra
This bar is named after a ghost, and it's dressed like the dressing room he might have haunted.

Rue Royale is the narrow street that runs north from the opera house, parallel to the Rhône. Number nineteen is a small, dark frontage, and the bar behind it is called Le Fantôme de l'Opéra, the Phantom of the Opera. The name is a nod to Gaston Leroux's novel, and to Jean Nouvel's opera house just round the corner.

The idea inside is an artist's dressing room. Deep sofas, little alcoves for two, sepia photographs on the walls, and an old gramophone. It's the sort of place where you lower your voice without being asked. The lighting does most of the persuading.

Have a look at the drinks if you go in. There's a serious list of whisky, gin and rum, and a run of house cocktails. The one people talk about is called Banzai My Love. It's made with sake and served in a Japanese bowl, with a rim decorated like a little rose garden. Theatrical, which seems fair, given the name.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how much this street has to offer in a very short stretch. A few doors along, at number twelve, is La Mère Brazier, where the first chef in the world to hold six Michelin stars cooked. Round the corner is the opera house. And this bar sits between them, the place to go before the curtain or after the last course. The bar is open from Tuesday to Saturday, in the evenings only.

[beat]

When you leave, walk down to the end of Rue Royale and turn round to face the opera house at night. After dark the glass vault on its roof glows red, and from here you can see why a phantom might feel at home.

=== 09 | mokxa | Mokxa
In 2011, the idea of paying extra for a good coffee in Lyon was so unfamiliar that the people who brought it here had to teach their customers how to drink it.

Up here on the plateau of the Croix-Rousse, a few minutes' walk from the Place de la Croix-Rousse, is Rue Victor Fort. Look for a small café with the name Mokxa over the door. This is a café run by a roaster, rather than a café that simply buys its beans in.

The story starts on the other side of the world. Roz Morris James is a New Zealander who worked as a barista at Matakana Coffee Roasters in her home town. Sadry Abidi is from Lyon. They met in Barcelona in 2006, spent six months in New Zealand in 2010, and came back convinced that French coffee could be much better than it was. In April 2011 they opened a café of just twenty-nine square metres in a former halal butcher's shop on the slopes below here. It was the first specialty coffee shop in Lyon.

Here's the gift detail. It took about six months for anyone to understand what they were doing. Roz said they had to train their customers, explaining filter coffee and single origins to people who had grown up on a quick, bitter espresso at the bar. That first little shop has since passed to new owners, but Mokxa carried on roasting, and this café on the plateau is where you can drink it now.

Have a look at the counter if you step in. The brewing is the point here: syphon, V60 filter and other slow methods, alongside the espresso. It's coffee made the way you might make tea, carefully and with time.

[beat]

From here, walk north up the Grande Rue de la Croix-Rousse. At number eighty-seven, on Saturday afternoons from spring to autumn, there's a garden built entirely of shells and stones by a single mason. It's one of the strangest and most touching places in the city.

=== 10 | la-commune | La Commune
For a hundred years this was a joiner's workshop. Now it's where Lyon's young chefs practise before they open restaurants of their own.

Rue Pré-Gaudry is in Gerland, the old industrial district south of the city centre. Look for a long, low workshop building with big doors and, on a fine evening, a crowd spilling out on to the terrace. That's La Commune.

A family carpentry business worked here from 1907. In 2018 the building, about fifteen hundred square metres of it, became what Lyon's tourist office calls the city's first food court. The project was led by the architect Déborah Hirigoyen with Damien Doublet and Damien Beaufils, and the industrial shell was kept rather than tidied away. You can still see the workshop in the bones of the place.

Here's the clever bit. La Commune isn't only a place to eat, it's an incubator. Chefs take a small equipped stall, about ten square metres each, for a residency. It's a chance to try out a restaurant idea for six months to a year in front of real customers, without the cost and risk of signing a lease. Some of them leave to open restaurants of their own. So the menu here changes as the chefs change, and in a sense you're tasting the next few years of Lyon's food scene.

Then there's the bar in the middle, and the terrace, which is why I've put it among the places to drink. On a summer evening, with a beer and three different kitchens to choose from, it's one of the most relaxed spots in the city.

And here's the thing. For a long time Gerland was a part of Lyon people drove through rather than visited. Places like this are what changed that. The idea has done well enough that the same team has since taken over the old market halls in Saint-Étienne.

[beat]

When you're done, head west towards the Rhône and cross on to the Confluence. The Musée des Confluences sits right at the point where the two rivers meet, and it's even better lit up at night.

=== 11 | le-sucre | Le Sucre
Lyon's best-known club is on the roof of a sugar warehouse.

Put the Saône behind you on Quai Rambaud, on the Confluence. In front is La Sucrière, a long concrete warehouse, and on its roof, behind the parapet, is Le Sucre, a club and cultural venue for seven hundred and eighty people.

Have a look at the building first. The first warehouse here, a hundred metres long, was built in 1925 to store sugar for the river port. Two more floors were added in 1927, and it was extended again in 1930 and 1960. By the early nineteen-nineties the port trade had gone and the building was empty. Then, in the early two thousands, it was renovated and renamed La Sucrière, the sugar refinery, and became one of the main venues for the Lyon Biennale of contemporary art.

The roof came later. In October 2011, during the Biennale, a temporary bar opened up there. It was so popular that it was made permanent, and after a renovation costing around a million euros, Le Sucre opened in the summer of 2013. It's run by the people behind Arty Farty, the organisation that created Nuits Sonores, Lyon's festival of electronic music.

Here's the gift detail. Come at sunset and you'll understand the appeal straight away: the Saône below, Fourvière on the hill, and the whole Confluence lit up around you. Club nights run on Friday and Saturday from eleven until five in the morning, and on Sunday from six in the evening until midnight. In between there are concerts, early-evening sessions and talks.

And here's the thing. Old ports usually lose their buildings when they lose their trade. This one kept its warehouse and gave it a second life as a place to dance.

[beat]

Before you leave, walk a few minutes north along the quay to the Cube Orange, the bright orange building with a great hole cut out of its side. It's one of the boldest new buildings on the Confluence, and it's hard to miss.
