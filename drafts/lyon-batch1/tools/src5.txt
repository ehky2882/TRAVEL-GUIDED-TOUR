=== 29 | cube-orange | Cube Orange
Someone has taken a bite out of this building. Quite a large one.

Quai Rambaud, on the Confluence, with the Saône beside you. In front is a bright orange block, covered all over in small, irregular holes, and with a huge round void cut right through one side of it. That's the Cube Orange. Next door, slightly greener, is its sibling.

The architects were Jakob and MacFarlane, a practice from Paris, who won the competition for the building in 2005. It was finished in 2011, an office building for the regeneration of Lyon's old river port. The structure behind it is simple: a regular grid of concrete columns, twenty-nine metres by thirty-three, over five levels. Then they cut into it.

Have a look at the big hole first. It's a cone-shaped void carved through the volume of the building. It lets light and air deep into the middle, and it opens the offices out towards the river and the view. Then look at the smaller holes in the facade. They're not random. The outer skin is pierced in a pixelated pattern that's meant to follow the movement of the river beside it, like ripples turned into a building.

Here's the gift detail. The orange isn't just cheerful. It's a nod to the orange lead paint that once protected the metalwork of industrial ports. The building is wearing the colours of the docks it replaced.

And here's the thing. Most office buildings try to disappear. This one stands on the quay of a former industrial port and refuses to. It's playful, a bit absurd, and it's rather done its job: everyone who walks along the Confluence remembers it.

[beat]

Now look next door at the green building. It's by the same architects, and it's the headquarters of the news channel Euronews. Then carry on south along the quay to the Sucrière and the Musée des Confluences. The whole walk is one of the best sequences of new architecture in France.

=== 30 | parc-de-la-tete-d-or | Parc de la Tête d'Or
The park is named after a treasure nobody has ever found: a golden head of Christ, said to be buried somewhere under the lawns.

The main gates on Place du Général Leclerc are the Porte des Enfants du Rhône, a grand entrance of gilded ironwork and stone. Through it, the park opens out in front of you, with the lake ahead and the paths spreading away under the trees. It's one of the largest city parks in France, and at a hundred and seventeen hectares you won't see the far end from here.

The land was bought in 1856, and the park opened the following year, before the work was even finished. The designers were two brothers from Switzerland, Denis and Eugène Bühler. They gave the city something closer to the English idea of a park than the formal French one: a great lake, open meadows, clumps of trees, and winding paths, with surprises around every bend. Their original plan even included grazing for sheep and cows.

Once you're inside, look out for the great greenhouses, a long range of iron and glass beside the botanical garden. The first ones were built in 1865, and they were rebuilt in the late eighteen-seventies. Inside is a tropical jungle, a rather lovely thing to find on a grey Lyon morning.

Here's the gift detail. The name comes from the legend. According to the old story, a treasure, including a head of Christ in gold, was buried on this land long ago. People have looked. Nobody has found it. The city kept the name anyway, which seems the right decision.

And here's the thing. A park like this was a political statement in the nineteenth century: fresh air, nature and space, for everyone in a crowded industrial city, free. It still works exactly as it was meant to. On a Sunday afternoon, half of Lyon seems to be here.

[beat]

If you've got an hour, walk to the lake and follow the path round to the island and the old botanical garden. And if you're here in June, don't miss the rose garden.

=== 31 | passage-thiaffait | Passage Thiaffait
This passage was built in 1827 and fell into such a bad way that people were afraid to walk through it. Then the designers moved in.

You should be on Rue René Leynaud, on the lower slopes of the Croix-Rousse. Look for the portico, the covered entrance in the building front. Go through it and you'll find yourself in a curving passage lined with small shopfronts, which ends in a staircase climbing up to Rue Burdeau on the hill above.

The passage was built in 1827 by a man called Thiaffait, who built both the house and the traboule through it. He was a local benefactor, involved in charity and education, and he died in 1861. For more than a century it was simply part of the warren of passages that the silk workers of the Croix-Rousse used to move around the hill.

Then it fell into decline. For about twenty years it was neglected, and it had a reputation for being unsafe. In 1997 a renovation was approved, and it was finished in 2001. The shops were turned into studios and workshops for young designers, and the passage was given a new name, the Village des Créateurs, the designers' village. For more than twenty years it was one of the places in Lyon where new fashion labels got their start.

Here's the gift detail. The Village des Créateurs closed in 2023. But the passage didn't go quiet. The landlord kept the designers' leases going, and the space now houses a hub for responsible fashion and a textile lab, still working with makers and small labels. So the story of fabric on this hill, which began with the canuts and their silk, carries on.

And here's the thing. Many traboules like this one are private property that the city has agreed with the owners to keep open to walkers. It's an old Lyon habit, and it still makes the city feel rather generous, as though the buildings were letting you in on a secret.

[beat]

When you reach the top of the stairs, turn round and look back down the passage. Then carry on up to the Cour des Voraces, a few minutes away. It's the grandest of all the traboules on the hill.

=== 32 | le-bal-des-ardents | Librairie Le Bal des Ardents
The way into this bookshop is through an archway made of books.

Rue Neuve runs through the first arrondissement, between Place des Terreaux and the Cordeliers. Look for number seventeen. Around the doorway is a great arch built out of dozens of books, stacked and fixed in place around the entrance. That's Le Bal des Ardents, and you'll know it the moment you see it.

The shop opened in March 2003, in premises that had housed a rather different kind of bookshop, an erotic one called La Musardine. It has since become one of the best-loved independent bookshops in Lyon, with a well-chosen stock and a strong taste for independent publishing.

Have a look at the arch on your way in. It's both a sign and a statement of intent. No poster or slogan could tell you more clearly what the shop is for.

Here's the gift detail. The name comes from a book by the writer and artist Pierre Bettencourt. And the phrase itself goes back further, to a notorious evening in Paris in 1393, when King Charles the Sixth and his courtiers danced at a masked ball dressed as wild men, in costumes of linen and pitch. A torch came too close. Several of the dancers burned to death, and the king only just survived. It was remembered as the Bal des Ardents, the ball of the burning ones. It's a rather dramatic name for a bookshop, but readers can be a passionate lot.

And here's the thing. Independent bookshops are always said to be in danger, and this one has had its uncertain moments. But it's still here, and its entrance is still the most memorable doorway on the Presqu'île.

[beat]

When you come out, walk five minutes north to Place des Terreaux and the Musée des Beaux-Arts. It's in a former abbey, and its cloister garden is one of the quietest places in the city centre.

=== 33 | pralus-lyon-monplaisir | Pralus Lyon - Monplaisir
In Lyon, pink is a flavour, and this is where you go to taste it.

You should be on Avenue des Frères Lumière in Monplaisir, a few minutes from the Institut Lumière. Look for the Pralus shop, and in the window, the thing everybody comes for: a round, golden brioche studded all through with bright pink pieces. That's the Praluline.

The pink is praline, but not the praline you might know. In Lyon, pralines are almonds and hazelnuts coated in a crisp shell of sugar coloured a vivid pink. They turn up everywhere in the city: in tarts, in brioches, scattered on cakes.

The family business was started in 1948 by Auguste Pralus, a pastry chef, and his wife Noëlie. Their first shop was in Roanne, north-west of Lyon. And here's the gift detail. Around 1955, the same year Auguste was named Meilleur Ouvrier de France, he came up with the idea of folding his pink pralines into brioche dough. The Praluline was born, and it has made the family name ever since. In 1988 his son, François, took over.

Have a look at how it's made, if you can see the counter. The pralines aren't just on top. They're folded right through the dough, so the sugar melts into the crumb as it bakes, and every slice is different.

And here's the thing. Most famous regional specialities are hundreds of years old and nobody knows who made them first. This one has an inventor, a date and an address, and it's still made by the same family.

[beat]

Before you go, buy a Praluline, or at least half of one, and take it with you. Then take it round the corner to the Institut Lumière, and eat it on the street where cinema began.

=== 34 | marche-de-la-creation | Marché de la Création
Every Sunday morning since 1979, the quay along the Saône has become an open-air gallery.

Quai Romain Rolland is on the Vieux Lyon side of the Saône, with the river beside you and the hill of Fourvière rising behind. On a Sunday morning the quay is lined with stalls, and behind every one of them is the person who made what's on it.

The market began in 1979. Today more than a hundred and fifty artists and makers set up here every Sunday, from eight in the morning until around two in the afternoon. Painters, sculptors, potters, jewellers, printmakers, photographers. And there's a rule that makes all the difference. Every piece must be sold by the person who made it. No dealers, no resellers, and no decorating of factory-made objects either.

Here's the gift detail. You can't simply turn up and set out a table. Exhibitors have to be working professionals, registered as artists or craftspeople, and a committee chooses who gets a pitch. So the standard is higher than at most street markets, and it's a good place to buy something that will remind you of Lyon long after the Praluline has gone.

Have a look along the stalls towards the bridges. Since 1997 a craft market has run alongside it as well, so on a good morning the whole quay is lined with makers.

And here's the thing. Most of the art you'll see on this trip is behind glass, in museums. This is art you can talk to. Ask the person behind the stall how they made something, and they'll tell you, usually at length, and usually with pleasure.

[beat]

When you've finished browsing, cross the footbridge over the Saône and look back at the stalls, the old town and the basilica above. Then come back across and climb up into Saint-Jean for lunch in a bouchon. That's a Lyon Sunday done properly.
