=== 12 | intercontinental-lyon-hotel-dieu | InterContinental Lyon - Hotel Dieu
For more than eight hundred years, people came to this building to be cured. Now they come to sleep.

Stand on the riverside of Quai Jules Courmont with the Rhône at your back and look across the road. Running away from you in both directions is one long, pale facade, nearly four hundred metres of it, and in the middle, rising above the roofline, a great dome. This is the Grand Hôtel-Dieu, and the hotel is inside it.

The first hospital here was built in 1184, beside the old bridge over the Rhône. It had about a dozen beds, for travellers, the poor and pilgrims. It grew for centuries. In 1532 it appointed a new doctor, a man called François Rabelais, who is rather better remembered today as the author of Gargantua and Pantagruel. And in the eighteenth century the architect Jacques-Germain Soufflot gave it the riverside front you're looking at, the face of the city on the Rhône.

Have a look at the dome. It was badly damaged in the Second World War, and rebuilt afterwards from the original plans. Then look along the facade for two statues. They're Childebert and Ultrogothe, a Frankish king and queen who founded Lyon's very first hospital in the sixth century. The building is keeping its own family history on the front.

The hospital closed at the end of 2010, the oldest working hospital in the city. Work on its conversion began in 2015, and the building reopened in 2018 with shops, restaurants, public courtyards and the InterContinental hotel. And here's the gift detail. The bar of the hotel, Le Dôme, sits right underneath that great dome.

And here's the thing. Most buildings like this become museums, and you visit them once. This one was turned back into a piece of the city. Its courtyards are open to anyone walking through, which means you can do what Lyon's residents have done for centuries, and simply wander in.

[beat]

Before you leave, step inside through any of the open doorways and find one of the courtyards. Then come back out, cross the Pont de la Guillotière and look back from the far bank. Only from across the river does the whole length of the facade make sense.

=== 13 | cour-des-loges | Cour des Loges
Italian merchants built the first of these houses in the fourteenth century. Behind this door, you can still sleep in them.

You're on Rue du Bœuf, the long, narrow street that runs through the old quarter of Saint-Jean, parallel to the Saône. Number six is the entrance to the Cour des Loges. From the street you see an old stone doorway, and very little else. Lyon's old quarter likes to keep its best rooms hidden.

The hotel is made up of four houses built between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries, joined by six inner courtyards. The oldest part dates from 1341, when it was built as a home for Italian merchants, part of the community of Italian traders who did so much to make medieval and Renaissance Lyon rich. Later the buildings became a Jesuit college. Then, until the nineteen-sixties, they were simply flats for families of modest means. In 1987 they were turned into a luxury hotel.

Have a look through the entrance if you can. The main courtyard, in the Florentine style, is covered by a contemporary glass roof seventeen metres high, so you can stand among Renaissance galleries and spiral staircases and look up at the sky. Here's the gift detail. The hotel bar, rather neatly, is called 1341.

The hotel reopened in May 2025 after a thirty-month renovation, which was planned to take nine. That's old buildings for you. It now has sixty-one rooms and suites, and two restaurants under the chef Anthony Bonnet, including Les Loges, with one Michelin star.

And here's the thing. In most cities the old quarter is a set of facades. In Vieux Lyon the interesting part is always through the door: the courtyards, the galleries, the passages the Lyonnais call traboules. This hotel is simply the grandest example of that.

[beat]

When you step back out, walk a few doors along Rue du Bœuf to number sixteen. Through that doorway is the Tour Rose, a pink stair tower in a courtyard. It's one of the most beautiful corners in the old town, and it's free to look at.

=== 14 | villa-florentine | Villa Florentine
This hotel sits halfway up the hill that Lyon calls the hill that prays, and it began as a convent.

The Montée Saint-Barthélémy is the steep, winding street that climbs the slope of Fourvière from the old quarter of Saint-Paul. Look up the hill and you'll see the Villa Florentine, set into the slope with its terraces looking out over the city.

The building dates from the seventeenth century, a time when Italian artists and merchants were still arriving in Lyon and leaving their mark on it. It was a convent first, and later a place of education. Fourvière is crowded with religious houses like it. The basilica at the top of the hill is the most famous, but the whole slope was built around convents, schools and chapels, which is why the Lyonnais call Fourvière the hill that prays and the Croix-Rousse, across the river, the hill that works.

Have a look at the view behind you. From the terraces of the villa you get the whole of Lyon laid out below: the Saône, the Presqu'île, the Rhône beyond, and on a clear day, the Alps. Here's the gift detail. The hotel has turned part of itself into a speakeasy bar called Segreto, which is Italian for secret. It seems the right word for a hotel tucked into a hillside of hidden gardens.

The hotel belongs to the Beauvallon collection and was recently renovated by the interior designer and curator Joséphine Fossey. Its restaurant, La Table, is run by the chef Henri Carlier.

And here's the thing. Most visitors take the funicular straight to the top of Fourvière and back down again. The slope itself, with its stairways, terraces and walled gardens, is the real pleasure, and this is one of the few places where you can stay on it.

[beat]

From here, keep climbing to the top of the hill and the Roman theatres. Then walk round to the Jardin des Curiosités in Saint-Just for the best view in the city, with six chairs placed there just for looking.

=== 15 | mob-hotel-lyon | MOB Hotel Lyon
This hotel would rather you thought of it as a neighbourhood than a place to sleep.

You should be on Quai Rambaud, on the Confluence, with the Saône on one side. In front of you is a building of chocolate-coloured metal cubes behind a perforated metal facade, with balconies stacked up its front. That's the MOB Hotel, which opened here in September 2017.

The project was led by Cyril Aouizerate, with the designer Philippe Starck among the partners behind the MOB idea. There are ninety-nine rooms over four floors, and nearly all of them have a balcony. They're not large. The Michelin guide describes them as thriftily decorated without feeling bare, which is a very polite way of putting it, and also quite true.

Here's the gift detail. The kitchen is ninety-five per cent organic, and the company is a certified B Corporation, which means it's been independently assessed for its social and environmental record. The hotel calls itself a place to live in common, open to everyone, and it means it. People who aren't staying come for the restaurant, the bar and the outdoor spaces.

And here's the thing. When the Confluence was being rebuilt, the risk was that it would become a district of offices and expensive flats, empty after six in the evening. Places like this, and Le Sucre on the roof of the old sugar warehouse two minutes away, are why it isn't. They give people a reason to stay after dark.

[beat]

From here, walk south along the quay. In about five minutes you'll reach the Musée des Confluences, standing right at the point where the Saône and the Rhône meet. Go at sunset, if you can.

=== 16 | couvent-sainte-marie-de-la-tourette | Couvent Sainte-Marie de La Tourette
Le Corbusier said of this building that it doesn't speak to you. You live it from the inside.

The park at Éveux is about half an hour west of Lyon. Stand on the slope below the convent. In front of you is a great concrete block sitting on the hillside. It's raised up on thin columns, so that the ground slips away underneath it. That's the Couvent Sainte-Marie de La Tourette, the last major building Le Corbusier completed in France.

He began designing it in May 1953 for the Dominican order, and it was finished in 1960. It's a monastery in the old sense, a set of wings arranged round a central courtyard, with a church closing one side. But nothing about it is old-fashioned. The three wings stand on columns of many different shapes, the friars' cells are on the top floor, and the whole thing is made of raw concrete.

Have a look at the windows on the long facades. Some of them are tall panes of glass divided by thin concrete fins set at irregular intervals, like the lines of a musical score. That's no accident. They were designed by Iannis Xenakis, who was working in Le Corbusier's office at the time and later became one of the most important composers of the twentieth century. He gave the building a rhythm you can almost hear.

Here's the gift detail. You can stay here. The convent still houses a community of about ten Dominican friars, and it offers fifty single rooms for guests, in more than fifty hectares of park. Guided tours run every day.

And here's the thing. People often call Le Corbusier's concrete harsh. Stand here, and then go inside, and you'll find the opposite: a building made almost entirely of light, silence and proportion. Since 2016 it has been on the UNESCO World Heritage list, as part of the architectural work of Le Corbusier.

[beat]

Before you go in, walk round to the church side and look at the plain, windowless wall. The light inside comes from slots and openings you can't see from here, and that's the surprise worth saving for the tour.

=== 17 | musee-des-confluences | Musée des Confluences
Lyon built a museum at the exact point where its two rivers meet, and then made it look like a cloud that had landed there.

Stand on the walkway at the southern end of the museum, at the very tip of the Presqu'île. The Rhône is on one side, the Saône on the other, and they join just ahead of you. Behind you, rising up on its legs, is a shape of glass and stainless steel that the architects described as a crystal and a cloud.

The architects were Coop Himmelb(l)au, from Vienna, and the museum opened on the twentieth of December 2014. The glass part at the front, the crystal, is the entrance hall, a sort of covered public square. The cloud behind it is the steel-clad block that holds the galleries. The whole thing is forty-four metres high and a hundred and fifty metres long, and much of it seems to float above the ground.

Have a look underneath. The building stands on piers so that you can walk beneath it, all the way to the point. The point is the reason for the whole site. For a long time this tip of the Presqu'île was industrial land beside the motorway, not somewhere you'd come for a walk. This building, and the walk under it, gave it back to the city.

Here's the gift detail. The collection inside comes largely from Lyon's old natural history museum, the Guimet, and runs to about two point two million objects, from fossils and meteorites to masks and religious objects from all over the world. The permanent exhibitions are arranged in four parts: Origins, Species, Societies and Eternities.

And here's the thing. The building cost far more than planned. The budget was a hundred and fifty-three million euros, and the final bill was forecast at close to three hundred. People argued about it for years. But on a summer evening, sitting at the point with the two rivers meeting in front of you, it's quite hard to stay cross.

[beat]

Before you leave, turn round and look north along the Saône. The orange building with the great round hole in it is the Cube Orange, ten minutes' walk up the quay, and it's worth seeing up close.

=== 18 | cite-musee-tony-garnier | Cité Musée Tony Garnier
This is a museum where the paintings are the size of buildings, and people live behind them.

Rue des Serpollières, in the eighth arrondissement, runs among blocks of flats laid out around gardens. On the end walls of the buildings around you are huge painted murals. This is the Cité Tony Garnier, and the whole estate is the museum.

Tony Garnier was born in Lyon in 1869, and he's the architect who shaped the city in the early twentieth century: the stadium at Gerland, the great cattle market hall that's now a concert venue, the hospital at Grange Blanche. As a young man he had drawn an imaginary industrial city, a complete plan for a modern town where housing, factories and parks were organised to make ordinary life better. These blocks, built between the wars and once known as the Cité des États-Unis, were his chance to build a piece of that dream for real, as housing for working families.

By the nineteen-eighties the estate was run down. The residents wanted to do something about it, and working with CitéCréation, the Lyon studio of mural painters, the project brought huge paintings to the blank end walls. The first went up around 1991, and there are now twenty-five of them. Have a look at the nearest mural. Many show Garnier's own drawings of his imagined city. Six others were designed by artists from around the world, each painting their idea of an ideal city.

Here's the gift detail. In 1992 UNESCO recognised the project as part of its World Decade for Cultural Development. And inside one of the blocks, a flat has been furnished as it would have been in the nineteen-thirties, so you can see what Garnier's tenants came home to. In 2024 the museum took its present name, the Cité Musée Tony Garnier.

And here's the thing. Most social housing projects end up being demolished, or forgotten. This one was saved by the people who lived in it, and turned into a gallery.

[beat]

From here, walk north for about twenty minutes to Monplaisir and the Institut Lumière. Garnier's city was about making modern life better, and that's where the brothers Lumière invented a very modern pleasure: going to the pictures.

=== 19 | opera-de-lyon | Opéra de Lyon
This opera house has eight muses on its roofline. There should be nine, and the ninth was never made.

You should be on Place de la Comédie, with the back of the Hôtel de Ville behind you. In front of you is the Opéra de Lyon: a classical stone front with arches at street level, and on top of it, far too big for it in the best possible way, a great black half-cylinder of glass.

The stone building opened on the first of July 1831, designed by Antoine-Marie Chenavard and Jean-Marie Pollet. By the nineteen-eighties it was worn out. In 1986 the city held a competition, which was won by Jean Nouvel, and the works ran from 1989 to 1993. Nouvel kept the four outer walls and the public foyer, and replaced almost everything else.

Have a look at the vault. The top of it is forty-two metres above the street. Here's the clever bit: Nouvel went just as far down as up. Below your feet the building goes another twenty metres into the ground. Of its eighteen storeys, the first five are underground and the last five are inside that glass vault. In between, a black auditorium for eleven hundred people, hung inside the old shell like a box inside a box.

Now look along the top of the stone facade for the statues. They're the muses of Greek mythology. Eight were commissioned in 1862 and put up the following year. One fell off in 1895, so in 1896 all of them were taken down, and the ones you see are cast-iron copies put back in 1912. The ninth muse, Urania, the muse of astronomy, was never made at all.

And here's the thing. This building divided opinion when it opened, a modern glass hat on a classical head. Thirty years later it's simply part of the skyline, and one of the few places where you can see an old building and a new one share the same body without either losing face.

[beat]

Come back after dark if you can. The vault glows red, and the bar Le Fantôme de l'Opéra, a few minutes up Rue Royale behind you, is the obvious place to end the evening.
