=== 20 | lugdunum | Lugdunum
The museum of Roman Lyon is almost invisible, and that was the whole idea.

Near the top of Fourvière hill, on Rue Cléberg, is the entrance to the archaeological park. Below you are the two Roman theatres cut into the slope. Now look for the museum. You'll find only a concrete entrance and, if you look down the hillside, a few long windows in the slope itself. The rest of the building is in the hill.

The architect was Bernard Zehrfuss, and the museum opened on the fifteenth of November 1975. He set it half into the flank of the hill, beside the great theatre and the smaller odeon, so that the building wouldn't compete with the ruins it was built to explain. Inside, the visit goes downwards, on a long concrete ramp that winds through the hill, and at the windows you look straight out at the theatres.

Here's the gift detail. Lyon was Lugdunum, one of the great cities of Roman Gaul, and the Emperor Claudius was born here. The museum holds the Claudian Table, a large bronze tablet found in 1528 on the slopes of the Croix-Rousse. It records a speech Claudius gave to the Senate in Rome, arguing that leading men from Gaul should be allowed to become senators. A speech about who belongs, preserved in bronze for two thousand years.

Then there's the circus mosaic, from the third century, found on the Presqu'île. It shows a chariot race in full flight, the whole thing from start to finish: the starting gates, the track, the chariots. It's a Roman sports report in tiles.

And here's the thing. Most museums want to be noticed. This one deliberately hides, and lets the hill and the theatres take the attention. Nearly fifty years on, that modesty looks rather wise.

[beat]

When you come out, walk down into the great theatre and climb the stone seats to the top row. From there you can see the whole of Lyon, much as the Romans did, only with rather more roofs.

=== 21 | hotel-de-ville-de-villeurbanne | Hôtel de Ville de Villeurbanne
In 1934, a doctor turned mayor built a skyline for working families, and called it the Gratte-Ciel, the skyscrapers.

Place Lazare Goujon is the heart of Villeurbanne, the town that runs on from Lyon to the east. In front of you is the town hall, a stern, pale building with a tall clock tower. Turn round and look down the avenue behind you. On either side, in pairs, are tall stepped blocks of flats. Together they're the Gratte-Ciel.

The man behind it was Lazare Goujon, a doctor who was elected mayor in 1924. Villeurbanne was a crowded industrial suburb, with poor housing and poor health, and he wanted a proper centre for it. He got the land, held competitions, and the whole district went up between 1927 and 1934. It was inaugurated on the tenth of June 1934.

Have a look at the town hall first. It's by Robert Giroud, a winner of the Prix de Rome, and its belfry is sixty-five metres high. Then look down the avenue at the towers. They were designed by Môrice Leroux, who also built the Palais du Travail, the Palace of Work, with a theatre, a swimming pool, a clinic and public showers. The plan was for around fifteen hundred homes, and the two tallest towers have nineteen storeys.

Here's the gift detail. The flats had central heating from the start, which was rare for working-class housing at the time. And the heating plant on Rue Verlaine, which went into service in 1932, ran on burning the town's rubbish.

And here's the thing. We tend to think of social housing as a post-war idea. This is from before the war, and it's built with genuine pride: stepped towers, a civic square, a theatre. It wasn't housing for the poor. It was a new town centre, and everybody was meant to have a share of it.

[beat]

Before you go, walk round to the Palais du Travail, which is now home to the Théâtre National Populaire. And if you walk on down the avenue, you'll see the new blocks of the Gratte-Ciel's extension, still being added to almost a century later.

=== 22 | gare-de-lyon-saint-exupery-tgv | Gare de Lyon Saint-Exupéry TGV
This is a railway station that looks like a bird about to take off, and it sits next to an airport.

Out by the airport at Saint-Exupéry, east of Lyon, stand on the forecourt of the station. In front of you is a great steel and glass roof that rises to a point and then spreads two wings to either side. If it reminds you of a bird with its wings opening, you're seeing exactly what everybody else does.

The architect was Santiago Calatrava, working with the station designers of the French railways. It was inaugurated on the twenty-eighth of June 1994, under its old name, Satolas TGV. The building is about four hundred metres long, forty metres wide and forty metres high, and it carries the high-speed line straight past the airport.

Have a look underneath the great hall when you go in. The station has five tracks, and here's the gift detail. The two in the middle run through a concrete tube, so that trains that aren't stopping can pass straight through at full speed, three hundred kilometres an hour. Stand on the platform as one goes through and you'll feel the building take a breath.

Then follow the signs for the airport. A footbridge two hundred and fifty metres long, with a moving walkway, links the station to the terminals. And since 2010 the Rhônexpress tram has run from here to Part-Dieu in under thirty minutes.

And here's the thing. Most stations by airports are corridors. Calatrava took a piece of transport infrastructure in the middle of fields and gave it a sense of occasion. It's rather lovely that arriving in Lyon, for many people, starts with a building that's so clearly glad to see them.

[beat]

If you've got time before your train, walk out to the far end of the forecourt and look back. It's only from a distance that the bird really comes into focus, wings open, about to leave with the planes.

=== 23 | institut-lumiere | Institut Lumière
The first film in history shows workers walking out of a factory. You're standing on the street where it was shot.

You should be on Rue du Premier Film in Monplaisir, in the eighth arrondissement. On one side is a large, ornate villa set in its gardens. That's the Villa Lumière. And nearby is a long, plain building, the Hangar du Premier Film. The street is named for what happened here.

The villa was built between 1899 and 1902 by Antoine Lumière, the father of the brothers Auguste and Louis. The family ran a large business making photographic plates, and their factory stood right here. In 1895 the brothers set up their new invention, the cinematograph, and filmed the workers leaving the factory gates at the end of the day. Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory in Lyon is often called the first film ever made.

Have a look at the hangar. It's the last surviving piece of the Lumière factory. It was restored in 1998 and is now a cinema with two hundred and sixty-nine seats. Here's the gift detail. The cinema stands on the very ground where those first workers walked out into the light, so when you watch a film inside, you're sitting where the very first film's subjects walked.

The Institut Lumière was founded here in 1982 to preserve and show films. The director Bertrand Tavernier was its president until his death in 2021, and its director, Thierry Frémaux, also runs the Cannes Film Festival. Every October since 2009 it has held the Festival Lumière, and given the Prix Lumière to a filmmaker for a lifetime's work.

And here's the thing. Most inventions are commemorated with a plaque. This one is commemorated by doing the thing itself, every day, in the same place. Lyon didn't build a museum to the cinema. It kept going to the pictures.

[beat]

Before you leave, step into the villa's garden and look at the house. It's an extravagant building, and it tells you how successful the Lumière family already were before cinema. Then, if you fancy something sweet, there's a Pralus shop just round the corner on Avenue des Frères Lumière.

=== 24 | cour-des-voraces | Cour des Voraces
This courtyard was a hiding place, a shortcut and a fortress, and it's still somebody's front hall.

Number nine, Place Colbert, on the slopes of the Croix-Rousse. Go through the doorway and you'll find yourself in a tall courtyard. In front of you is a vast open staircase climbing six storeys up the face of the building, with landings at every floor. This is the Cour des Voraces.

It was built around 1840, when the Croix-Rousse was the heart of Lyon's silk industry. The workers were called canuts, and they lived and worked in buildings like this one, with ceilings high enough for their great looms. To get their silk down the hill to the merchants without getting it wet, they used traboules, covered passages that cut through buildings and courtyards from one street to the next. This is the most famous of them. It has three ways in and out: here, on Montée Saint-Sébastien and on Rue Imbert-Colomès.

Here's the gift detail. The name comes, most likely, from the Voraces, a society of canuts who took part in the republican uprisings of 1848 and 1849. A passage that runs through a whole block is a very useful thing in a revolt. You can disappear from one street and come out on another before anyone has noticed.

Have a look at the plaque on the wall. It remembers the courtyard as a hive of silk work, where the canuts fought for their lives and their dignity.

And here's the thing. People live here. This isn't a museum, it's a block of flats that the city opens to walkers. So come quietly, keep to the stairs and the courtyard, and remember that this is somebody's home.

[beat]

When you leave, take the exit to Montée Saint-Sébastien and walk on up the hill. At the top, on the Boulevard des Canuts, there's a whole building painted to look like a Croix-Rousse street, and it's the best way to see how this neighbourhood thinks about itself.

=== 25 | tour-rose | Tour Rose (Maison du Crible)
Behind a door in the old town is a tower so tall and so pink that it has become one of the best-loved sights of Vieux Lyon.

Find number sixteen, Rue du Bœuf, in Saint-Jean. Go through the doorway and along the passage into the courtyard. Look up. Rising out of the courtyard is a stair tower, washed in a warm pink, much taller than the houses around it. This is the Tour Rose, in the house once known as the Maison du Crible.

The tower holds the staircase that served the house, and it's the height that's remarkable. In Renaissance Lyon, it's often said, a tall stair tower was a status symbol, a way for a merchant to show his wealth from the courtyard, where his neighbours would see it, rather than from the street. Some historians have dated the house to the sixteenth century, others to the seventeenth, and the arguments go on.

Have a look at the doorway as you come in. Its design has been linked to the Italian architect Sebastiano Serlio, whose books of architecture travelled across Europe in the Renaissance, and whose ideas are all over this part of Lyon. The influence of Italy is everywhere in this street, from the merchants who lived here to the shapes of the doors.

Here's the gift detail. The pink is the colour the tower came to be known by, after its restoration, and the house took its present name from it. You'll hear people say it wasn't always pink at all.

And here's the thing. The tower can no longer be climbed. It's a thing to stand under and look up at, which is rather what its builder had in mind. A staircase built to impress, still doing the job four or five centuries on.

[beat]

When you come back out on to Rue du Bœuf, walk a few doors along to number six, the Cour des Loges. Its courtyard, under a modern glass roof, is the grandest of the old town's hidden spaces.

=== 26 | mur-des-canuts | Mur des Canuts
The street in front of you is real. The one on the wall is not, and it's far busier.

At the corner of the Boulevard des Canuts and Rue Denfert-Rochereau, on the plateau of the Croix-Rousse, look at the building in front of you. In front of you is the side of a building, and on it, a whole Croix-Rousse street: tall houses, steps, shop windows, people. It's painted. All of it. This is the Mur des Canuts, the wall of the silk weavers.

It was first painted in 1987 by CitéCréation, the Lyon studio of mural painters, on a blind facade of around twelve hundred square metres. It's often described as one of the largest murals in Europe. It's certainly one of the most convincing. On a grey day, from a little way back, it can be genuinely hard to tell where the real buildings stop and the painted ones begin.

Have a look at the great staircase in the middle. Those steep stairways are the signature of the Croix-Rousse, where the hill is so steep that streets turn into steps. Then look at the people on the wall. There are figures from every age, and they've changed over time.

Here's the gift detail. The wall is repainted every so often to keep up with the neighbourhood. It was updated in 1997, and again in 2013. The people in it get older, the shops change, and the children painted in the first version grow up. It's a portrait of a neighbourhood that ages along with it.

And here's the thing. The canuts were the silk weavers who made the Croix-Rousse, and who rose up in famous revolts in the eighteen-thirties. Their looms are long gone. This wall is how the neighbourhood remembers them: not with a statue, but with a crowded, ordinary street.

[beat]

From here, walk a few minutes south and down the hill. Through the doorway at Place Colbert is the Cour des Voraces, the real staircase that this painted one might have been modelled on.

=== 27 | jardin-rosa-mir | Jardin Rosa Mir
A mason fell ill and made a promise: if he lived, he'd build a garden for his mother. He lived, and here it is.

You should be on the Grande Rue de la Croix-Rousse, outside number eighty-seven. From the pavement there's not much to see. The garden is tucked inside the block, and you go in through the doorway when it's open, which is only on Saturdays, from spring to autumn.

The builder was Jules Senis, a mason, born in 1913 near Valencia, in Spain. He moved to France in 1947 and settled in Lyon in 1951. Then he developed cancer. He made a vow that, if he recovered, he'd build a garden in honour of his mother, Rosa Mir. He recovered, and in 1957 he began.

He worked on it for around twenty years, on his own, stone by stone. Have a look around you once you're inside. Pillars and arches, baskets and basins, all of stone, and every surface covered with shells and coloured pebbles set into the mortar in stars and geometric patterns. Many of the stones came from building sites he worked on. There's a small shrine with a statue of the Virgin, and everywhere, Mediterranean plants. It's a little piece of Spain, in a courtyard in Lyon.

Here's the gift detail. The whole garden is only a few hundred square metres, and only fifteen visitors can be inside at a time, so you may have to wait at the door. It's worth it.

Senis died in 1983. His friends looked after the garden, then the city took it over, and today it's a listed historic monument. It closed for some years when it became unsafe, and reopened in 2016 after restoration.

And here's the thing. Lyon is full of grand architecture paid for by merchants, bishops and kings. This is architecture paid for by one man's gratitude. It might be the most moving building in the city.

[beat]

When you come out, carry on to the Place de la Croix-Rousse and then down to the Boulevard des Canuts, where a whole building is painted to look like the neighbourhood. It's another way of telling the same story about who built this hill.

=== 28 | jardin-des-curiosites | Jardin des Curiosités
This viewpoint was a present from Montréal, and it came with six chairs.

This small walled garden sits on the hill of Saint-Just, high above the old town, at the end of Place de Montréal. In front of you, over the low wall, the whole of Lyon is spread out: the roofs of Saint-Jean below, the Saône, the Presqu'île, the Rhône beyond, and in the distance, on a clear day, the Alps.

The garden was a gift from the city of Montréal to Lyon, to mark twenty years of twinning between the two cities. It was inaugurated in 2000 and opened to the public in August 2001. It's about six thousand square metres, and it was designed with the help of the Canadian landscape firm Vlan Paysages. Lyon sent a garden back, and it's in the centre of Montréal.

Have a look at the chairs. There are six of them, by the Québec sculptor Michel Goulet. They're said to echo a set of chairs on a belvedere back in Montréal, and they give the garden its name. Each is a little curiosity of its own, and between them they tell you exactly what this place is for: sit down, and look.

Here's the gift detail. The garden has several names. Some people call it the Jardin de Montréal, some the Belvédère Abbé Larue. Most locals simply call it the best view in Lyon that the visitors haven't found yet.

And here's the thing. Most people see Lyon from the top of Fourvière, beside the basilica, with a crowd. This view is from lower down and closer in, and somehow you see the city better for it. You can pick out the streets and the roofs, not just the skyline.

[beat]

Before you go, find the chair with the best view, sit in it, and stay for a while. Then walk down the steps of the Montée du Gourguillon into Saint-Jean. It's one of the oldest streets in Lyon, and you'll come out right by the cathedral.
