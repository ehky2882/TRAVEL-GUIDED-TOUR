Hôtel de Ville de Villeurbanne

In 1934, a doctor turned mayor built a skyline for working families, and called it the Gratte-Ciel, the skyscrapers.

Place Lazare Goujon is the heart of Villeurbanne, the town that runs on from Lyon to the east. In front of you is the town hall, a stern, pale building with a tall clock tower. Turn round and look down the avenue behind you. On either side, in pairs, are tall stepped blocks of flats. Together they're the Gratte-Ciel.

The man behind it was Lazare Goujon, a doctor who was elected mayor in 1924. Villeurbanne was a crowded industrial suburb, with poor housing and poor health, and he wanted a proper centre for it. He got the land, held competitions, and the whole district went up between 1927 and 1934. It was inaugurated on the tenth of June 1934.

Have a look at the town hall first. It's by Robert Giroud, a winner of the Prix de Rome, and its belfry is sixty-five metres high. Then look down the avenue at the towers. They were designed by Môrice Leroux, who also built the Palais du Travail, the Palace of Work, with a theatre, a swimming pool, a clinic and public showers. The plan was for around fifteen hundred homes, and the two tallest towers have nineteen storeys.

Here's the gift detail. The flats had central heating from the start, which was rare for working-class housing at the time. And the heating plant on Rue Verlaine, which went into service in 1932, ran on burning the town's rubbish.

And here's the thing. We tend to think of social housing as a post-war idea. This is from before the war, and it's built with genuine pride: stepped towers, a civic square, a theatre. It wasn't housing for the poor. It was a new town centre, and everybody was meant to have a share of it.

[beat]

Before you go, walk round to the Palais du Travail, which is now home to the Théâtre National Populaire. And if you walk on down the avenue, you'll see the new blocks of the Gratte-Ciel's extension, still being added to almost a century later.
