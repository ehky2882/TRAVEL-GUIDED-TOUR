# Couvent Sainte-Marie de La Tourette

Le Corbusier said of this building that it doesn't speak to you. You live it from the inside.

The park at Éveux is about half an hour west of Lyon. Stand on the slope below the convent. In front of you is a great concrete block sitting on the hillside. It's raised up on thin columns, so that the ground slips away underneath it. That's the Couvent Sainte-Marie de La Tourette, the last major building Le Corbusier completed in France.

He began designing it in May 1953 for the Dominican order, and it was finished in 1960. It's a monastery in the old sense, a set of wings arranged round a central courtyard, with a church closing one side. But nothing about it is old-fashioned. The three wings stand on columns of many different shapes, the friars' cells are on the top floor, and the whole thing is made of raw concrete.

Have a look at the windows on the long facades. Some of them are tall panes of glass divided by thin concrete fins set at irregular intervals, like the lines of a musical score. That's no accident. They were designed by Iannis Xenakis, who was working in Le Corbusier's office at the time and later became one of the most important composers of the twentieth century. He gave the building a rhythm you can almost hear.

Here's the gift detail. You can stay here. The convent still houses a community of about ten Dominican friars, and it offers fifty single rooms for guests, in more than fifty hectares of park. Guided tours run every day.

And here's the thing. People often call Le Corbusier's concrete harsh. Stand here, and then go inside, and you'll find the opposite: a building made almost entirely of light, silence and proportion. Since 2016 it has been on the UNESCO World Heritage list, as part of the architectural work of Le Corbusier.
