Le Bouchon des Filles

Lyon's cooking was built by women. This little bouchon is carrying on the family business.

Rue Sergent Blandan runs along the lower slopes of the Croix-Rousse, a few minutes' climb from Place des Terreaux. You're looking for number twenty, a small restaurant with a sign that reads Le Bouchon des Filles. It only opens in the evening, so by day you may simply see a dark window and the checked tablecloths waiting inside.

It opened at the end of 2007. The founders were two young Lyonnaises who had run the dining room at the Café des Fédérations, one of the city's best-known bouchons, and decided to open one of their own. The space had been a restaurant called the Gousse d'Ail, the garlic clove. They kept the bouchon spirit and softened the look. The tablecloths here are checked in white and pink rather than the usual red.

Have a look at the menu if it's posted. It's a set menu of Lyonnais cooking with a lighter hand: the bouchon classics, served without ceremony, to tables of friends who have usually booked.

And here's what's worth knowing, standing here. Lyon's food reputation rests on the mères lyonnaises, the mothers of Lyon. They were women, mostly from the countryside, who left service in rich households in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and opened their own restaurants. Eugénie Brazier, the most famous of them, became the first chef anywhere to hold six Michelin stars. A bouchon called the girls' bouchon, run by women, is a rather neat continuation of that story.

From here, carry on uphill. A few minutes above you is the Cour des Voraces, with its great open staircase, the most famous of the passages the silk weavers used to cut through these slopes.
