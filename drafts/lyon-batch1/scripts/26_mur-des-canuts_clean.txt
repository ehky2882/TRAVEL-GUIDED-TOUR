Mur des Canuts

The street in front of you is real. The one on the wall is not, and it's far busier.

At the corner of the Boulevard des Canuts and Rue Denfert-Rochereau, on the plateau of the Croix-Rousse, look at the building in front of you. In front of you is the side of a building, and on it, a whole Croix-Rousse street: tall houses, steps, shop windows, people. It's painted. All of it. This is the Mur des Canuts, the wall of the silk weavers.

It was first painted in 1987 by CitéCréation, the Lyon studio of mural painters, on a blind facade of around twelve hundred square metres. It's often described as one of the largest murals in Europe. It's certainly one of the most convincing. On a grey day, from a little way back, it can be genuinely hard to tell where the real buildings stop and the painted ones begin.

Have a look at the great staircase in the middle. Those steep stairways are the signature of the Croix-Rousse, where the hill is so steep that streets turn into steps. Then look at the people on the wall. There are figures from every age, and they've changed over time.

Here's the gift detail. The wall is repainted every so often to keep up with the neighbourhood. It was updated in 1997, and again in 2013. The people in it get older, the shops change, and the children painted in the first version grow up. It's a portrait of a neighbourhood that ages along with it.

And here's the thing. The canuts were the silk weavers who made the Croix-Rousse, and who rose up in famous revolts in the eighteen-thirties. Their looms are long gone. This wall is how the neighbourhood remembers them: not with a statue, but with a crowded, ordinary street.

From here, walk a few minutes south and down the hill. Through the doorway at Place Colbert is the Cour des Voraces, the real staircase that this painted one might have been modelled on.
