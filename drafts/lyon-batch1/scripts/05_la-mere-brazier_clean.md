# La Mère Brazier

In 1933, the woman who cooked here became the first chef anywhere to hold six Michelin stars.

You're on Rue Royale, a narrow street running parallel to the Rhône, just north of the Opéra. Look for number twelve, a corner building with a discreet sign reading La Mère Brazier. From the pavement it looks like a comfortable family house. That's deliberate.

Eugénie Brazier was born on a farm in the late nineteenth century and came to Lyon as a young woman to work as a wet nurse and then as a cook in private houses. She learned her trade with another great cook, the Mère Fillioux, and in 1921 she opened her own restaurant here. She later opened a second, up in the hills at the Col de la Luère. In 1933 the Michelin guide gave three stars to each of them. Six stars, held by one cook. Nobody had done it before.

Here's the gift detail. In the years just after the Second World War, a young apprentice called Paul Bocuse came to work for her at the Col de la Luère. He went on to become the most famous chef in France. When people talk about a Lyonnais school of cooking, this is where much of it began.

Brazier died in 1977. Her granddaughter Jacotte kept the restaurant going for many years, and today it belongs to Mathieu Viannay, himself a Meilleur Ouvrier de France, and it holds two Michelin stars. When he restored it, he brought back the original nineteen-thirties tilework and kept the layout of a family house, with a main dining room and small salons upstairs.

And here's the thing. The great names of French cooking are mostly men, but the foundations in this city were laid by women like Brazier, cooking simple food perfectly. This doorway is one of the places where that history is still being served for lunch.

If you've a few minutes, walk a few doors along Rue Royale to number nineteen and the cocktail bar Le Fantôme de l'Opéra, and then on to Jean Nouvel's opera house itself. The whole street is a rather good evening in miniature.
