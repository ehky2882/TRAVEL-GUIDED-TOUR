Luh Fon tome duh lop ay rah

This bar is named after a ghost, and it's dressed like the dressing room he might have haunted.

Roo Roy al is the narrow street that runs north from the opera house, parallel to the Rone. Number nineteen is a small, dark frontage, and the bar behind it is called Luh Fon tome duh lop ay rah, the Phantom of the Opera. The name is a nod to Gas ton Luh roo's novel, and to Zhon Noo vel's opera house just round the corner.

The idea inside is an artist's dressing room. Deep sofas, little alcoves for two, sepia photographs on the walls, and an old gramophone. It's the sort of place where you lower your voice without being asked. The lighting does most of the persuading.

Have a look at the drinks if you go in. There's a serious list of whisky, gin and rum, and a run of house cocktails. The one people talk about is called Banzai My Love. It's made with sake and served in a Japanese bowl, with a rim decorated like a little rose garden. Theatrical, which seems fair, given the name.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is how much this street has to offer in a very short stretch. A few doors along, at number twelve, is La Mair Brah zee ay, where the first chef in the world to hold six Michelin stars cooked. Round the corner is the opera house. And this bar sits between them, the place to go before the curtain or after the last course. The bar is open from Tuesday to Saturday, in the evenings only.

When you leave, walk down to the end of Roo Roy al and turn round to face the opera house at night. After dark the glass vault on its roof glows red, and from here you can see why a phantom might feel at home.
