Kah fay Comp twahr Ah bel

This restaurant has been serving the same kind of food in the same rooms since nineteen twenty eight.

You'll find it on Roo Geen mair, a quiet street in Ay nay, the old quarter at the southern end of the Presk eel, just a few steps from the Romanesque church of San Mar tan Day nay. Look for the old shopfront with the name painted above it. It looks as if it has always been here, which, for most practical purposes, it has.

Kah fay Comp twahr Ah bel opened in nineteen twenty eight in a building that was already around two centuries old. Step inside, if the door's open, and you'll see why people treat it as a museum of the Lee on nay boo shon. Waxed parquet floors. Wood panelling. Big old mirrors. And a great clutter of objects, collected over the decades by one owner after another, none of whom seems ever to have thrown anything away.

The cooking is just as loyal. The dish people cross the city for is the kuh nell duh bro shay, a soft dumpling of pike baked in a crayfish sauce called Non tew ah, and plenty of locals will tell you this is the best one in Lyon. Then there's the sah lad lee on nez, with bacon and a soft egg. And, for the brave, the tab lee ay duh sah pur, which translates as the fireman's apron. It is tripe, breaded and fried, and the name is said to come from its shape.

And here's the thing. A restaurant like this survives not by changing but by refusing to. Ah bel carries the Boo shon Lee on nay label, which exists to protect exactly this sort of place from being smartened up into something else. Old rooms, old recipes, and a menu that would have been familiar to the first customers who sat here.

Before you go, walk round the corner to the church of San Mar tan Day nay. It's one of the oldest churches in Lyon, and it makes the restaurant look positively young.
