Luh Boo shon day Fee

Lyon's cooking was built by women. This little boo shon is carrying on the family business.

Roo Sair zhon Blon don runs along the lower slopes of the Krwah Roos, a few minutes' climb from Plass day Teh ro. You're looking for number twenty, a small restaurant with a sign that reads Luh Boo shon day Fee. It only opens in the evening, so by day you may simply see a dark window and the checked tablecloths waiting inside.

It opened at the end of two thousand and seven. The founders were two young Lee on nez who had run the dining room at the Kah fay day Fay day rah syon, one of the city's best known boo shons, and decided to open one of their own. The space had been a restaurant called the Goos dye, the garlic clove. They kept the boo shon spirit and softened the look. The tablecloths here are checked in white and pink rather than the usual red.

Have a look at the menu if it's posted. It's a set menu of Lee on nay cooking with a lighter hand: the boo shon classics, served without ceremony, to tables of friends who have usually booked.

And here's what's worth knowing, standing here. Lyon's food reputation rests on the mair lee on nez, the mothers of Lyon. They were women, mostly from the countryside, who left service in rich households in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and opened their own restaurants. Oo zhay nee Brah zee ay, the most famous of them, became the first chef anywhere to hold six Michelin stars. A boo shon called the girls' boo shon, run by women, is a rather neat continuation of that story.

From here, carry on uphill. A few minutes above you is the Coor day Vor ass, with its great open staircase, the most famous of the passages the silk weavers used to cut through these slopes.
