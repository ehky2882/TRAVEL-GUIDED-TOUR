# Opéra de Lyon

This opera house has eight muses on its roofline. There should be nine, and the ninth was never made.

You should be on Place de la Comédie, with the back of the Hôtel de Ville behind you. In front of you is the Opéra de Lyon: a classical stone front with arches at street level, and on top of it, far too big for it in the best possible way, a great black half-cylinder of glass.

The stone building opened on the first of July 1831, designed by Antoine-Marie Chenavard and Jean-Marie Pollet. By the nineteen-eighties it was worn out. In 1986 the city held a competition, which was won by Jean Nouvel, and the works ran from 1989 to 1993. Nouvel kept the four outer walls and the public foyer, and replaced almost everything else.

Have a look at the vault. The top of it is forty-two metres above the street. Here's the clever bit: Nouvel went just as far down as up. Below your feet the building goes another twenty metres into the ground. Of its eighteen storeys, the first five are underground and the last five are inside that glass vault. In between, a black auditorium for eleven hundred people, hung inside the old shell like a box inside a box.

Now look along the top of the stone facade for the statues. They're the muses of Greek mythology. Eight were commissioned in 1862 and put up the following year. One fell off in 1895, so in 1896 all of them were taken down, and the ones you see are cast-iron copies put back in 1912. The ninth muse, Urania, the muse of astronomy, was never made at all.

And here's the thing. This building divided opinion when it opened, a modern glass hat on a classical head. Thirty years later it's simply part of the skyline, and one of the few places where you can see an old building and a new one share the same body without either losing face.

[beat]

Come back after dark if you can. The vault glows red, and the bar Le Fantôme de l'Opéra, a few minutes up Rue Royale behind you, is the obvious place to end the evening.
