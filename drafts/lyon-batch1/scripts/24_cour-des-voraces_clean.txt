Cour des Voraces

This courtyard was a hiding place, a shortcut and a fortress, and it's still somebody's front hall.

Number nine, Place Colbert, on the slopes of the Croix-Rousse. Go through the doorway and you'll find yourself in a tall courtyard. In front of you is a vast open staircase climbing six storeys up the face of the building, with landings at every floor. This is the Cour des Voraces.

It was built around 1840, when the Croix-Rousse was the heart of Lyon's silk industry. The workers were called canuts, and they lived and worked in buildings like this one, with ceilings high enough for their great looms. To get their silk down the hill to the merchants without getting it wet, they used traboules, covered passages that cut through buildings and courtyards from one street to the next. This is the most famous of them. It has three ways in and out: here, on Montée Saint-Sébastien and on Rue Imbert-Colomès.

Here's the gift detail. The name comes, most likely, from the Voraces, a society of canuts who took part in the republican uprisings of 1848 and 1849. A passage that runs through a whole block is a very useful thing in a revolt. You can disappear from one street and come out on another before anyone has noticed.

Have a look at the plaque on the wall. It remembers the courtyard as a hive of silk work, where the canuts fought for their lives and their dignity.

And here's the thing. People live here. This isn't a museum, it's a block of flats that the city opens to walkers. So come quietly, keep to the stairs and the courtyard, and remember that this is somebody's home.

When you leave, take the exit to Montée Saint-Sébastien and walk on up the hill. At the top, on the Boulevard des Canuts, there's a whole building painted to look like a Croix-Rousse street, and it's the best way to see how this neighbourhood thinks about itself.
