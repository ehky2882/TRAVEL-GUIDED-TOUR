Historische Tuin Schoonoord

Within a few hundred metres of the busiest museums in Rotterdam lies a garden that it is very easy to walk past without suspecting it is there.

Behind a gate on the Kievitslaan, Schoonoord opens out as a small, quiet landscape of lawns, old trees and water. Once through the entrance, a winding path leads on ahead, mature cedars and beeches stand on either side, and the pond lies somewhere just beyond, with the noise of the city already muffled by the planting.

The land was meadow outside the city until about 1700, when it was protected by dykes, and a country estate called Rust en Lust was established here in 1706. It took the name Schoonoord in 1816, and in 1860 its owners commissioned the garden you see now, laid out in the English landscape style with the pond, the beech trees and the cedars of Lebanon, which are the stars of the show.

Those cedars spread their branches wide and flat, almost like layered shelves, and give the garden its old-world, country-house feeling.

The banker Jacob Mees bought the main part of the estate in 1927, his heirs opened the garden to the public in 1973, and it has been a protected national monument since 2000. There is also a memorial plaque to David Mees, who was shot here in the garden on Liberation Day in 1945, and it is worth pausing at it quietly.

More than anything, Schoonoord gives the city a kind of privacy that has been shared out, so anyone can now sit in what was once a family's own green world.

Leave by the Kievitslaan gate and turn towards the Museumpark, a few minutes away. The contrast between this hidden garden and the bold modern museums down the road is one of the nicest surprises in this part of town.
