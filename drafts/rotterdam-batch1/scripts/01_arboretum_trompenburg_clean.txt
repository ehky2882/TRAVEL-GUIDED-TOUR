Arboretum Trompenburg

Most museums keep their collection safely behind glass, but the one around you has to be watered, pruned, and now and then rescued from a storm.

Step through the entrance pavilion on the Honingerdijk and pause where the paths begin to fan out, with the traffic of Kralingen already fading behind you. Lawns run away between single specimen trees, each with a small label at its foot, and somewhere beyond them the pond catches whatever light the sky is offering.

Those labels are the key to the whole place. Trompenburg is registered as a museum, and every tree here is catalogued much as a painting would be, with its species and origin noted down. The oaks are the headline act, kept alongside the beeches, the hollies and the hostas as registered Dutch national plant collections, and oak specialists from abroad come specifically to walk these paths.

The garden is older than almost anything growing in it. Planting began around 1820 in the English landscape manner of curving paths and borrowed views, and in 1870 the landscape architect Zocher added a western section stocked with exotic trees. The Smith family, later the van Hoey Smiths, owned the estate and simply could not stop collecting, generation after generation.

Private obsession, rather than any civic plan, is what built Trompenburg, because one family's appetite for unfamiliar trees became something the city could not bear to lose, and so in 1958 a foundation took the estate on to make sure it would survive the family that made it.

Before you leave, find the desert greenhouse, where cacti and succulents grow happily under glass on ground that was marshland two centuries ago, which strikes me as a fine piece of Dutch stubbornness. And if you can come back in spring, the rhododendrons turn these lawns into something else entirely.
