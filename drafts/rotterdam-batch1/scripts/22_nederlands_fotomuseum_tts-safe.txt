Nayderlants Fotomuseum

This warehouse was built to store Brazilian coffee, and it now holds more than six and a half million photographs.

Facing the Rinehahven from the Kahtendrekht side, the tall brick warehouse with its rows of windows and the name Santos on it is the one you want. Nine storeys high, it dominates this stretch of the quay, and if you look right up to the top, you can see where a rooftop restaurant now looks out over the harbour.

The building is called Pakhice Santos, after the Brazilian port the coffee came from, and it was built in nineteen oh two, designed by the architects Stok and Kanters. For most of the twentieth century it did the slow, heavy work of the port, storing sacks of beans until they were needed, and it is now a protected monument.

In February twenty twenty six it reopened as the new home of the Nayderlants Fotomuseum, the national museum of photography. The collection runs from dagairotypes of the eighteen forties to the present day, with more than one hundred and seventy five complete archives of Dutch photographers, and inside there are exhibition spaces, a photography library, a working darkroom and conservation rooms you can see into through glass walls.

The heart of the museum is the Gallery of Honour of Dutch Photography, a permanent selection of ninety nine images that together tell the story of photography in the Netherlands, and it is the place to start if you only have a little time.

The change of cargo is the part that stays with me, because a building made to keep a commodity cool and dark now keeps something much more fragile in the same conditions, and the warehouse turns out to be rather good at both.

Afterwards, walk along the Rinehahven quay to Fenix, the migration museum in another old warehouse, or cross into Kahtendrekht, the old sailors' quarter, which has become one of the liveliest eating districts in Rotterdam.
