De Bijenkorf Rotterdam

Bijenkorf means beehive, and the architect took the name so seriously that he wrapped the whole building in honeycomb.

Stand back on the Coolsingel pavement far enough to take in both the department store and the strange, soaring sculpture beside it. The store is the long, near-windowless block clad in pale stone, and the sculpture is the twisting, ribbed tower of bronze-coloured metal rising from a polished black base out towards the street.

The facade was designed by Marcel Breuer, one of the great names of the Bauhaus, and built between 1955 and 1957, clad in travertine laid mostly in a honeycomb pattern. The tiny vertical slit windows are deliberate, because a department store wants its walls for displays rather than views, so Breuer gave the shoppers light where they needed it and the city a sealed, textured box.

The sculpture is by Naum Gabo, the Russian-born constructivist, and it stands more than twenty-six metres tall, built from steel ribs coated in bronze and threaded with black copper wire. It was unveiled in May 1957, made at a steelworks just outside the city, and Rotterdammers have long called it Het Ding, which simply means The Thing.

There is a sad prequel standing just out of sight. The earlier Bijenkorf, designed by Willem Dudok and opened in 1930, was badly damaged in the bombing of 1940 and finally cleared for the metro, so the store in front of you is a statement of confidence from a city still rebuilding itself, choosing the most modern architect it could find.

Walk round the sculpture slowly before you go in, because it changes shape as you move and looks entirely different from each side. Then carry on along the Coolsingel, where the reconstruction-era city keeps unfolding, one bold post-war building after another.
