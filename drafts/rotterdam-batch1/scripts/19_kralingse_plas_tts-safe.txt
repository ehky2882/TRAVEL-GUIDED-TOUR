Krahlingsa Plahs

This lake was dug out by hand, a spadeful at a time, by people who were burning the ground beneath them to keep Rotterdam warm.

From the shore path, the water spreads out wide in front of you, with sailing boats, rowing boats and the occasional swimmer, and woodland wrapping around the far side. Somewhere along the edge of the lake, the sails of two windmills rise a little apart from each other on the shore.

In the seventeenth century, Rotterdam's growing population and its brick and pipe industries needed fuel, and peat dug from this land was the obvious source. The peat was cut away, the holes filled with water, and the lake you are looking at is the result, about a hundred hectares of it, with a walk of around four kilometres to go all the way round.

The woods came later, laid out in the early twentieth century on material dredged from the harbour, so the trees are growing on the port's own leftovers.

As for the mills, de Laylee, the lily, dates from seventeen forty, and de Stair, the star, from eighteen eighty six, and both still grind spices, a survival from the old Dutch trade in pepper, cinnamon and cloves.

A louder memory belongs here too, because in nineteen seventy the woods around the lake hosted the Holland Pop Festival, the first big pop festival in the Netherlands, and a monument in the woods remembers it.

Step back and the pattern is clear: even Rotterdam's nature is man made, and the lake, the forest and the mills, all products of hard work, have somehow become the city's favourite place to do nothing at all.

Walk at least part of the circuit towards the mills, and if you can, time it for late afternoon, when the light comes low across the water and the city skyline appears over the trees.
