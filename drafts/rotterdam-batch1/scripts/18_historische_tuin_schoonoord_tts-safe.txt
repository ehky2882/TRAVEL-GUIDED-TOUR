Historeesa Towin Skonort

Within a few hundred metres of the busiest museums in Rotterdam lies a garden that it is very easy to walk past without suspecting it is there.

Behind a gate on the Keevitslahn, Skonort opens out as a small, quiet landscape of lawns, old trees and water. Once through the entrance, a winding path leads on ahead, mature cedars and beeches stand on either side, and the pond lies somewhere just beyond, with the noise of the city already muffled by the planting.

The land was meadow outside the city until about seventeen hundred, when it was protected by dykes, and a country estate called Rust en Lust was established here in seventeen oh six. It took the name Skonort in eighteen sixteen, and in eighteen sixty its owners commissioned the garden you see now, laid out in the English landscape style with the pond, the beech trees and the cedars of Lebanon, which are the stars of the show.

Those cedars spread their branches wide and flat, almost like layered shelves, and give the garden its old world, country house feeling.

The banker Yahkob Mace bought the main part of the estate in nineteen twenty seven, his heirs opened the garden to the public in nineteen seventy three, and it has been a protected national monument since two thousand. There is also a memorial plaque to Dahvit Mace, who was shot here in the garden on Liberation Day in nineteen forty five, and it is worth pausing at it quietly.

More than anything, Skonort gives the city a kind of privacy that has been shared out, so anyone can now sit in what was once a family's own green world.

Leave by the Keevitslahn gate and turn towards the Museumpark, a few minutes away. The contrast between this hidden garden and the bold modern museums down the road is one of the nicest surprises in this part of town.
