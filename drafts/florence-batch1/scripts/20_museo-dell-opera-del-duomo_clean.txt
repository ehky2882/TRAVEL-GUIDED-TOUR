Museo dell'Opera del Duomo

Michelangelo carved the David on this spot. That alone would justify a visit. It's far from the best thing here.

Piazza del Duomo, at the east end, behind the cathedral's great apse. You should be facing a long, sober building on the far side of the square from the dome, at number nine, with the entrance to the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo. Behind you rises the back of Brunelleschi's dome.

This site has belonged to the Opera del Duomo, the office that built and still looks after the cathedral, since twelve ninety-six. Around fifteen hundred, the young Michelangelo worked here on the block of marble that became the David. The museum itself opened in eighteen ninety-one, and between twenty twelve and twenty fifteen it was reimagined by the architect Adolfo Natalini with the studio Guicciardini and Magni. They absorbed an old eighteenth-century theatre next door into the plan, and the museum now has twenty-eight rooms across three floors.

The room to head for is the Sala del Paradiso. It holds a full-scale reconstruction of the cathedral's original medieval front by Arnolfo di Cambio, the one that was pulled down in the sixteenth century and never properly replaced until the eighteen hundreds. Opposite it stand Ghiberti's original bronze doors from the Baptistery, the Gates of Paradise.

The scale is the point. The cathedral front and the Baptistery doors face each other across the room, as they once faced each other across the square outside.

Upstairs, there's Michelangelo's Bandini Pietà, Donatello's wooden Mary Magdalene, and the wooden models and actual tools used to build Brunelleschi's dome.

[beat]

What I love is that this museum doesn't treat the cathedral as finished. It shows you the drafts, the arguments, the tools. You see a building as a long, unfinished conversation.

Afterwards, if your legs are willing, climb the dome itself. The stairs run between the inner and outer shells that you've just seen in the models, and you'll understand exactly how Brunelleschi did it.
