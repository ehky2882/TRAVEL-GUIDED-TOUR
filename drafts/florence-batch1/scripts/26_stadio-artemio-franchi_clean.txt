Stadio Artemio Franchi

Look at the stairs. They spiral up the outside of the stadium in concrete, with nothing holding them but their own shape.

Campo di Marte, the residential district east of the centre. You should be outside the stadium, near the tall, slender tower that marks the main entrance, the Torre di Maratona. From here you'll also see one of the spiral staircases winding up the outside wall.

The stadium was designed by the engineer Pier Luigi Nervi from nineteen twenty-nine, and built in two phases between nineteen thirty and nineteen thirty-two. It opened in September nineteen thirty-one. It was originally named after a Fascist militant, Giovanni Berta, then became simply the municipal stadium after the war, and since nineteen ninety-one it has carried the name of Artemio Franchi, a Florentine football administrator.

Have a look at those staircases first. There are three spiral stairs on the outside of the stadium, and another inside leading to the royal stand. They're made of reinforced concrete, and they seem to coil upwards without any visible support. Then the tower, slim and vertical, a counterweight to the long, low bowl.

Inside, the main stand is covered by a cantilevered canopy, a roof held out over the seats with no columns underneath. It was a big part of why the building made Nervi's name.

[beat]

What I find moving here is that Nervi treated concrete like a material with its own logic. Not an imitation of stone, but something that could curl, reach and float. A football ground became a lesson in engineering for the rest of the century.

The stadium is being renovated, so check what you can see. On your way back into town, you'll cross the Viali, the broad ring road Giuseppe Poggi laid out in the eighteen sixties where the medieval walls had stood. Stop at Piazza della Libertà, where an old city gate and a triumphal arch still stand in the middle of the traffic.
