Lay moo rah tay

For five hundred years, people lived behind these walls who couldn't leave. First they were nuns, then prisoners. Now it's the neighbourhood's living room.

Pyat sa del lay moo rah tay, on vee ah gee bel lee na, east of santa kro chay. You should be in a long, paved courtyard with old buildings around it, and on one side, rows of wooden doors set along open galleries on the upper floors. There's usually a café spilling out into the square.

The name comes from a group of women who had themselves walled in to live as hermits, around thirteen ninety. They were called the moo rah tay, the walled up ones. In fourteen twenty four they moved here, the convent grew large, and many noble Florentine women lived in it until the last nuns left in eighteen seventeen.

Then the complex became a prison. It held men from the eighteen thirties, and it went on as the main prison in Florence until the nineteen eighties. The cells were tiny, less than nine square metres each.

Have a look at those galleries and doors. When the site was restored from two thousand and one, with ren tso pyah no involved in the scheme, the original wooden cell doors and the walkways along the galleries were kept on purpose. So you can stand in this square and look straight at the prison's architecture, still in place.

Today, the upper floors hold around forty five homes of social housing, and the ground floors have shops, offices and exhibition spaces. In twenty eleven the kaf fay let teh rah ryo opened inside the old prison, with talks, concerts and festivals.

And here's the thing. Most cities would have demolished it. Florence kept the walls and let people back in, and the memory became part of the neighbourhood rather than something to forget.

Before you go, have a drink at the kaf fay let teh rah ryo, and look for the old cells that have been kept for exhibitions. Then walk west along vee ah gee bel lee na towards kah za bwo nar ro tee, the house that Michelangelo's family owned.
