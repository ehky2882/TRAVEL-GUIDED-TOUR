Villa Cora

For six years, Florence was the capital of Italy. This villa is one of the things it left behind.

You should be on Viale Machiavelli, the tree-lined road that climbs the hill south of the Boboli Gardens. Look for the gates and the gravel drive, and at the end of it, a pale neoclassical villa standing in its own park.

Florence was capital from eighteen sixty-five to eighteen seventy-one, and the city remade itself to suit. The road you're standing on was part of that, the great boulevard of hills laid out by the architect Giuseppe Poggi. In the late eighteen sixties, Baron Gustave Oppenheim, of the German banking family, commissioned this house for his young bride, Eugenia Fenzi. The exterior was designed by Pietro Comparini, and the interiors were finished in Carrara marble, yellow Siena marble, Murano glass, ebony and silk.

Here's where it gets dramatic. According to the house's own history, the baron came to believe his wife was unfaithful and planned to burn the villa down. He was stopped, left Florence and sold it. The next owner was the Empress Eugénie, widow of Napoleon the Third, who turned it into a meeting place for Florentine society. In eighteen ninety-four it passed to Egidio Cora, and his name is the one that stuck.

So it's a capital-city house in a city that stopped being the capital almost as soon as it was finished. That's the quiet irony of this whole hillside. It was built for a future that moved to Rome.

[beat]

What survives is a rather lovely piece of nineteenth-century confidence, and it's now a hotel, so you can have a drink on the roof terrace and see what the baron saw.

When you leave, carry on up Viale Machiavelli and follow Poggi's road round to Piazzale Michelangelo. It's the same project, the same ambition, and the view at the end is the one he designed the whole road to deliver.
