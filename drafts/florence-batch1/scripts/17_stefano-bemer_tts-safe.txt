Steh fa no bay mair

A pair of shoes made here can take months. They're made inside what used to be a church.

Vee ah dee san nee ko lo, on the south bank, in the quiet stretch just below the old city gate. You should be at number two, outside a tall building with the name steh fa no bay mair on it. Through the windows, you'll see the workshop, wooden lasts, leather and craftsmen at their benches.

Steh fa no bay mair was a largely self taught shoemaker who set up in nineteen eighty three with the aim of making the perfect Italian shoe. He built a following among people who care a great deal about their feet. The actor Daniel Day Lewis is widely reported to have spent months here learning the craft. After bay mair died in twenty twelve, the Florentine tom mah zo meh lah nee, from a family long tied to the leather trade, took the business on. Its workshop is now here.

Have a look at the building itself. It's a converted church, so the workshop has height and light that most workshops can only dream of, and the craft is done in the open, where you can watch it.

The heart of it is bespoke. Your foot is measured and a wooden last is carved to its shape, then the shoe is cut, stitched and built by hand around it. There are ready to wear shoes too now, but the made to measure work is what the name rests on.

And it's rather fitting. A space built for slow, careful ritual is now given over to another kind of slow, careful work.

When you leave, walk up the street to the por ta san nee ko lo, the tall old gate tower standing by itself near the river. From there the ramps climb to pyat sah lay my kel an jelo, or you can wander up the steep little lanes to san mee nyah to.
