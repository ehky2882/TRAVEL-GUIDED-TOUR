Moo zay oh steh fa no bar dee nee

A dealer in antiquities turned a church into a palace, painted the walls an unexpected blue, and then gave the whole lot to the city.

Vee ah day reh nye, on the south bank, beside the little garden of pyat sa dem ee doff. You should be in front of a large, rather severe building at number thirty seven, with the entrance to the moo zay oh steh fa no bar dee nee. The river is just behind you.

Steh fa no bar dee nee, who lived from eighteen thirty six to nineteen twenty two, was one of the most powerful art dealers in Italy, known in his lifetime as the prince of antiquarians. In eighteen eighty one he bought what had been the church and convent of san greh go ryo della pah chay, and turned it into a neo Renaissance palace, reusing old doorways, staircases and pediments he'd collected, and setting up workshops to restore works for sale.

Have a look at the walls inside. They're painted a deep, electric blue, known as bar dee nee blue. His clients loved it. The collector nay lee zhak mar used it in her Paris house, and Isabella Stewart Gardner asked for it in hers. After his death the walls were painted over, and the blue was rediscovered under later paint during restoration.

Then find the covered courtyard on the ground floor, lit from above. There are more than eighty stone and marble coats of arms set into the walls. Many were rescued when the old centre of Florence was demolished between eighteen eighty one and eighteen ninety five, to make way for the pyat sa della reh poob lee ka.

And that's what makes this place so interesting. Florence was knocking down its own past just as bar dee nee was selling it to the world, and he kept some of the pieces for himself.

When you leave, walk a few steps along the river to the pon tay al lay grat syay and look back. Behind the museum, the green slope rising up the hill is the jar dee no bar dee nee, also his, and well worth the climb.
