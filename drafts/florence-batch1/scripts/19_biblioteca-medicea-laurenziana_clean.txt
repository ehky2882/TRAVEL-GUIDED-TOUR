Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana

Michelangelo was asked to design a staircase to a library. What he made is a staircase that seems to pour downhill at you.

The cloister of San Lorenzo, the Medici family church. You should be in the peaceful two-storey cloister beside the basilica. In the far corner, a stair leads up to the upper walk, and from there you enter the library. If you're inside the vestibule already, you'll be standing in a tall grey-and-white room, with a great stone stair filling most of the floor.

The library grew out of the manuscripts Cosimo the Elder began collecting in the fourteen hundreds, and that Lorenzo the Magnificent expanded. It was his nephew Giulio, by then Pope Clement the Seventh, who gave Michelangelo the commission. Work began in fifteen twenty-four and ran until fifteen thirty-four, when Michelangelo left Florence for good. The library finally opened to the public in fifteen seventy-one.

Have a look at the walls of this vestibule. Pairs of columns sit sunk into the wall, as though they've been pushed back into it. Brackets that hold up nothing. Window frames with no windows, framed by pilasters that taper the wrong way, narrower at the bottom. It's every rule of classical architecture, deliberately turned inside out.

Then the stair. Michelangelo first imagined it in walnut. It was built in grey pietra serena in fifteen fifty-nine by Bartolomeo Ammannati, from Michelangelo's model. Three flights, and the central one has oval steps that spill outwards like a slow wave.

[beat]

And here's the thing. This room was meant as a dark prelude. You climb that stair out of the gloom and into the long, calm, light-filled reading room above, with its rows of carved desks. The vestibule is anxiety, the reading room is peace, and Michelangelo designed you through it on purpose.

On your way out, stop in the cloister, where a statue of the historian Paolo Giovio sits in a niche. Then walk round the back of the basilica to Piazza di Madonna degli Aldobrandini, where the Medici Chapels have their entrance. It's the family's other great monument, with Michelangelo's New Sacristy inside.
