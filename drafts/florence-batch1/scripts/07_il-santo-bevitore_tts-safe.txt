Eel sahn toh bev ee tor ay

The name means "the holy drinker". It comes from a novel, and the room has been many things before it was a restaurant.

Vee ah dee sahn toh spee ree toh, in the ol trar no, one block back from the river. You should be outside the restaurant's doorway, at numbers sixty four and sixty six, with the name eel sahn toh bev ee tor ay above it. Through the glass, a long dining room, and at one end a big wall of shelving.

The space has a properly layered history. It began as a coaching inn, a place to stable horses, then became a garage, then a shop selling riding equipment, then a restaurant called ta vair na chek ko bep pay. In the early two thousands three friends took it over, mar ko bal day zee, his sister Martina and the lawyer steh fa no seb ass tee ah nee, and renovated it largely by hand, with family and friends. A local craftsman built the big bookshelf that still defines the room.

Their idea was a bistro, which in Florence at the time was rather new. Good, lighter food rooted in Tuscan tradition, fairly priced wine, and service that's professional without being stiff. The name points the same way. "eel sahn toh bev ee tor ay" is the Italian title of Joseph Roth's last novella, "The Legend of the Holy Drinker", and the restaurant's website still opens with Roth.

Look a few doors along the street. Their wine bar, eel san tee no, opened nearby because the restaurant didn't have a cellar. And round the corner on vee ah dee santa mo na ka is their bakery, sfor no, in the space of the baker who had supplied the neighbourhood for over a century.

So it's less a single restaurant than a small piece of the neighbourhood, kept alive one shopfront at a time.

If you're early for your table, stop at eel san tee no for a glass first. Then, after dinner, walk two minutes to pyat sa sahn toh spee ree toh, where broo nel less kee's plain church front glows under the streetlights.
