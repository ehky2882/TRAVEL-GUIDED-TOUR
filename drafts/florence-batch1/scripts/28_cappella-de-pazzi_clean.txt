Cappella de' Pazzi

A room built on simple whole numbers. Walk in, stand in the middle, and you'll feel the maths before you understand it.

The first cloister of Santa Croce. Go through the basilica's ticketed entrance to the cloister on its south side. At the far end of the lawn, you should see a porch of six slender columns and a small dome. That's the Pazzi Chapel.

It was commissioned by Andrea de' Pazzi, a banker, who offered to rebuild the chapter house of Santa Croce after a fire in fourteen twenty-three. That's the room where the friars met. Look at the low stone bench running round the walls, which is where they sat. It was also the Pazzi family's own chapel. Filippo Brunelleschi is generally thought to have been involved from around fourteen twenty-nine, though historians still argue about how much of the final design is his. He died in fourteen forty-six, long before it was finished.

Now stand in the centre. The space is built on a module of twenty Florentine braccia, an old unit of length, so the main room is essentially a cube topped by a hemisphere. The structure is drawn in grey pietra serena against white plaster, so you can read the geometry like a diagram.

Then look up and around. Twelve glazed terracotta roundels of the Apostles by Luca della Robbia sit in the walls, and four coloured roundels of the Evangelists in the corners of the dome, arranged to be seen from just where you're standing.

[beat]

And here's the thing. In fourteen seventy-eight, the Pazzi family led a plot to murder the Medici, inside the cathedral. It failed, and the family was ruined. The calmest room in Florence was built by the family behind its most violent conspiracy.

Before you leave, go back into the basilica and find Donatello's wooden crucifix in the left transept. According to Vasari, Brunelleschi teased his friend that he'd put a peasant on the cross, then carved his own, for Santa Maria Novella, to show him how.
