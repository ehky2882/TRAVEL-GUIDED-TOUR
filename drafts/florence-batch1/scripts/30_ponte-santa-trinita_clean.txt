People argue about the exact curve of these arches. They've been arguing for four hundred years.

You should be on the riverbank, ideally on the Lungarno Corsini, just downstream of the bridge, looking upstream. Ahead of you is a low, wide bridge of three very flat arches, with a statue standing at each of its four corners. Behind it, further upstream, you'll see the Ponte Vecchio.

The bridge was designed by Bartolomeo Ammannati, for Cosimo the First de' Medici, in the fifteen sixties. Ammannati is thought to have drawn on ideas from Michelangelo. Have a look at the arches. They're unusually flat and elegant, and the shape has been described as close to a catenary, the curve a hanging chain makes, flipped upside down. Nobody quite agrees what it is, which rather adds to the charm.

Then the statues. They're the Four Seasons, added in sixteen oh eight for the wedding of Cosimo the Second.

On the night of the third of August nineteen forty-four, retreating German troops mined and blew up the bridge. After the war, Florence decided to rebuild it exactly as it had been. Work began in nineteen fifty-five, led by Riccardo Gizdulich, with the engineer Emilio Brizzi doing the calculations, and it reopened in nineteen fifty-eight.

And the detail I love most. When the bridge was rebuilt, Spring's head was missing. It was finally found in the river in nineteen sixty-one, a few dozen metres downstream, and it was put back. Her right arm never turned up.

Walk out to the middle of the bridge at sunset and look upstream. The view of the Ponte Vecchio, framed by the river and the houses, is the one most people take from here, and it's best when the light is going.
