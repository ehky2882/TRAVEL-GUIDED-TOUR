Ponte Vecchio

The shops on this bridge used to sell meat. The butchers threw the scraps straight into the river below. That's how the gold got here.

You should be in the middle of the bridge, where the rows of little shops break open on both sides. Stand at the downstream opening, the one with a bronze bust on a tall stone plinth, and face the water. The Arno flows away from you, towards the next bridge, Ponte Santa Trinita. Behind you, across the bridge, the opposite gap is roofed over by a long, plain passageway running along the top of the shops.

A flood swept the previous bridge away in November thirteen thirty-three, and this one was finished around thirteen forty-five. Look at the arches. Three of them, very low and very wide. That was a bold idea for its day, and it's part of why the bridge has come through every flood since.

Now the shops. In the fourteen hundreds the city gave them to butchers and greengrocers, who could tip their waste into the Arno. Owners later built back rooms out over the water, propped on brackets, which is why the bridge looks so wonderfully lumpy from the riverbank. Then, in fifteen ninety-four, Grand Duke Ferdinando de' Medici threw the butchers out and moved the goldsmiths in. The bust beside you is the goldsmith and sculptor Benvenuto Cellini, who learned his trade in this city.

The reason was that passageway above the shops. The Vasari Corridor was built in fifteen sixty-five for Cosimo de' Medici, so the family could walk from their offices in the Palazzo Vecchio to their home at the Palazzo Pitti without meeting the public. A Duke walking over a meat market. The smell had to go.

And here's the thing. On the night of the third of August, nineteen forty-four, the retreating German army blew up every bridge in Florence except this one. They destroyed the streets at either end instead. So the oldest bridge still standing in Florence is the one that was spared.

Before you go, walk to the south end of the bridge and look up at the old stone tower on the corner. The corridor bends round it on brackets instead of going through. The story goes that the family who owned it, the Mannelli, wouldn't give way, even for a Medici. And since December twenty twenty-four, you can walk that corridor yourself, with a ticket from the Uffizi.
