Museo Novecento

The building is a medieval hospital and school. The art inside is twentieth century. And the courtyard carries a sentence in neon.

Piazza Santa Maria Novella. Stand in the square facing the basilica's marble front, then turn right round so the church is behind you. The long arcaded loggia now ahead of you belongs to the former Spedale delle Leopoldine, and the entrance to the Museo Novecento is under it, at number ten.

This was once the Ospedale di San Paolo, later known as the Scuole Leopoldine. It's now a museum of twentieth- and twenty-first-century Italian art, opened by the city in twenty fourteen. Florence is so identified with the Renaissance that a museum like this is a deliberate statement. The city had a modern century too.

Have a look, once you're inside, at the courtyard. The first floor is wrapped by a Renaissance loggia on all four sides, and it carries a neon work by the Florentine artist Maurizio Nannucci. It reads "Everything might be different". It's a good line to meet in a city that rarely lets anything change.

The core of the collection was given to the city in nineteen seventy by the collector Alberto Della Ragione. Two hundred and forty-one works from roughly nineteen twenty to nineteen forty-five, including Morandi, de Chirico, Carrà, Guttuso and Casorati. There's also a story about the flood of nineteen sixty-six. The critic Carlo Ludovico Ragghianti appealed to artists to donate work to help Florence recover, and many answered.

And here's the thing. A city that only looks backwards becomes a stage set. This building quietly argues that Florence is still part of the conversation.

When you leave, cross the square and look at the basilica's façade. Alberti designed its upper half in the fourteen hundreds, and if you've just been looking at twentieth-century geometry inside, you'll suddenly notice how geometric he was too.
