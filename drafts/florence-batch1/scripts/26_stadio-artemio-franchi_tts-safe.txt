Kam po dee mar tay, the residential district east of the centre. You should be outside the stadium, near the tall, slender tower that marks the main entrance, the tor ray dee ma ra toh na. From here you'll also see one of the spiral staircases winding up the outside wall.

The stadium was designed by the engineer pyair loo ee jee nair vee from nineteen twenty nine, and built in two phases between nineteen thirty and nineteen thirty two. It opened in September nineteen thirty one. It was originally named after a Fascist militant, jo vah nee bair ta, then became simply the municipal stadium after the war, and since nineteen ninety one it has carried the name of ar teh myo fran kee, a Florentine football administrator.

Have a look at those staircases first. There are three spiral stairs on the outside of the stadium, and another inside leading to the royal stand. They're made of reinforced concrete, and they seem to coil upwards without any visible support. Then the tower, slim and vertical, a counterweight to the long, low bowl.

Inside, the main stand is covered by a cantilevered canopy, a roof held out over the seats with no columns underneath. It was a big part of why the building made nair vee's name.

What I find moving here is that nair vee treated concrete like a material with its own logic. Not an imitation of stone, but something that could curl, reach and float. A football ground became a lesson in engineering for the rest of the century.

The stadium is being renovated, so check what you can see. On your way back into town, you'll cross the vee ah lee, the broad ring road joo zep pay pod jee laid out in the eighteen sixties where the medieval walls had stood. Stop at pyat sa della lee bair tah, where an old city gate and a triumphal arch still stand in the middle of the traffic.
