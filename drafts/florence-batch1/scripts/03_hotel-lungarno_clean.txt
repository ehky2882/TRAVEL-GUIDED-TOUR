Borgo San Jacopo, south bank, a few steps from the Ponte Vecchio. You should be at the entrance of Hotel Lungarno, on the river side of this narrow street. If you walk onto the bridge and look downstream, along the bank on your left, you'll see the hotel's riverfront rising straight out of the water.

This street has a hard history. In August nineteen forty-four, as the German army retreated, it blew up the bridges of Florence, sparing only the Ponte Vecchio, and destroyed the buildings at either end of it to block the approaches. Borgo San Jacopo took a lot of that damage. So what you're walking through is a patchwork of medieval towers and post-war rebuilding, and it's worth reading it that way.

The hotel itself is the founding property of the Lungarno Collection, which belongs to the Ferragamo family. They bought it in the nineteen nineties. The interiors are by the Florentine architect Michele Bönan, who refurbished them again in twenty seventeen, and the brief was always a private house rather than a grand hotel.

Have a look inside at the art. There are more than four hundred and fifty twentieth-century works hung through the public rooms and bedrooms, among them pieces by Picasso and Jean Cocteau. It's a collection you'd expect in a gallery, hung casually beside armchairs.

And that's rather the Florentine way of doing it. In a city where the Renaissance hangs in museums, the twentieth century hangs in someone's sitting room, and you're invited to have a drink beneath it.

Before you go, walk a hundred metres west along Borgo San Jacopo and look up. Several medieval tower-houses survived the war here, and their narrow, fortified faces sit right beside the post-war buildings. The contrast tells you exactly what happened in nineteen forty-four.
