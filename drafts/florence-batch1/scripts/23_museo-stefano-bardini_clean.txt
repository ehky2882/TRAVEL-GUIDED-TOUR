Museo Stefano Bardini

A dealer in antiquities turned a church into a palace, painted the walls an unexpected blue, and then gave the whole lot to the city.

Via dei Renai, on the south bank, beside the little garden of Piazza Demidoff. You should be in front of a large, rather severe building at number thirty-seven, with the entrance to the Museo Stefano Bardini. The river is just behind you.

Stefano Bardini, who lived from eighteen thirty-six to nineteen twenty-two, was one of the most powerful art dealers in Italy, known in his lifetime as the prince of antiquarians. In eighteen eighty-one he bought what had been the church and convent of San Gregorio della Pace, and turned it into a neo-Renaissance palace, reusing old doorways, staircases and pediments he'd collected, and setting up workshops to restore works for sale.

Have a look at the walls inside. They're painted a deep, electric blue, known as Bardini blue. His clients loved it. The collector Nélie Jacquemart used it in her Paris house, and Isabella Stewart Gardner asked for it in hers. After his death the walls were painted over, and the blue was rediscovered under later paint during restoration.

Then find the covered courtyard on the ground floor, lit from above. There are more than eighty stone and marble coats of arms set into the walls. Many were rescued when the old centre of Florence was demolished between eighteen eighty-one and eighteen ninety-five, to make way for the Piazza della Repubblica.

And that's what makes this place so interesting. Florence was knocking down its own past just as Bardini was selling it to the world, and he kept some of the pieces for himself.

When you leave, walk a few steps along the river to the Ponte alle Grazie and look back. Behind the museum, the green slope rising up the hill is the Giardino Bardini, also his, and well worth the climb.
