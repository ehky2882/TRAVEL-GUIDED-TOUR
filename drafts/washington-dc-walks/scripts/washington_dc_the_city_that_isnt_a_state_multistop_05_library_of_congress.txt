# The Library of Congress

---

You should be on First Street, at the foot of the Library's front steps, with the fountain in front of you. Above it, the steps climb to a long gray granite front with a row of arched windows, and over the middle of it all rises a dark dome with a gold flame on top.

Start with the fountain, which is the most cheerful thing on the Hill. In the middle, a bronze sea god sits on a rock, and around him are his attendants: young men blowing into shells, women riding sea horses, and, along the rim, turtles and frogs spitting water at each other. It went in at the end of the 1890s, along with the building. On a hot day, children stand at the edge and get wet, and nobody stops them.

Then look up at the building. This is the Jefferson Building, the oldest of the Library's three, and it is the one building on this hill that decided to be ornate. The Capitol is white and grave, the Court is white and graver; this one is gray, and covered in carving, with carved faces looking out of the keystones over the windows. It opened in 1897, and it was built to make reading look like the most splendid thing a country could do.

It was built, too, as Congress's own library, and in a formal sense it still is. But anyone sixteen or older can walk in and apply for a reader's card, from this city or from anywhere else, and sit and read under that dome. Free timed passes get you into the public parts of the building on the days it is open.

Of all the national buildings on this walk, this is the one that takes in the people who live next to it on exactly the same terms as everyone else.

Walk south on First Street, with the Library on your left, to Independence Avenue. Turn right, keep the Capitol grounds on your right, and follow the avenue downhill to the glass building on your right at the bottom. That is the next stop.
