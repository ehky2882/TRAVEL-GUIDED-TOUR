You have come down Thomas Jefferson Street, under the elevated highway and past the old brick buildings below it, and you are at the river. Go through the plaza with the fountain to the railing at the water's edge and face the puh toe mik. Virginia is the far bank. To your right the river comes down from under Key Bridge, where you started, past a long green park with stone steps to the water; to your left it goes on under more bridges toward the city. Behind you, a curved complex of apartments and restaurants around the plaza.

This shore was working ground until the nineteen eighties, mills, coal yards, a cement plant, then parking, and the park is newer than most of the people walking in it. The curved buildings behind you keep a wall hidden in their plaza that rises out of the paving when the river comes up. Georgetown still builds for the river first and the view second.

Downriver on your left, past the mouth of Rock Creek, a set of white curved buildings stands on the bank. That is the Watergate. It is a building before it is a word. Across the water, the long wooded island is a park. The tall glass beyond it is roz lin, in Virginia.

Look at the water itself. It moves. Twice a day it rises and falls here, because this is the top of the tide: a few miles upstream the river comes down over rocks, and the ocean's pull reaches this far and no farther. That is the reason there was a town here at all. The sea arrives at the foot of Georgetown, in the form of a river.

Everything you walked past was built to meet this, the trough across the river, the staircase of locks, the stone house on the main street of a town the river made. The plan came later and stopped at the creek. Watch the water for a minute. It is still coming in, or still going out, on a schedule nobody in this city wrote.
