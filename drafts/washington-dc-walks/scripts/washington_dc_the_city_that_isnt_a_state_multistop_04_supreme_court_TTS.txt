The Supreme Court

You should be on the sidewalk on First Street, facing the Supreme Court across its plaza, with the steps and the columns in front of you and the Capitol directly behind you, across the street.

That arrangement is the thing to notice. For more than a century the Court had no building of its own. It met inside the Capitol, in space borrowed from Congress, and it did not move out until nineteen thirty five. When it finally got a building, the building was put here, across a street from the one it had left, and it was turned to face it. The front of the Court looks at the east front of Congress, and Congress looks back.

The building is all white marble, with a pediment full of carved figures over the columns and a pair of seated figures at the foot of the steps, one on each side. It was meant to look as old and settled as the institution inside it, though it is younger than most of the rowhouses you have just walked past.

Of the three branches of the national government, two have their buildings face to face. The president's house is a mile and a half away, behind its own fence, and faces neither of them; the Court and the Congress are across a street from each other, near enough to watch. It is a statement in stone about how the system was supposed to work, each part keeping an eye on the next.

The Library of Congress is across East Capitol Street, on your right as you face the Court: the gray building with the green dome and the fountain at its foot. Walk over to the fountain, on First Street, and stop there.
