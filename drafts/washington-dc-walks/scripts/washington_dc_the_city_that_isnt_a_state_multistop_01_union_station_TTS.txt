Union Station

You should be facing the station now, with its three arches in front of you and the fountain at your back. Along the top of the facade, above the arches, stands a row of carved figures in pale stone, and above them the long flat roofline runs the width of the building.

A great many people who are not from here arrive in Washington through those arches, and the building was made for exactly that. When it opened in nineteen oh seven it gathered the city's railroads into one station, and the people who built it wanted the way in to look like it led somewhere that mattered. The arches are sized for crowds, not for doors. The stone is plain and pale, the way the buildings on the hill ahead of you are plain and pale, so that the first thing a visitor sees already matches the rest.

If the doors are open, the main space inside is worth a minute: a long vaulted ceiling in white and gold, high enough that the noise of the trains and the shops goes up into it and stays there.

Then watch who comes out. On a weekday morning, most of the people coming through these arches are commuters, off trains from Maryland and Virginia, walking to work. By the middle of the day it is visitors, from everywhere else in the country, with luggage and a map. Both groups are arriving at the capital, and the station serves both of them well. What most of them are not is from here.

The rest of this walk goes looking for the people who are, the ones who were already up the hill when the morning trains came in.

Walk back across the circle and take Massachusetts Avenue, the wide street that leaves it on your left as you face away from the station, up the hill. You will pass a small park with benches. Just past it, turn right onto Seventh Street and walk south, across East Capitol Street, to the long brick market on your left. That is the next stop.
