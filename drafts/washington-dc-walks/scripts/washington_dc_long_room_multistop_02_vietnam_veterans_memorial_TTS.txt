You should be at the western tip of the Wall, where the polished black stone is only a few inches high, with the path sloping down to your right and the two arms of the Wall opening ahead of you.

Walk it as the path intends, down into the center and back up the far side. Two things to hold while you go.

The first is where the arms point. The Wall is two walls meeting at an angle, and the designer, a student named my uh lin, aimed one arm at the Monument and the other at the Lincoln Memorial behind you. The memorial does not stand in the room so much as hold it: it reaches back toward the stage you just left and forward toward the far end of the pool, and at the join, where you are lowest, you are standing between the two.

The second is what the surface does. The stone is polished so that you see yourself and the trees and the sky in it, with the names cut through the reflection. It was among the most contested designs of its decade, and the compromise that let it be built added the three bronze soldiers on the rise behind you in nineteen eighty four, and the women's memorial in the trees in nineteen ninety three. The Wall absorbed both. People leave things at its foot: letters, boots, photographs, a can of beer. The things are collected every day and kept.

That is the second use of this room. The Lincoln steps became a stage. The Wall became a surface that people answer, and the answers are the point of it.

Come back up to the lawn and cross in front of the Lincoln steps to the far side of the pool. The Korean War Veterans Memorial is in the trees directly opposite, on the south side.
