You are inside the Roosevelt Memorial, at its south end, with a wall of rough red stone on your left and the Basin through the trees on your right. The wall is a little above head height, rising in places to about twice that, and the rooms it makes have no roof.

You have come in at the wrong end, which is fine. There are openings at both ends and four rooms in a line between them, one for each term, and the loop brings you in at the last. Walk it backward; the scale is the point, not the order.

Look at the figures as you go. The seated man in the cloak, with the dog at his feet, is the size of a seated man. The dog is the size of a dog, with a bronze of his own, a rare thing on a national memorial. The people in line against the wall, the man leaning in to a radio, the couple from the farm: all life size, on the level you are standing on. Most of the words on the walls are at eye height, some also in braille, and the water that runs in every room is close enough to touch. There is nothing here you have to crane your neck for.

Think back to the dock and what you could see from there: the round white memorial, the Monument, the trees, and not this. From across the water this memorial is a dark line under the trees, and from the Inlet Bridge it is nothing at all. It chose to be no taller than the people in it.

That choice is this walk's idea made into a building: a city that stays below its trees, and a memorial that stays below its visitors.

Walk on through the rooms to the north end and out onto the path, with the water on your right again. The Martin Luther King Junior Memorial is a few minutes ahead, a split white stone you walk through to the shore. Go through to the plaza at the water's edge and turn around to face the stone.
