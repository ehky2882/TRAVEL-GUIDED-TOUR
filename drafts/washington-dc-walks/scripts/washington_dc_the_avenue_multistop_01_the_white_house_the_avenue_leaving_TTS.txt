You should be at the northeast corner of Fifteenth and Pennsylvania, with the Treasury's long north side across the street to your right and the closed block of the avenue running away west in front of you. Look down it. Barriers at this end, then a wide empty street, then the north front of the White House at the far end, with the square to its right.

That is the avenue as the plan laid it out, and it is the only block of it you cannot drive. For most of two centuries this was an ordinary street with traffic on it, and the house had a public road running past its front steps, which is a strange thing for the home of a head of state and was more or less the idea. It was closed to cars in nineteen ninety five, and what you are looking at is a street turned into a forecourt, with its cut end where you are standing.

The house was begun in seventeen ninety two, before the avenue existed as anything but a surveyor's line, and it was built, like the Capitol, with the labor of enslaved people alongside hired hands. When the second president moved in, in November of eighteen hundred, the plaster was still wet and the avenue outside was stumps and mud. The connection to the Capitol was real on paper and barely real underfoot.

Turn and look the other way, east and south. The avenue cannot go straight from here, because the Treasury is in the way, so it drops two blocks down Fifteenth and picks up again at the Treasury's far corner. That is the direction the inaugural parades have come from since eighteen oh five, when a president rode back along it from the Capitol after taking the oath and the ones after him kept doing it. Every one of those parades ends about where you are standing, turning in at the house.

Walk south on Fifteenth, two blocks, with the Treasury on your right, until the avenue opens up again on your left. The next stop is the wide stone plaza a block ahead on the left side, Freedom Plaza.
