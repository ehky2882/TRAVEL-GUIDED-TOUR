You should be at the top of the steps, on the wide landing in front of the columns, with the seated figure in the chamber behind you and the pool below.

This is the stage, and you are standing on it. Look down the steps at the paved terrace, the pool, the walks along it, and the lawns beyond. Every big crowd this city has drawn has filled that view: the Easter Sunday concert of nineteen thirty nine, when a singer refused a hall downtown was given these steps instead and seventy five thousand people came to listen, and the afternoon in August nineteen sixty three when a quarter of a million people stood where you are looking and heard one of the most quoted speeches in American history given from roughly where you stand. Both crowds faced this way. Both speakers faced that way. The building was designed as a temple, and the temple became a platform the day the first crowd arrived.

An inscription cut into one of the landings marks where the nineteen sixty three speaker stood. It is a few steps down, and people find it without looking very hard.

Here is what to notice about the view from up here. The pool points at the Monument. The Monument marks the crossing of the city's plan. So this platform is aimed, by design, at the middle of Washington, and anyone speaking from it speaks down the length of the room toward it. The architects who set the building here in the nineteen twenties were finishing a vista. The crowds turned the vista into a hall.

Go down the steps and bear left at the bottom, to the north, along the line of trees. The next memorial is set below the level of the lawn, so you will not see it until you are nearly on it. That is the Vietnam Veterans Memorial.
