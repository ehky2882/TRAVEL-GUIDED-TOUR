# Eastern Market

---

You should be on Seventh Street, in front of a long, low brick building with a row of tall arched windows down its side. That is Eastern Market. If it is a weekend, the sidewalk and the open ground beside the building will be full of tables, with produce and crafts and a crowd; on a weekday, the trade is indoors.

Go in if the doors are open. It is one long space under a high iron roof with skylights, and down both sides run the counters: a butcher, fish on ice, cheese, bread, flowers. The building went up in 1873, and selling food to the neighbors is the job it was built for. There has never been much else to it, which is the point of it.

Everything else on this walk is here because the country put it here: the station, the court, the library, the glass house, the building at the end. This is the one stop that is here because people live on this hill and need to buy dinner. Look at what is around it when you come back out. Rowhouses, mostly, in brick and painted brick, with small front yards behind iron fences; churches; corner shops; a school. This is a neighborhood in a city with a long life of its own: a city that for about half a century had a Black majority, with its own music, its own accents and its own way of talking about itself, none of which the station was built to show anyone.

[beat]

The people who shop at these counters are the people this walk is about. They are the ones who live under the arrangement the last stop will describe, and they do it the way people anywhere do, by getting on with things: a pound of something from the butcher on a Saturday, a chat at the counter, a bag carried home along the block.

Walk back up Seventh Street to East Capitol Street, the wide one you crossed, and turn left. The next segment plays as you walk.
