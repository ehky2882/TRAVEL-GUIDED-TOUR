You should be on the south sidewalk of Pennsylvania Avenue at Twelfth Street, looking north across the traffic at a building that does not match anything near it. Rough gray stone, round arches, a steep roof, and a square tower that goes up a little over three hundred feet with a clock near the top. Everything else on this stretch is pale, flat and horizontal. This is not.

It opened in eighteen ninety nine as the city's post office, and within a generation the people planning the avenue had decided it was a mistake. The buildings going up around it were meant to be low, classical and matched, and this thing stood in the middle of them like a visitor who had not got the message. It was supposed to come down. It did not, mostly because the money for the demolition never came together, and by the time it did, in the nineteen seventies, enough people had decided they liked it that it was saved and converted instead. It became a hotel in twenty sixteen, and the public can still go up the tower most days, for a clear view back along the way you came.

Stand here a moment and look at the length of the avenue in both directions. Nothing else between the house and the hill goes up like this.So for a mile of street, this is the one vertical, and you will find yourself steering by it for the rest of the walk, the way people in this city have for more than a century.

Walk on east, on this side of the avenue. The sidewalk is wide and the buildings on your right are big and matched. In about five blocks the avenue reaches Seventh Street, and the long columned building on your right just before it, with a flight of steps up to a dark bronze door, is the National Archives. Stop at the foot of those steps.
