This whole neighbourhood is named after a juniper bush that was never really there, thanks to a slip of the ear between two languages.

Luotsikatu, Pilot Street, is the one to find, here in the old Art Nouveau quarter of Katajanokka, and around you the apartment blocks rise in pastel render and rough-cut granite, with turrets, bay windows and steep roofs crowding the skyline above the street corners.

The name means Juniper Point in Finnish, but the story goes that Finnish speakers misheard the Swedish word for a point, skata, as kataja, their own word for juniper, and the mistake simply stuck.

Look for number one Luotsikatu, the Tallberg House, finished in 1898, which was the first competition win for the young trio of Herman Gesellius, Armas Lindgren and Eliel Saarinen, who went on to design the National Museum and, in Saarinen's case, the Central Station. They used its triangular plot to make a miniature castle, with towering slate roofs and a whiplash curve of wrought iron on the main door.

Across the street at number two, the Aeolus House of 1903 by Selim A. Lindqvist takes its name from the Greek keeper of the winds, and its decoration alludes to wind as well, above a stone ground floor, green plaster and a corner turret topped by a pointed spire.

Katajanokka's real distinction is the speed of it, because at the end of the nineteenth century this was a district of shacks, and within roughly a decade stone houses had replaced them, so the quarter reads as one coherent piece, as though a single generation of architects had been handed an island to play with.

Follow Luotsikatu down to the northern shore, where Finland's state icebreakers tie up between their winter tours of duty, and then circle back along the water towards the red-brick Uspenski Cathedral at the western tip of the island.
