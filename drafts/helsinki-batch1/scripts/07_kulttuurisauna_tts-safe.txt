On a still evening the only sounds here are the hiss of water on hot stone and the occasional splash of someone lowering themselves into the sea.

Hakka nyemen ranta, the waterside path below the concrete terraces of Merri haka, brings you right to it, a low white building at the water's edge with dark wooden doors and a squat concrete pyramid rising at one corner, and with a way down into the water for swimmers.

Kooltoori sowna, the Culture Sauna, was designed by a Finnish and Japanese couple, the architects Twomas Toyvonen and Nehneh Tsooboy, who still run it themselves, and it opened in May twenty thirteen. It was the first new public sauna Helsinki had seen in decades, and the couple set out to make a place where a Finnish sauna and a Japanese bathhouse could meet.

The stove is the first surprise, a wood fired concrete cube with a small hollow in its top, and rather than flinging a ladle of water at it, you pour slowly, so the steam arrives with a deep hiss and spreads gently through the room. The second is that pyramid outside, cast in untreated concrete as a nod to the hard, grey materials of the neighbourhood around it.

The third is a house rule called sowna rowha, or sauna peace, which means conversation belongs in the salon, the courtyard or outdoors and never in the bathing room, groups of more than two aren't admitted, and Friday evenings are held as silent sessions.

And that's the thought behind the whole building, I think, since a sauna is one of the few places left where strangers sit side by side with nothing to look at and nothing to do, and this one has been designed to protect that stillness rather than sell it.

Mornings from Friday to Sunday are walk in, while evenings from Wednesday to Sunday need booking, and first timers are encouraged to come alone; afterwards, follow the shore path west towards Hakka nyemi and finish with something warm from the old market hall.
