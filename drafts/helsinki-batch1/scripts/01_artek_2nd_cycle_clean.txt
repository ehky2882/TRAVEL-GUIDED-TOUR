The chairs in this shop are valued for precisely the things most shops would try to hide: the scuffs, the scratches and the faint rings left by decades of other people's coffee cups.

Pieni Roobertinkatu is the short side street you've turned into, a block south of the Esplanadi, and the shopfront at numbers four to six belongs to Artek 2nd Cycle, its window usually holding a few stools and chairs whose birch has mellowed to the colour of honey.

Artek itself was founded in Helsinki in 1935 by Alvar and Aino Aalto with two partners, and its name was coined to bring art and technology together. Look in the window for its best-known product, the Stool 60, a round seat on three bent birch legs that Alvar Aalto designed in the early 1930s and that the company still makes today.

The idea of selling these pieces second-hand took shape around 2006, and the British designer Tom Dixon, then working with Artek, helped launch it: over roughly six months the team gathered about four hundred used Aalto pieces from old people's homes, factories, schools and libraries across Finland. When the first batch went on show in London in 2007, every chair carried a radio tag that a mobile phone could read, linking it to an online record of its own past.

This shop opened in 2011 to mark the company's seventy-sixth birthday, a pleasingly unround anniversary, and its stock now reaches well beyond Aalto, to Finnish lighting, carpets, ceramics and prints from the early twentieth century to the present.

What the shop is really selling, when you think about it, is time, because a new stool leaves the factory looking exactly like every other, while one that has spent forty years in a school canteen has gathered a character no workshop could fake, and the team reconditions each piece gently rather than sanding that history away.

The shop keeps its doors open on Thursdays and Fridays, or by appointment, and if you'd like to see Aalto furniture in its original setting, walk five minutes north to the South Esplanade, where the Savoy restaurant still occupies the rooms the Aaltos furnished in 1937.
