It's Christmas 1897, and the first traders are setting out their stalls on a brand-new square, laid on land that had only recently been reclaimed from the water.

Hakaniementori spreads out around you, a broad cobbled square framed by lime trees, and along one edge stands the long red-brick market hall, while a short way to the south-west, across the road, the tower of Paasitorni, the old Workers' House, rises above the rooftops.

The square was laid out on the strip of land that once separated the islet of Siltasaari from the mainland, with food stalls joined over the years by tailors and cloth sellers and, from the 1920s, by fishmongers, and today trade still carries on here every weekday.

The market hall opened on the first of June 1914, designed by Einar Flinckenberg and praised at the time as the largest and most modern in Europe, with grocers downstairs and more than a hundred little shops for cloth, leather and handicrafts upstairs. It closed for renovation in 2018 and reopened in April 2023, with its interior restored close to how it first looked.

Paasitorni carries a different kind of history, because on the evening of the twenty-sixth of January 1918 a red lantern was raised on its tower, a signal widely regarded as the start of the Finnish Civil War, and this square has hosted May Day marches and demonstrations for as long as the workers' movement has made Hakaniemi its home.

Put the two halves of the square together and they make perfect sense, because Hakaniemi has always been where the northern working districts came to buy their potatoes and herring, and also where they came to make themselves heard, so the shopping and the marching have shared these cobbles for well over a century.
