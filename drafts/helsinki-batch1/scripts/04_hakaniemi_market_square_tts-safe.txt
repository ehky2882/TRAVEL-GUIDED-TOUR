It's Christmas eighteen ninety seven, and the first traders are setting out their stalls on a brand new square, laid on land that had only recently been reclaimed from the water.

Hakka nyementori spreads out around you, a broad cobbled square framed by lime trees, and along one edge stands the long red brick market hall, while a short way to the south west, across the road, the tower of Pahsi torni, the old Workers' House, rises above the rooftops.

The square was laid out on the strip of land that once separated the islet of Silta sahri from the mainland, with food stalls joined over the years by tailors and cloth sellers and, from the nineteen twenties, by fishmongers, and today trade still carries on here every weekday.

The market hall opened on the first of June nineteen fourteen, designed by Ayenar Flinkenberg and praised at the time as the largest and most modern in Europe, with grocers downstairs and more than a hundred little shops for cloth, leather and handicrafts upstairs. It closed for renovation in twenty eighteen and reopened in April twenty twenty three, with its interior restored close to how it first looked.

Pahsi torni carries a different kind of history, because on the evening of the twenty sixth of January nineteen eighteen a red lantern was raised on its tower, a signal widely regarded as the start of the Finnish Civil War, and this square has hosted May Day marches and demonstrations for as long as the workers' movement has made Hakka nyemi its home.

Put the two halves of the square together and they make perfect sense, because Hakka nyemi has always been where the northern working districts came to buy their potatoes and herring, and also where they came to make themselves heard, so the shopping and the marching have shared these cobbles for well over a century.

Head inside the hall and climb to the upper floor, where the textile and craft stalls still follow the spirit of the original plan, and if you're here on the first Sunday of the month, give yourself an hour for the fair outside.
