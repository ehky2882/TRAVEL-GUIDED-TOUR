A glass vase that now sits in homes all over the world took its name from the restaurant at the top of this building.

The sober block in front of you, number fourteen on the South Esplanade, was built for the Ahlstrurm industrial company in the nineteen thirties, and the Savoy restaurant fills its top two floors, with terraces looking out across the Esplanahdee park and the rooftops of the city centre.

The restaurant opened in nineteen thirty seven, and the vision behind it came from Myreh Goolixen, an heiress of the Ahlstrurm family and a co founder of Artek, who wanted a dining room that showed off modern Finnish design. Eyeno and Alvar Aalto designed the interior, with walls of Oregon pine, elm wood armchairs by Eyeno, and above the tables a brass pendant lamp Alvar made for the room, which became known as the Golden Bell.

Then there's the vase, because in nineteen thirty six Aalto entered a glass competition with a series of wavy, free form vessels, and after they were shown at the Paris World Exposition in nineteen thirty seven, one of them took its place in this restaurant and has been called the Savoy vase ever since.

Field Marshal Mannerheim was a regular here, and some of his favourites are still on the menu, including forshmak, a rich dish of minced meat and herring, and a shot of spirits poured so generously that it arrives filled right to the brim.

Step back and it's the homeliness that stands out, because the designer Ilse Crawford, who refreshed the rooms in twenty twenty, described the Aaltos approach as creating warm, domestic spaces, and that's exactly the trick: a grand restaurant at the top of a corporate headquarters, furnished like a well loved house.

Even without a table booked, you can spot the terraces from the park below, and then stroll the length of the Esplanahdee towards the Market Square and the harbour, about five minutes away, past the bandstand and the cafes under the trees.
