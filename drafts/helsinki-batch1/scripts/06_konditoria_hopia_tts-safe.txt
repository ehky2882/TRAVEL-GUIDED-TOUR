Some cafes chase every new trend, but this one has stayed so faithful to its recipes and its furniture that it has come all the way round to feeling fashionable again.

Pohyoynen Hesperiankatoo cuts through the solid apartment blocks of Taka Turlur, and the small cafe at number nine is the one you're after, with room inside for only around ten people, so you may well find a short queue at the counter.

Konditoria Hopia has been baking here since nineteen forty nine, and the recipe behind its most famous product came from further east, since according to the city's own guide it was brought to Helsinki by Emma Hopia from Sortavala, a town on the shores of Lake Ladoga in Karelia that has been on the Russian side of the border since the Second World War.

That product is the Karelian pie, a thin rye crust folded around a filling of porridge, traditionally eaten warm with butter mixed with chopped egg, and Hopia is particularly admired for its barley version. The cinnamon buns are made to a recipe that hasn't changed in more than sixty years, and the coffee menu is refreshingly short, so don't come looking for a latte.

Then have a look at the room itself, which is decorated with Karelian style woodwork, and at the benches, where lines from the Kalevala, Finland's national epic, were painted on long ago and have been left undisturbed ever since.

In a sense, you're tasting a piece of a region that Finland no longer holds, because Karelian baking spread across the country with the people who moved west from that border region, and places like this have kept it going so steadily that the pie now feels simply Finnish, eaten in kitchens everywhere.

Hopia opens on weekdays only, from early morning until late afternoon, so take a warm pie with you if the tables are full, and walk about fifteen minutes west to Sibelius Park, where the steel pipe monument to the composer stands among the trees.
