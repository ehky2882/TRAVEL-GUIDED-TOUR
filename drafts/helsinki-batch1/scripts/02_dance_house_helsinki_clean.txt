Finland's dancers waited a very long time for a home of their own, and when one finally arrived in 2022, it was attached to a factory that once made cables.

Kaapeliaukio, the open square at the edge of the old Cable Factory, is where you've arrived, with the factory's long, pale industrial bulk stretching away on one side and, beside it, the Dance House itself, a newer block wrapped in rust-red steel, mirror-bright metal and a shimmering skin of small aluminium discs.

Start with the factory, which the architect Wäinö Gustaf Palmqvist designed for Nokia and which went up in stages between 1939 and 1954 to make cables, many of them for use at sea. When production wound down, artists drifted into the empty halls, and the city backed turning the whole complex into a cultural centre, now the largest in Finland.

The Dance House, designed by JKMM Architects together with ILO Architects, opened on the second of February 2022 as the first extension in the factory's history. Inside there are two stages, the seven-hundred-seat Erkko Hall, which is entirely new, and the smaller Pannu Hall, whose name recalls the boiler that once stood in that part of the factory.

Now look again at the outside, because the architects compared the building to a kite, and they hung its heavy steel walls so that they seem to float just above the ground, while on another side hundreds of aluminium discs catch the light like sequins on a costume.

That's the clever trick of the design, really: tonnes of steel made to look weightless, which is more or less what a dancer spends years of training learning to do with a body, so the building quietly performs the art it was built to house.

Step into the glass-covered courtyard that serves as the shared lobby, and look for Toinen, The Other, an interactive light work by Eetu Huhtala in the entrance foyer, before wandering on through the Cable Factory's long corridors of studios and galleries.
