Until 2001, swimsuits were not allowed in this swimming pool at all.

Head for number twenty-one on Yrjönkatu, in the middle of the city centre, where a new artwork called Water Bodies by Noora Sandgren, six photographs printed on glass, now greets you at the entrance.

The hall opened on the fourth of June 1928 as Finland's first public swimming hall, designed by Väinö Vähäkallio in the restrained classicism of the 1920s, the first architectural style of newly independent Finland, and he drew ideas from Stockholm's Centralbadet and from the spa traditions of Europe.

Inside, a twenty-five-metre pool sits in a long, tiled hall with galleries above it and a café upstairs looking down over the water, and in 1931 Toivo Reingoldt set a world record here in the five-hundred-metre breaststroke, becoming the first Finnish swimmer to set a world record.

The rules are as distinctive as the room, since women and men still swim on separate days, women on Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays and Sundays and men on the other three, and swimsuits are entirely optional, so you may bathe in one or in nothing at all.

There are several saunas, including a traditional wood-heated one, and the restorers kept the 1920s ceramic tiles and the character of the spaces, after the city's deputy mayor for culture asked, in effect, that nobody change the building's soul.

Seen that way, the hall is less a gym than a civic ritual, a stately room where bathing is slow and unselfconscious, and nearly a century on it still invites you to treat a swim as an occasion rather than a workout.
