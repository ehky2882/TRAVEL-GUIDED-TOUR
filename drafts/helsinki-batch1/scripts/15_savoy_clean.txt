A glass vase that now sits in homes all over the world took its name from the restaurant at the top of this building.

The sober block in front of you, number fourteen on the South Esplanade, was built for the Ahlström industrial company in the 1930s, and the Savoy restaurant fills its top two floors, with terraces looking out across the Esplanadi park and the rooftops of the city centre.

The restaurant opened in 1937, and the vision behind it came from Maire Gullichsen, an heiress of the Ahlström family and a co-founder of Artek, who wanted a dining room that showed off modern Finnish design. Aino and Alvar Aalto designed the interior, with walls of Oregon pine, elm-wood armchairs by Aino, and above the tables a brass pendant lamp Alvar made for the room, which became known as the Golden Bell.

Then there's the vase, because in 1936 Aalto entered a glass competition with a series of wavy, free-form vessels, and after they were shown at the Paris World Exposition in 1937, one of them took its place in this restaurant and has been called the Savoy vase ever since.

Field Marshal Mannerheim was a regular here, and some of his favourites are still on the menu, including vorschmack, a rich dish of minced meat and herring, and a shot of spirits poured so generously that it arrives filled right to the brim.

Step back and it's the homeliness that stands out, because the designer Ilse Crawford, who refreshed the rooms in 2020, described the Aaltos' approach as creating warm, domestic spaces, and that's exactly the trick: a grand restaurant at the top of a corporate headquarters, furnished like a well-loved house.

Even without a table booked, you can spot the terraces from the park below, and then stroll the length of the Esplanadi towards the Market Square and the harbour, about five minutes away, past the bandstand and the cafés under the trees.
