Museo Egizio

The oldest museum in the world devoted entirely to ancient Egypt isn't in Cairo, London or Paris, but here, behind this baroque brick front in the middle of Turin.

The long brick building at number six Via Accademia delle Scienze, beside Piazza Carignano, is the Museo Egizio, with its main entrance on the street. It shares the building with the Academy of Sciences, so the whole block rising above you belongs to learning of one kind or another.

The building has a history of its own, having been built in 1679 as the Collegio dei Nobili, a school for the sons of the aristocracy, and the museum came into being after King Carlo Felice bought the great collection of Bernardino Drovetti in 1824, the founding moment of the Museo Egizio.

One man did more than anyone to fill it further. Ernesto Schiaparelli, director in the early twentieth century, led excavations in Egypt that brought around thirty thousand objects back to Turin between 1903 and 1937, and today the collection runs to roughly forty thousand pieces across more than twelve thousand square metres.

Inside, the gallery of statues is the moment most visitors remember. In 2006 the film designer Dante Ferretti, who worked with Fellini and Scorsese, rearranged the great figures of kings and gods in dim light against mirrors, so that they seem to loom out of the dark.

The French scholar Champollion, who deciphered hieroglyphs, is said to have remarked that the road to Memphis and Thebes passes through Turin, and that still holds. A city in the far north-west of Italy became one of the world's great places for ancient Egypt, almost by accident of royal shopping.
