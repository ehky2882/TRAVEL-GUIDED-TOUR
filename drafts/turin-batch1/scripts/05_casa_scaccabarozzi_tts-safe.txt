Casa Skackabarottsee

The building in front of you is nine floors tall, and at its narrowest point it measures just fifty four centimetres across.

Find the corner of Corso San Mowreetseeo and Veeah Joolya dee Barolo, in the Vankeelya neighbourhood, and face the narrow end of the tall, pale yellow block standing there, so that the whole thing tapers away from you like a wedge sliced off the end of the street.

Look closely at that narrow end, because it explains the nickname. Turin calls this the Fetta dee Polenta, the slice of polenta, for its colour and its shape; it widens to just over four metres at the far end, and it runs sixteen metres along Veeah Joolya dee Barolo.

Now lift your eyes up the facade to the roofline. The architect was Alessandro Antonellee, the man who built the Mohleh, and he was given this awkward triangle of land as part of his payment for planning the Vankeelya district in the eighteen forties. Unable to buy the plot next door, he simply went upwards, starting in eighteen forty and adding the top floor in eighteen eighty one, until he had seven storeys above ground and two below.

The building's official name honours his wife, Franchesska Skackabarottsee, and it's said that Antonellee moved in himself to convince doubtful neighbours that it wouldn't fall down, which it never has; it came through the explosion of the gunpowder store in Borgo Dorah in eighteen fifty two, the earthquake of eighteen eighty seven and the bombing of the Second World War without serious damage.

Antonellee spent his career treating limits as a dare, and this is the Mohleh's instinct turned sideways: one building shot up into the sky, while this one squeezed a whole house into a sliver of leftover land.

It's a private home, so admire it from the pavement. Then head a few streets west along Corso San Mowreetseeo, watching for the spire of the Mohleh rising over the rooftops, the bigger sibling of the slice you've just seen.
