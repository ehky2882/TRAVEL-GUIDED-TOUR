Kaffeh Plahttee

On the first of November eighteen ninety seven, a group of schoolboys from the nearby Dazzelyo high school decided to start a football club, and the story goes that they did it right in front of these windows; the club became Juventus.

Two broad avenues meet at this corner — Corso Veettoreeo Emmanwelleh the Second and Corso Reh Oombairto — and Plahttee occupies the angle between them, with its name in gold above long windows. Through the glass you'll be able to make out chandeliers, mirrors and a long wooden counter.

Take a good look at the furnishings, because many of them have been here for generations. The cafe opened in eighteen seventy five as a liquor shop called Preencheepeh Oombairto, and the brothers Airnesto and Peeyetro Plahttee turned it into the room you can see now; much of the furniture is original Louis the Sixteenth style by the Turin firm Vahlahbraygah, and the inlaid wooden counter dates from the nineteen twenties.

Picture, too, the people who used to sit at these tables. The writer Chayzareh Pavayzeh met the publisher Joolyo Eyenowdee here, the economist Looeejee Eyenowdee, later President of Italy, was a customer, and so was the industrialist Jovahnnee Anyellee, which makes this corner a fair snapshot of twentieth century Turin: books, money, cars and, out on the pavement, football.

Here's what strikes me about Plahttee: it grew up alongside the institutions people now associate with this city, and it never needed to shout about it. The chandeliers and the counter did the talking, and they still do, a hundred and fifty years on.

Go in for the house cake, the Torta Plahtteena, or one of the soft trahmetseenee made fresh each day. Then take a stroll along Corso Reh Oombairto, where trees line the avenue, and imagine those schoolboys arguing over what colour their shirts should be.
