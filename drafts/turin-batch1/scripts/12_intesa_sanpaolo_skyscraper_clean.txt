Intesa Sanpaolo Skyscraper

This tower is a hundred and sixty-six metres tall, and that number was very carefully judged, because the Mole Antonelliana, the symbol of the city, stands at a hundred and sixty-seven.

The glass tower rising from the raised plaza beside Corso Inghilterra is the one you want, with the curved glass roof of Porta Susa station just to the north-east and the tower's broad faces turned east and west. Right at the top you can make out a crown of glass and green, and that's a greenhouse.

Look first at those broad faces, which have a double skin of glass with rows of glass louvres inside it that open and close according to the weather outside, letting the building breathe rather than relying entirely on air conditioning; the south face, meanwhile, carries around sixteen hundred square metres of solar panels.

Raise your eyes to the summit, where the upper three floors form a bioclimatic greenhouse, a garden in the sky, with a restaurant on the thirty-fifth floor, exhibition space above it and a panoramic terrace at the very top that looks out to the Alps on clear days.

Down at the base sits the auditorium, which seats three hundred and sixty-four people and can be rearranged into a concert hall or an exhibition space.

Renzo Piano's studio won the competition in 2006 and finished the building in 2015, and its gentle deference to the Mole is the most telling thing about it. A bank's headquarters could easily have tried to dominate the skyline; instead, it stops just short and lets the nineteenth-century landmark keep the crown.

If you'd like to see that greenhouse up close, the restaurant and lounge bar at the top can be booked. Otherwise, walk round the plaza to the far side and look up the full height of the glass, where the louvres catch the light.
