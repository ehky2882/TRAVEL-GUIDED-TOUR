Caffè Platti

On the first of November 1897, a group of schoolboys from the nearby D'Azeglio high school decided to start a football club, and the story goes that they did it right in front of these windows; the club became Juventus.

Two broad avenues meet at this corner — Corso Vittorio Emanuele the Second and Corso Re Umberto — and Platti occupies the angle between them, with its name in gold above long windows. Through the glass you'll be able to make out chandeliers, mirrors and a long wooden counter.

Take a good look at the furnishings, because many of them have been here for generations. The café opened in 1875 as a liquor shop called Principe Umberto, and the brothers Ernesto and Pietro Platti turned it into the room you can see now; much of the furniture is original Louis the Sixteenth style by the Turin firm Valabrega, and the inlaid wooden counter dates from the nineteen twenties.

Picture, too, the people who used to sit at these tables. The writer Cesare Pavese met the publisher Giulio Einaudi here, the economist Luigi Einaudi, later President of Italy, was a customer, and so was the industrialist Giovanni Agnelli, which makes this corner a fair snapshot of twentieth-century Turin: books, money, cars and, out on the pavement, football.

Here's what strikes me about Platti: it grew up alongside the institutions people now associate with this city, and it never needed to shout about it. The chandeliers and the counter did the talking, and they still do, a hundred and fifty years on.

[beat]

Go in for the house cake, the Torta Plattina, or one of the soft tramezzini made fresh each day. Then take a stroll along Corso Re Umberto, where trees line the avenue, and imagine those schoolboys arguing over what colour their shirts should be.
