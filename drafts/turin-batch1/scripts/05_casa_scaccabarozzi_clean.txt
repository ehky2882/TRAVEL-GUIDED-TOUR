Casa Scaccabarozzi

The building in front of you is nine floors tall, and at its narrowest point it measures just fifty-four centimetres across.

Find the corner of Corso San Maurizio and Via Giulia di Barolo, in the Vanchiglia neighbourhood, and face the narrow end of the tall, pale yellow block standing there, so that the whole thing tapers away from you like a wedge sliced off the end of the street.

Look closely at that narrow end, because it explains the nickname. Turin calls this the Fetta di Polenta, the slice of polenta, for its colour and its shape; it widens to just over four metres at the far end, and it runs sixteen metres along Via Giulia di Barolo.

Now lift your eyes up the facade to the roofline. The architect was Alessandro Antonelli, the man who built the Mole, and he was given this awkward triangle of land as part of his payment for planning the Vanchiglia district in the eighteen forties. Unable to buy the plot next door, he simply went upwards, starting in 1840 and adding the top floor in 1881, until he had seven storeys above ground and two below.

The building's official name honours his wife, Francesca Scaccabarozzi, and it's said that Antonelli moved in himself to convince doubtful neighbours that it wouldn't fall down, which it never has; it came through the explosion of the gunpowder store in Borgo Dora in 1852, the earthquake of 1887 and the bombing of the Second World War without serious damage.

Antonelli spent his career treating limits as a dare, and this is the Mole's instinct turned sideways: one building shot up into the sky, while this one squeezed a whole house into a sliver of leftover land.

[beat]

It's a private home, so admire it from the pavement. Then head a few streets west along Corso San Maurizio, watching for the spire of the Mole rising over the rooftops, the bigger sibling of the slice you've just seen.
