Moozayo Natsyonahleh del Reesorjeemento Eetalyahno

Inside this building there's a parliament chamber where a clock and a calendar still record the moment of its last session: half past two in the afternoon, on the twenty eighth of December eighteen sixty.

King Karlo Albairto sits on horseback in the middle of the square around you, and the vast building along one side is the back of Palatso Kareenyahno, its grand nineteenth century facade lined with columns. The entrance to the Moozayo Natsyonahleh del Reesorjeemento Eetalyahno is here, at number eight Piazza Karlo Albairto.

This facade is the first thing to take in. The wing was built after eighteen sixty one to house the hall of the first Italian parliament, but the capital moved to Florence in eighteen sixty five and the hall was never used for its purpose, which makes this whole grand front a monument to a plan that changed.

On the far side is the older building. The front of Palatso Kareenyahno, facing Piazza Kareenyahno, was designed by Gwareeno Gwareenee and begun in sixteen seventy nine, with a famous curving facade in brick, and in eighteen forty eight its central hall became the chamber of the Subalpine Parliament, the elected assembly of the Kingdom of Sardinia.

That chamber is Room sixteen of the museum, and it's described as the only parliamentary chamber from eighteen forty eight still intact in Europe. The benches are ivory coloured, the boxes are lined with red velvet, and figures like Kavoor, Jobairtee and Dazzelyo sat here while the case for a united Italy was argued out.

The Reesorjeemento, the movement that unified the country, still means different things to different people across Italy, and this museum, founded in eighteen seventy eight, is one of the places where that story is laid out in its fullest form, for visitors to weigh up for themselves.

Round the side of the palace, on Piazza Kareenyahno, Gwareenee's rippling brick front is waiting, the older face of the same building, and the two make a fascinating comparison.
