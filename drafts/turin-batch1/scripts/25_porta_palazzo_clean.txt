Porta Palazzo

More than a thousand traders, around a hundred thousand shoppers a week, and over fifty thousand square metres of stalls make Porta Palazzo the biggest open-air market in Europe.

Across the whole of Piazza della Repubblica, inside a ring of curved, arcaded buildings, rows of stalls stretch away under their awnings. On one side stands a long iron pavilion with a clock on it, and on another, the covered halls of the fish and food markets.

The square itself is older than the market. In the eighteenth century the architect Filippo Juvarra laid it out for the House of Savoy as a grand military square, and you can still see his ambition in those curving buildings around the edge, even if the drilling soldiers have long since given way to boxes of peaches.

The clock pavilion is the Mercato dell'Orologio, a metal structure from 1916, while the market itself goes back to 1835, when the first covered stalls were put up here; the pavilions of the food and fish markets grew around it over the following decades.

Listen as you walk along the rows, because Porta Palazzo has always been a crossroads, and today you'll hear Italian, Arabic, Romanian, Chinese and plenty more, see Piedmontese cheeses next to North African spices, and smell fresh herbs beside the fishmongers' counters.

Its size is impressive, but the deeper point is the way it has absorbed every wave of newcomers to Turin, from the southern Italians who came north to work in the factories to more recent arrivals, and turned all of them into customers and traders on the same square.

Come in the morning for the best of the produce, and look for the farmers' section, where growers from the surrounding countryside sell their own vegetables. Then walk downhill along Via Borgo Dora into the Balôn, the old flea market district just behind the square.
