Tre Galline

The story goes that in 1592 three sisters ran an inn near here, and because most of their customers couldn't read, they painted three hens on the wall instead of a sign.

Painted in red above a doorway at number thirty-seven Via Bellezia, in the old Roman quarter, the name Tre Galline — the three hens — marks the restaurant you're looking for. Through the windows you'll see a warm, wood-panelled dining room, and perhaps a few hen figurines near the entrance.

According to the legend, the sisters' painted hens gave their name both to the inn and to the street it stood on, and the restaurant has kept the story — and a small flock of china and ceramic hens — ever since.

The room is almost entirely wood, with parquet floors that creak a little, panelled walls and exposed beams overhead, and red accents and furniture that give it a slightly theatrical air.

The food is why people come, because this is where you eat like the Turinese. The menu is Piedmontese: vitello tonnato, made here without mayonnaise, agnolotti in roast meat sauce, finanziera, the old Turin stew of offal and mushrooms, and bollito misto, a selection of boiled meats served at the table from a trolley, with a cheese trolley and a grappa cart to follow.

Whether or not the sisters existed exactly as the story says, the tale tells you what this place is for. Turin's food was never really court cooking; it was hearty, practical and shared, and that's what Tre Galline still serves.

Dinner fills up, so it's wise to reserve, and before or after your meal you could walk a few steps north to Porta Palazzo, the huge market where much of this cooking begins, or wander the Quadrilatero's lanes to find the restaurant's near-namesake, Tre Galli.
