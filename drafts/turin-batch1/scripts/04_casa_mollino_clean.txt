Casa Mollino

Carlo Mollino spent eight years designing an apartment in this building, filled it with his own furniture and symbols, and then, as far as anyone knows, never lived in it.

On Via Napione, close to where it meets the river, stands a late nineteenth-century villa built in 1888 in a French style, with tall upper windows looking straight out over the Po. Behind those windows, on one floor of the villa, is the apartment that became Museo Casa Mollino, and the street door here is the way in for visitors.

It's worth turning first to that view across the water. Mollino — architect, designer, photographer, racing driver and skier — reworked the flat between 1960 and 1968, and he chose rooms that face the river and the green hill opposite, which matters once you know what he had in mind.

The interior, which you can only see on a booked visit, is full of references to ancient Egypt and to the passage from this life to the next. There's a nineteenth-century bed shaped like a boat, the vessel that carries the soul onward; there's an oval table that recalls a funerary monument, and the walls carry painted butterflies, an old symbol of resurrection.

The surprising part is that this isn't really a home at all, but something closer to a private tomb in the Egyptian sense, a house prepared for an afterlife. The man who now runs it, Fulvio Ferrari, describes it as Mollino's final thesis on existence, and so the city with the great Egyptian Museum ended up with a quietly Egyptian apartment as well.

Visits are by appointment only, in small groups of about six, and they last around two hours, so it's worth booking ahead. Whether or not you go in, walk down to the embankment and look back up at those windows, because that was the view Mollino designed for.
