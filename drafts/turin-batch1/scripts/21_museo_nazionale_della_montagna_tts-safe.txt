Moozayo Natsyonahleh della Montahnya, Dooka delyee Abroottsee

On a clear day, from the terrace on this little hill, you can see something like four hundred kilometres of the Alps laid out in a single sweep.

Make your way to the open terrace in front of the old Capuchin church at the top of Monteh day Kappoocheenee, the wooded hill just across the Poh from the city centre. The Moozayo Natsyonahleh della Montahnya is in the buildings beside the church, the city is spread out below you with the river nearest, and the mountains stretch along the far horizon.

Pick out the Mohleh's spire among the rooftops first, then lift your eyes to the white wall of peaks beyond it, which runs most of the way round the western horizon when the air is clean.

The reason the museum is up here goes back to eighteen seventy four, when the Italian Alpine Club set up an observation post on this hill with a telescope, so that people in Turin could study the mountains they were planning to climb. It was inaugurated on the ninth of August that year, and the first museum rooms followed in eighteen seventy seven.

The museum takes its name from Prince Looeejee of Savoy, Duke of the Abroottsee, an explorer and mountaineer who gave it the equipment from his expeditions in nineteen oh one. Today it covers climbing, skiing, tourism and the lives of the people who live in the mountains, and it also holds the Alpine Club's national library.

This city has always had the Alps in its line of sight, and the museum turns that view into a subject, treating the mountains not as scenery but as neighbours that have shaped how Turin thinks about itself.

The museum's own viewing terrace, at the top of the building, is higher still and has the best of the panorama. Coming down, take the winding footpath back towards the river and the bridge to Piazza Veettoreeo, catching glimpses of the city through the trees.
