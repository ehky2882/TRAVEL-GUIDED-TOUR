Museo Nazionale del Risorgimento Italiano

Inside this building there's a parliament chamber where a clock and a calendar still record the moment of its last session: half past two in the afternoon, on the twenty-eighth of December 1860.

King Carlo Alberto sits on horseback in the middle of the square around you, and the vast building along one side is the back of Palazzo Carignano, its grand nineteenth-century facade lined with columns. The entrance to the Museo Nazionale del Risorgimento Italiano is here, at number eight Piazza Carlo Alberto.

This facade is the first thing to take in. The wing was built after 1861 to house the hall of the first Italian parliament, but the capital moved to Florence in 1865 and the hall was never used for its purpose, which makes this whole grand front a monument to a plan that changed.

On the far side is the older building. The front of Palazzo Carignano, facing Piazza Carignano, was designed by Guarino Guarini and begun in 1679, with a famous curving facade in brick, and in 1848 its central hall became the chamber of the Subalpine Parliament, the elected assembly of the Kingdom of Sardinia.

That chamber is Room 16 of the museum, and it's described as the only parliamentary chamber from 1848 still intact in Europe. The benches are ivory-coloured, the boxes are lined with red velvet, and figures like Cavour, Gioberti and D'Azeglio sat here while the case for a united Italy was argued out.

The Risorgimento, the movement that unified the country, still means different things to different people across Italy, and this museum, founded in 1878, is one of the places where that story is laid out in its fullest form, for visitors to weigh up for themselves.
