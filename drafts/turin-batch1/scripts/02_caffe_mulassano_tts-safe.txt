Kaffeh Moolahssahno

The trahmetseeno — that soft, crustless sandwich sold in practically every bar in Italy — was invented in a room smaller than a decent hotel suite, and you're about to look inside it.

Under the arcades on the south east side of Piazza Kastello, a few doors along from the entrance to the Gallereeah Soobalpeena, there's a narrow cafe front with Moolahssahno written above it. Palatso Madahma is across the square behind you, and through the glass you'll catch the glint of brass, polished wood and a great many mirrors.

Peer through the glass at the interior, which is about thirty one square metres in all. An engineer named Antonio Vandohneh fitted it out in marble, bronze and carved wood, then lined it with mirrors so that the tiny space seems to repeat itself in every direction; it's a trick, and a very elegant one.

The sandwich counter carries the real story, which belongs to Anjela Demmeekaylis Nebyoalo. She had emigrated to America as a teenager and run restaurants in Detroit with her husband, and after the couple came home and took over this cafe in nineteen twenty five, she began serving soft white bread, untoasted and with the crusts cut away, filled with things like butter and anchovies; a brass plaque on the display case records the year as nineteen twenty six.

The name came from Gahbreeelleh Dannoontseeo, the poet and a regular here, who wanted an Italian word for what was essentially an American idea, and he settled on trahmetseeno, roughly a little something in between.

What lingers, standing here, is how much of a nation's everyday habit can begin in one small room, since the sandwich went everywhere, the name went everywhere, and the room stayed exactly the size it was.

Moolahssahno makes more than thirty kinds now, including lobster, truffle and bahnya cowda, so go in and order at the counter like a regular. Afterwards, slip next door into the Gallereeah Soobalpeena, where the glass roof turns the light silvery even on a grey afternoon.
