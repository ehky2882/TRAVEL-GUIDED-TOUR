Put your back to the bridge.

Two towers are holding the gate of the street, one on each side. To your left, on the west, the pale one with the clock tower. To your right, on the east, the darker one with the crown of stone spines at the top.

Take the pale one first.

The Wrigley Building went up starting in nineteen twenty, and the thing to hold onto is what wasn't around it. No major office buildings stood north of the river. The bridge was unfinished when the work began. A chewing gum company bought land on a street that had been renamed but not yet built, and put a tower on it.

The architects were Graham, Anderson, Prohbst and White, the firm that inherited Daniel Burnum's practice after he died. So the office that produced the plan for this street also produced the first building on it. And they gave it white terra cotta, graded in shades that get palest toward the top so the tower seems to lift, and floodlights that have washed the front of it since the day it opened. Nobody was lighting office buildings all night in nineteen twenty. It was an advertisement, and it worked.

The Tribune Tower followed in nineteen twenty five, across the road, from an international design competition the paper ran for its own headquarters.

Neither of these became a landmark. Both were built as one, on purpose, by two companies whose actual business was getting your attention, a newspaper and a gum manufacturer, for a street that as yet had nothing on it worth stopping for. The avenue's opening statement is two very large pieces of advertising, and the earlier one is named after gum.

Keep walking north. Stop when you reach Ontario Street.
