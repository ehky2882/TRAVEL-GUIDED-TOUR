The street got named a second time in nineteen forty seven, and this time by a man who was selling it.

Arthur Rub loff was a real estate developer who ran the business association for this stretch of the avenue, and after the war he launched a campaign to rebuild it. He is widely credited with supplying the phrase that came with the campaign: the Magnificent Mile. It was a marketing line for roughly thirteen blocks that were, at the time, a mixed and somewhat tired commercial district.

Sight up the avenue.

What you're looking at is mostly the result of that campaign and the twenty years after it, the postwar boom that filled in the gaps between the twenties buildings, moved the city's shopping weight north off State Street, and finished with the black tower and its crossed steel braces you can see far up the street.

One career runs the whole length of it. Alfred Shaw came to Chicago in the twenties to work at the firm you just looked at, Graham, Anderson, Prohbst and White, where he had a hand in the Civic Opera House and the Merchandise Mart. From nineteen thirty seven he ran a series of his own partnerships, which turned out to be among the busiest practices in the city, and they built up here too. One man, one avenue, from the boh zar version to the plate glass one.

The pattern holds. The name arrives, and then the street is rebuilt until the name is defensible. It happened in nineteen seventeen with an ordinance, and it happened in nineteen forty seven with a slogan, and both times the buildings came afterward to justify the label.
