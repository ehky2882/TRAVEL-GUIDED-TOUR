El Oombrakleh

Fifty five fixed arches and fifty four floating ones stand over this walkway, each of them about eighteen metres tall.

Stand on the raised promenade inside the Oombrakleh and look along its length, and the white ribs repeat into the distance like the skeleton of something enormous, with palm trees growing up between them, and through the gaps on either side you can see the rest of Santiago Kalatrava's City of Arts and Sciences: the long science museum, the eye shaped Emeesfairik and, beyond them, the opera house.

The Oombrakleh was finished in two thousand and one, and it runs for some three hundred and twenty metres. Its name is an old Valencian word for a shaded garden structure, the kind of slatted pergola gardeners used to protect delicate plants from the Mediterranean sun, and Kalatrava simply scaled the idea up.

Look down at the planting, too, which includes ninety nine full sized palms, more than sixty bitter orange trees, hundreds of climbing plants and beds of rosemary and lavender, so on a warm afternoon the whole walkway smells faintly of a herb garden.

Along the promenade runs a walk of contemporary sculptures by artists including the Valencian sculptor Meekel Navarro and Yoko Ono, which turns a stroll into a small outdoor exhibition.

The neatest detail lies underneath, since the entire structure is a roof over a car park, and the most poetic piece of the whole complex is, at heart, a very elegant answer to a practical question about where to put the cars. It's also the one building here that you don't need a ticket for, which goes some way to explaining why it's the place people actually linger.

Walk to the far end, where the arches open out towards the lake and the opera house, and come back around sunset if you can, when the low light catches the ribs and the whole structure glows a soft gold against the pools.
