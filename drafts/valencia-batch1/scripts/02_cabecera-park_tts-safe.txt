Kabethaira Park

Almost seventeen hectares of lake, pine wood and meadow mark the spot where Valencia's old river enters the city.

Head down to the edge of the lake and face the water, and you'll see small wooded islands scattered across it, swan shaped pedal boats drifting between them, and, rising above the trees on one side, a rounded grassy hill with a path winding up to its top, the viewpoint.

Kabethaira means headwaters, and that word explains the design, because while most of the long Tooria garden is formal, with sports pitches, orange trees and tidy paths laid out in the dry bed of the diverted river, this western end was designed in two thousand and three and two thousand and four by Edwardo deh Meegel, Arancha Moonyoth and Veethenteh Korel to do something different: to look like a river again.

So the islands out there are real islands, the planting is the riverside woodland you'd find along a wild Mediterranean stream, and the water itself arrives through a canal fed from the Molee del Sol, an old mill upstream, tumbling over little waterfalls on its way in. The designers wanted a soft handover between the stiff eighteenth century flood embankments and the natural riverbed upstream.

The hill earns its climb, since from the top you can see across the whole park and, on a clear day, all the way to the Seeyerra Kalderona mountains on the northern horizon, and next door sits the Bio park zoo, which shares the same idea of letting landscape do the work instead of fences.

What makes this end of the garden worth the trip is that it remembers. Everywhere else the Tooria has been tidied into a park, but here it has been given a chance to pretend, quite convincingly, that the water never left.

If you've got half an hour, follow the signed Molee del Sol walk out towards the old mill, and you'll trace the canal back to where the water begins its short, gentle journey into the city.
