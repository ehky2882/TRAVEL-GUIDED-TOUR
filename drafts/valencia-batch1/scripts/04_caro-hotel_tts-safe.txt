Caro Hotel

Very few hotels can say that a Roman mosaic, a twelfth century Arab wall and an aristocrat's palace all share a single address.

The discreet frontage in front of you, at number fourteen on the quiet Kahyeh del Almeeranteh a few minutes east of the cathedral, belongs to an old town house that gives very little away from the pavement about the layers stacked up inside.

This was the palace of the Markess deh Karo, and when it was turned into a hotel of twenty six rooms, the interior designer Franthesk Reefay was given a puzzle: how to make a modern place to sleep without hiding a single century. His answer was to treat the building like a gallery, with clean, pale, minimal furnishings that step back and let the history take the light.

Downstairs, in the bar, remains of the Roman city are on open display, including original mosaic from Valentia, the settlement founded here in the second century before Christ. The hotel's restaurant goes further still, set directly against a stretch of the twelfth century Arab wall that once ringed the Islamic city, so dinner comes with medieval fortifications.

Upstairs, a few of the rooms carry on the theme, with medieval coffered ceilings and Moorish arches built into the bedrooms, and the guide inspectors at Michelin have described it as the only hotel in the city that is itself a listed historic monument.

The interesting bit is how ordinary the street outside looks. Valencia is a city built in layers, Roman under Islamic under Christian, and usually you need a museum to see them, but here the whole cross section has been sliced open and turned into somewhere you can order a coffee.

Round the corner, on the street named after them, the Banyos del Almeeranteh are well worth ten minutes: a medieval bathhouse built in the Islamic tradition after the Christian conquest, with star shaped skylights cut into the vaults, and a rare survivor of the medieval city's daily rituals.
