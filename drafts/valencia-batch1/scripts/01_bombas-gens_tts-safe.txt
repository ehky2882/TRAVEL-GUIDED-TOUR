The brick factory in front of you spent sixty years making water pumps, and these days it pumps out light instead.

Aveengooda deh Boorjasot runs along the front of the complex here in Marshalaynes, and from the pavement opposite you can take in the frontage: walls of warm clay brick, terracotta roof tiles, and the clean geometric styling of an Art Deco factory built to look modern in nineteen thirty.

Kah yeh tahno Borso dee Karmeenahti designed it for two businessmen, Karlos Jens and Rafa el Dalyi, who turned out hydraulic pumps, valves and small farm machinery for the irrigated fields that once came right up to this edge of the city. During the Civil War the factory was requisitioned for munitions, and around nineteen thirty eight an air raid shelter was dug beneath it to protect the workforce, a space that has been preserved.

The works closed in nineteen ninety one and stood empty until a private foundation bought it, spending around ten million euros on a restoration led partly by the New York architect Annabelle Seldorf. Digging beneath it turned up a fifteenth century wine cellar, centuries older than the factory, which was kept as part of the new design.

Since early twenty twenty four the old production halls have been run by a studio called Layers of Reality, and the programme has moved to digital and immersive work, with giant projections and virtual reality filling rooms that once held lathes.

There's something rather neat in the way a building made to move water around the fields now moves images around its walls, while the steel roof trusses that watched over pump casings now watch over projectors, a neat summary of how the city itself has changed.

On your way out, walk the length of the frontage and look for where restored brick meets new, since the architects deliberately let the two sit side by side, and if a shelter visit is running on the day, it's well worth booking a place inside that quiet piece of Civil War history.
