Velez eh Vents

This building went up in just eleven months for a sailing race, and it was designed as a place to look out from rather than a place to look at.

The white building in front of you rises in layers on the edge of the marina, four storeys of horizontal decks stacked on top of each other, each one sliding out beyond the one below, so that the upper floors cantilever out as much as fifteen metres over the terraces.

It was designed by the British architect David Chipperfield, later a winner of the Pritsker Prize, together with the Spanish firm of Fairmeen Vasketh, and it opened in two thousand and six as the base for the America's Cup, the sailing competition that came to Valencia in two thousand and seven. The brief was simple: a building where people could watch the racing, standing outside as much as in.

The name comes from a famous poem by the medieval Valencian poet Owzeeas March, and it means Sails and Winds. It became widely known when the singer Lyoo eess Lyak set the poem to music, so the building carries a little of the Valencian language and its culture with it.

The decks are laid with timber, with crisp white edges, and the deep overhangs throw sharp shadows that keep the terraces cool, a useful trick in a city with this much sun. The building won a Reeba award and was shortlisted for the Stirling Prize.

The building's real achievement is that it outlasted its purpose, because plenty of venues made for one off sporting events end up empty, while Velez eh Vents now houses restaurants and bars, proof that a good building can outlive the job it was made for.

Climb up to the higher terraces if they're open, for views over the marina and the sea, and then walk along the waterfront to the beaches of Las Areynas and the Malvarrosa, where the city meets the Mediterranean.
