Palace of the Marqués de Dos Aguas

The doorway in front of you is carved from alabaster, and the whole thing is essentially a pun.

Lift your eyes to the palace's extraordinary entrance on Calle del Poeta Querol, a great cascade of pale carved stone, with two powerful male figures sprawled on either side of the door, each pouring water from a jar, and above them, in a niche, a figure of the Virgin Mary looking down.

The family who lived here held the title of Marqués de Dos Aguas, which means Marquis of the Two Waters, and the two figures are pouring those waters, usually identified as the region's two great rivers, the Turia and the Júcar, which is heraldry turned into theatre.

The portal dates from the 1740s, when the family transformed an older mansion into this rococo showpiece. It was designed by the painter Hipólito Rovira and carved by the sculptor Ignacio Vergara, and it remains one of the most exuberant pieces of rococo carving in Spain.

Originally the rest of the facade was every bit as lavish, painted all over with frescoes by Rovira himself, but by the nineteenth century they had deteriorated so badly that in 1867 they were removed and replaced with the grey and pink stucco, imitating marble, that you can see today, along with the curving French-style balconies.

Since the middle of the twentieth century the palace has housed the National Museum of Ceramics, named after the collector Manuel González Martí.

What this building does so well is advertise. Before newspapers or logos, a doorway like this was how a family told the whole city exactly who they were, and nearly three hundred years later, people still stop in the street to stare at it, which suggests it worked rather well.

The ceramic collection inside runs from ancient pottery to modern design, and on the ground floor you'll find the family's gilded eighteenth-century carriages, every bit as outrageous as the door.
