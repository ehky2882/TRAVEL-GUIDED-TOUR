Lavoh eh Arroth ee Mar

Fifty litres of fresh stock are made here every day, and the owner has said there's no cutting corners with any of it.

Through the window here on Karreh deh la Krayoo Nova, a narrow old town street a few minutes from the Plaza deh la Rayna, you can see a bright dining room and a bar facing an open kitchen, with modern artworks hanging on the walls.

Tony Boysh opened Lavoh eh in twenty seventeen, after three years spent cooking paellas for well heeled clients in Ibeetha, and he has described it less as a restaurant and more as a kind of gastronomic society, a club for people who take rice seriously. The interior is by the designer Ramon Bandress, with the kitchen in full view of the tables.

The menu focuses on rice and the sea, with around twenty different rice dishes alongside feedeh wah, made with short noodles in place of grains. Look out for rice with longaneetha sausage and green garlic shoots, a rich rice made with the meats of a traditional stew, and a feedeh wah with sea bass and prawns, all of them built on that daily stock.

It helps to know how Valencians eat this kind of food. Paella is a lunchtime dish, cooked to order in a wide, shallow pan, and the prize is the sokarrat, the thin layer of rice that toasts and crackles against the bottom of the pan, which locals will scrape out with real determination.

The appeal of Lavoh eh is in that seriousness about a single thing. In a city where paella is sold on every tourist street, a small kitchen that makes fifty litres of stock every morning is quietly making the case for doing the most famous dish in Valencia properly.

Come at lunchtime, book ahead, and when the pan arrives, ask for some sokarrat; afterwards, walk round to the Plaza deh la Rayna for a look at the cathedral's ornate Baroque front.
