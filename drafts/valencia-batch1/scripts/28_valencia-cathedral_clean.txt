Valencia Cathedral

Every Thursday at noon, a group of farmers in black smocks sits down outside one of these doors to settle disputes over water.

Plaza de la Virgen opens out around you, and facing you across it is the Gothic Door of the Apostles, its tall pointed arch lined with stone figures of saints and apostles, while the cathedral's octagonal bell tower, the Miguelete, rises over the rooftops nearby.

The Water Tribunal that meets at this door is one of the oldest legal institutions in Europe, and it still deals with disputes between farmers who share the ancient irrigation channels of the huerta. It meets in public, judges in Valencian, and keeps no written records, and UNESCO has listed it as part of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity.

The cathedral itself was begun in 1262, on the site of the city's main mosque. It grew in every style going, so the Romanesque Palau door, this Gothic one and the Baroque Iron Door at the front span three eras around one building.

Inside, in a side chapel that was once the chapter house, is the Holy Chalice, an agate cup which many believe to be the vessel used at the Last Supper. Historians debate how far back its story can be traced, though its place in the city's faith is secure.

Then there's the Miguelete, the bell tower that has become a symbol of the city, with two hundred and seven steps to the top.

What this square really shows is how long things last here. A building that has been growing for more than seven centuries stands beside a court said to be older still, and every Thursday the old rules about sharing water are still being applied, just as they were before the cathedral's first stone was laid.

If your legs are up to it, climb the Miguelete for the view, or walk round the side to the Palau door to see the oldest entrance of all.
