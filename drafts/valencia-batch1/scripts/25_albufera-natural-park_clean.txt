Parque Natural de la Albufera

The name Albufera comes from the Arabic for the little sea, and when the water is flat and the light is low, it's easy to see why.

At the water's edge, where the reeds part, look out across the lagoon and you'll see a wide, shallow sheet of water fringed with reed beds, low wooden boats with flat bottoms, perhaps a heron or an egret standing very still in the shallows, and, on the far side, the green or golden line of the rice fields.

The lagoon lies about eleven kilometres south of the city, inside a natural park of more than twenty thousand hectares protected since 1986, and it was once a salty inlet of the sea until irrigation channels and growing sandbars turned it fresh.

People have fished here for centuries, with rights recognised as early as 1250, and from the eighteenth century onwards rice took over the land around the water. The rice fields still set the rhythm of the year, planted in spring, harvested in early autumn, and flooded in winter for the birds.

Not far away lies El Palmar, a village of fishermen and cooks, where you'll see barracas, the traditional thatched cottages with whitewashed walls and steep roofs, and where the local speciality is all i pebre, a stew of eels in garlic and paprika. This landscape also inspired the writer Vicente Blasco Ibáñez, whose novel Cañas y Barro, or Reeds and Mud, is set here.

What's worth knowing, out here, is that this is where paella comes from. The rice, the eels, the ducks and the snails that went into the original dish all came from this lagoon, so the city's most famous meal is really a portrait of this landscape.

If you can stay until evening, take one of the flat-bottomed boats out from El Palmar at sunset, and wait, since the richest colour usually arrives ten or fifteen minutes after the sun has actually gone down.
