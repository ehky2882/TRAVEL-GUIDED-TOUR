Jardín del Turia

On the fourteenth of October 1957, the river that once flowed under this bridge burst its banks and flooded much of Valencia.

From the middle of the old stone bridge here, the Pont de la Trinitat, look down over its parapet: below you, where water used to run, are paths, palm trees, lawns and joggers, part of a green ribbon that curves right through the city, and two stone statues of saints keep watch over the crossing.

The flood of 1957 killed at least eighty-one people, and afterwards the authorities decided the river could never be allowed back. Under what became known as the Plan Sur, the Turia was diverted into a new channel well to the south, leaving its old bed through the centre empty.

The first plan for that empty riverbed was a motorway. Valencians pushed back, with a campaign whose slogan translates roughly as the Turia's bed is ours and we want it green, and they won, so from 1986 the riverbed was turned, section by section, into a park almost nine kilometres long. Ricardo Bofill designed one stretch, and other teams added sports fields, fountains and an urban forest of pines.

The bridge under your feet is a survivor in its own right. It was built between 1401 and 1407, with ten Gothic arches, and it's the oldest bridge in the city, having outlasted centuries of floods, partial rebuilding and the river itself.

That origin story is what sets the Turia garden apart from most city parks. It wasn't handed down by a planner or a monarch; it came out of a disaster and then out of an argument, won by people who wanted somewhere to walk rather than somewhere to drive.

Cross to the far end of the bridge, where the Museo de Bellas Artes holds a superb collection of Spanish old masters, or take the steps down into the riverbed and follow the paths east towards the City of Arts and Sciences.
