La Lonha deh la Sayda deh Valencia

Inside this building, a Latin inscription in gold reminds merchants that trade is an honourable thing, provided nobody tells lies.

Across the square, with the Central Market at your back, stretches the long Gothic front of the Silk Exchange, its roofline lined with crenellations like a castle, its walls carved with gargoyles, and in the middle a square tower that rises above everything else.

It was built between fourteen eighty two and fifteen thirty three under the master builder Pereh Kompteh, at the height of Valencia's wealth as a Mediterranean trading city, and the main trading hall went up in just fifteen years, a speed the building rather proudly mentions in that same inscription.

When you go in, the hall is the thing to see. Twisted stone columns spiral up from the floor and open out at the top into ribbed vaults, so the space feels less like an office and more like a petrified palm grove, which is very much the effect Kompteh was after. It was here that silk merchants and traders shook hands on contracts.

The tower had a sterner job, since its rooms were used to lock up merchants who couldn't pay their debts, while another wing housed the Konsolat del Mar, a court that judged disputes over maritime trade. Between them sits a quiet courtyard of orange trees.

Along the outside walls, you'll notice that the gargoyles aren't all saints and beasts; some are distinctly earthy, which suggests the stonemasons had a sense of humour about the business going on inside.

What's striking is how modern the message is. In nineteen ninety six Yoonesko listed this as a World Heritage Site for its architecture, but its real legacy might be that inscription: a fifteenth century stock exchange that put business ethics on the wall in letters of gold.

Once inside, find the orange tree courtyard, then come back out and walk slowly round the outside, picking out your favourite gargoyle, since there's an odd one for everyone.
