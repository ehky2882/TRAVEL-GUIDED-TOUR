Espai Alfaro

On the edge of Godella, in the middle of an industrial estate, a sculptor quietly built himself an oasis.

The street sign gives the game away: this is Avinguda Andreu Alfaro, renamed for the man whose studio stands before you, and the complex is two plain modern buildings arranged around a central square, where large sculptures of steel stand in the open air.

Andreu Alfaro was born in Valencia in 1929, the son of a butcher, and taught himself to sculpt, working mostly in steel and aluminium, building forms out of repeated lines and modules that seem to shift as you walk around them, and he went on to show at the Venice Biennale and win Spain's National Prize for Fine Arts in 1981.

The first building went up in 1985, designed by the architect Emilio Giménez as a workshop for metal and stone, and a second, square volume was added beside it and turned forty-five degrees, which is where Alfaro kept his own studio. Since 2014, two years after his death, that studio has housed a permanent display of his work, roughly fifty smaller pieces plus a handful of monumental ones.

The rest of the site has been handed on to other makers. There's the architecture practice of Fran Silvestre, a master's school in design, a library, and the Alfaro Hofmann collection of more than five thousand household objects, from radios to toasters, about four hundred of them on show.

What lifts this place out of the ordinary is that it's still working. Plenty of artists' studios become shrines when the artist dies, but this one has become a school and a workshop, so the steel in the square is surrounded by students arguing about the next thing.

Visits are usually arranged in advance, so check before you come, and when you're inside, ask to see the appliance collection, where a century of kitchen gadgets turns out to be a surprisingly moving history of everyday life.
