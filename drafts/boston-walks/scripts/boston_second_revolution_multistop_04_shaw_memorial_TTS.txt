The man who raised this regiment watched it go by from the building behind you.

You're at the Beacon Street edge of the Common, near Park Street, facing a bronze relief in a stone frame: a young officer on horseback, rows of soldiers on foot beside him, a figure floating above. Across Beacon Street, behind you, is the State House.

The soldiers are the Fifty fourth Massachusetts, among the earliest Black regiments of the Civil War, and the officer is their colonel, Robert Gould Shaw. On the twenty eighth of May, eighteen sixty three, the regiment marched through the city on its way to the ships, and it came along Beacon Street, past this corner. Governor John Andrew had spent the winter persuading Washington that Massachusetts should raise a Black regiment at all, and he reviewed the men from the State House as they passed.

Look at where the memorial was put. When Augustus saint gaw denz's bronze was unveiled in eighteen ninety seven, the city set it here, on the Common's edge, facing the State House across the street, so that the regiment is forever passing the building whose governor sent it. The relief shows the men mid stride, still going by.

This is what the second revolution looked like once it had the government on its side. Twelve years after the courtroom it's a governor on the steps and a column of men in uniform under the state's flag. The revolution had gotten inside the seat.

One more climb, and it's the real one. Walk west along Beacon Street, past the State House steps, and turn up Joy Street. It climbs steeply to the crest at Mount Vernon Street and drops down the north slope. At Myrtle Street turn left, take the first right onto Irving Street, and the first left onto Phillips Street. Number sixty six is a brick row house a short way along, with a bronze plaque on the front.
