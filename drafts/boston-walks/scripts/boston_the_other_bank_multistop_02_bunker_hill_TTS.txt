The picture you have of this battle was painted eleven years after it, by a man who watched the smoke from across the water.

You're in Monument Square, on the downhill side, the granite shaft across the street and Charlestown's rooftops dropping away behind you toward the city. This is Breed's Hill. The monument marks the earthwork the militia dug on the night of June sixteenth, seventeen seventy five, and lost the next afternoon.

Most Americans carry one image of that afternoon, and it is a painting. John trum bul, a young officer in the American army, watched the battle from the Roxbury lines beyond Boston, and in seventeen eighty six he painted it in London: a fallen officer in white at the center, a comrade shielding him from a bayonet, a British officer calling for mercy, smoke and flags around them. The officer in white is wor en.

The record runs differently. Joseph wor en was a doctor from Roxbury and, that June, the presiding officer of the Provincial Congress, the closest thing the province had to a head of government. Named a general three days earlier, he had not taken the commission; he came to the hill as a volunteer and asked to serve in the ranks. He was shot in the final assault, and the British buried him where he fell, with another man. The next spring, after the British left Boston, his brothers found the grave, and a friend who had once made him false teeth knew him by the wire in his jaw.

None of that is in the painting. In it, trum bul gave the war a gentleman's ending, because that was what a painting of a battle was for, and the country has looked at his version since. The record has a doctor in the ranks, an unmarked grave, and a dentist. Boston's memory of seventeen seventy five keeps arriving as a picture. The record is usually a receipt.

Leave the square on the downhill side and follow Winthrop Street down to City Square, the park at the bottom of the hill where the roads meet.
