# BOSTON · W5 — The Name Above the Door
Smith Court and the African Meeting House · walk · segment 2 of 6 · clean · 29 September 2026
---
Over the door of the building in front of you there is a man's name and a year, cut into the stone.

You're in Smith Court, facing the African Meeting House. The name is Cato Gardner's, and the year is 1806. He had been born in Africa, and when Boston's Black Baptists set out to build a church of their own, he raised a large share of the money. Black craftsmen did most of the work, and when it was done the congregation put his name where everyone who came in would pass under it. Nobody gave him the building. They gave him the door.

*[beat]*

Keep the congregation in mind, because they had come from somewhere. In 1805 a preacher named Thomas Paul and a small group of Black Bostonians met to form their own church, and the building they met in was Faneuil Hall. Their early services were held there too, in a hall named for a merchant who had made part of his fortune in the slave trade. They wanted a room that was theirs, so they climbed the hill and built one. This walk ends at the building they left, seen from the other end of the story.

Once it was built, the room was used for far more than church. Boston's Black community met here to organize, to argue and to hear speakers, and for the crowds and the weight of what was decided here, people around the city took to calling it the Black Faneuil Hall. The name was a compliment and a comparison at once, and the comparison ran uphill. One night in December of 1860 a meeting about ending slavery was broken up by a mob in a hall downtown, and Frederick Douglass and the others who had been driven out finished the evening up here.

The brick building on the corner is the Abiel Smith School, and the two are a museum now. Turn left out of the court onto Joy Street and stop at the top before you start down.
