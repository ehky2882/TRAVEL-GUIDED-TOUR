# BOSTON · W2 — The Second Revolution
Phillips Street — the Hayden House · walk · segment 6 of 6 · clean · 29 Sept 2026
---
For twelve years the man who lived here made sure he didn't own it.

You're on Phillips Street, on the north slope of Beacon Hill, across from number sixty-six: a plain brick row house of the 1830s with a bronze plaque on its front. It's a private home, like every house here; this stop stays on the sidewalk.

Lewis and Harriet Hayden escaped slavery in Kentucky in 1844 and were in Boston by 1846, he with a clothing shop on Cambridge Street at the bottom of this slope, and by 1850 they were living here. The same year Congress passed the Fugitive Slave Act, under which a person could be seized in a free state and sent back. The Haydens' answer was to take people in. The records of Boston's Vigilance Committee show freedom seekers sheltered here again and again through the 1850s; the Park Service calls it the most active safe house in the city. William and Ellen Craft stayed here; Harriet Beecher Stowe visited while writing Uncle Tom's Cabin.

Sheltering them was a federal crime, and a house is easy to take. So in 1853 a white abolitionist named Francis Jackson bought it, and the Haydens lived on as his tenants, behind a deed in somebody else's name. It came back to Harriet in 1865, when there was no one left to hide.

*[beat]*

Then the seat. In 1873 Lewis Hayden was elected to the Massachusetts House and took his seat in the State House you just walked around, on the far side of this hill. Harriet outlived him, and in 1893 her will left their whole estate, about five thousand dollars, to Harvard for a scholarship for Black medical students, still awarded.

This walk began at a building the government left and ends behind the one it moved into. The first revolution came down from a balcony. The second went up this hill, house by house, until one of the people who fought it had a seat inside.
