About every forty five minutes, day and night, for years on end, a train pulled in here and tipped out its load.

You're on Dartmouth Street at the corner of the Public Library, with kop lee Square open in front of you, boil stun Street along its left edge, and Trinity Church, brown and rough cut, on the far side. This is the last stop.

The ground you're on came from nee dum, a town about nine miles west. From eighteen fifty eight the contractors ran a rail line out to the gravel pits there, loaded the cars with steam shovels, and sent them back to the bay. The figure Boston repeats is a train about every forty five minutes, around the clock, each one dumping its gravel at the edge of the water and going back for more. It went on for decades. The square in front of you was under the tide until around eighteen seventy, when the trains reached it.

That's the third measure, and the last. The hill's top went a few hundred yards. Charles Street went a few hundred feet. This came nine miles, on a timetable, and it's the ground under everything you can see from here.

There is one more layer, farther down. When workers dug the subway under boil stun Street in nineteen thirteen, they found wooden stakes about thirty feet down in the clay, driven in rows. They were fish weirs, fences of stakes and brush built in the tidal flats to hold fish as the water went out, and they are thousands of years old, the work of the ancestors of the mass uh choo set people, who fished this bay long before anyone decided it was in the way.

Feel the ground now. You walked downhill for a third of a mile at the start, and you haven't gone down or up since. This is about the flattest place in the city, and it's flat because it was delivered, load after load, to one level, by people working to a schedule. Nothing here rose to meet you. It was brought.

This is kop lee Square, and the walk ends here. The library beside you is open to anyone, and Trinity is a short cross of the square away. But the route stops on this spot, on the ground that came the farthest.
