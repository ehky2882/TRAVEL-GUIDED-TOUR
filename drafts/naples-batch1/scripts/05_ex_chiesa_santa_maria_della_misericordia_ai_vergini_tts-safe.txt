For years after the Second World War this church was a dumping ground, and the art that rescued it is still made inside.

At the corner of Veeah Meezereekordyella, close to the old Portah San Jennahroh, the church in front of you shows a tall, flat facade in two storeys, with pilasters below, a large window above and a triangular pediment on top that holds a round window at its centre. The doorway is the giveaway, framed in dark grey stone carved into curling eighteenth century shapes.

That stone is peepairnoh, a volcanic rock quarried around Naples, and it's carved here in a playful rococo style that seems almost too light for such a heavy material.

The church itself is far older than its portal, and a tombstone dated thirteen fifty eight turned up during sixteenth century renovations. In fifteen thirty three the building passed to the Theeateen fathers, whose founder, Saint Guyetahnoh dah Tyehneh, stayed next door. By the early seventeen hundreds it had been filled with paintings by leading Neapolitan artists, among them a work by Johvahnee Baldoochee on the corporal works of mercy, which means feeding the hungry, sheltering strangers and caring for the sick. Beneath the nave lies a burial chamber of the same size, where the confraternity laid its dead.

War damage closed it for decades, and that's when the rubbish arrived. Since twenty fifteen it has been looked after by Smahveh, a centre for contemporary art founded by the sculptor Christian Lepereenoh, whose volunteers cleared and restored the building and now run exhibitions, concerts, workshops and studio places for young sculptors.

What I find rather lovely is that the old dedication still fits, since a church built around works of mercy, rescued by volunteers, now spends its days teaching.


Walk on into Veeah day Verjeenee afterwards, with its morning street market, and look for the Pahlatsoh dello Spanyohloh, whose open double staircase by Ferdeenandoh Sanfeleecheh is one of the great sights of this quarter.
