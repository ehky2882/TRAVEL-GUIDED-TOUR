Reekardoh Dahleezee, Rooah Kahtahlahnah

For something like seven hundred years, this street has made its living out of metal.

Narrow and slightly crooked, Rooah Kahtahlahnah threads between the port and the old city, a couple of minutes' walk from the Maskyoh Anjoeenoh, and if you look up at the walls of the pahlatsee around you, you'll find figures in iron and tin fixed here and there to the stone.

In the fourteenth century the Anjevin queen Joanna the First gave this stretch to Catalan merchants, who traded in metal goods such as pots, pans and tools, and the word rooah is usually explained as a Neapolitan bending of the French roo, from the days when the city's rulers were French. Metalworkers have kept shops here ever since, though fewer than there once were.

The figures on the walls are the work of Reekardoh Dahleezee, the architect, designer and artist who was born in Potentsah in nineteen thirty one and died in Naples in twenty twenty two. In nineteen seventy nine the design firm Alessee asked him to rethink the traditional Neapolitan coffee pot, and he worked with the tinsmiths of streets like this one, making some two hundred tin models before the design was finished. That research won him the Kompassoh Doroh, Italy's leading design prize, in nineteen eighty one, and in nineteen ninety seven he gave something back, fixing his sculptures to these walls in a project that brought architecture students to work alongside the craftsmen.

Most designers take a craft out of the workshop and into a factory, and Dahleezee did very nearly the opposite, taking his ideas back to the workshop and leaving them on the street where they'd been made. Years later he even founded a prize of his own, the Kompassoh dee Lattah, or tin compass, to encourage young designers to work the same way.


From the end of the street, the Maskyoh Anjoeenoh is just ahead, and its white marble triumphal arch, squeezed between two dark stone towers, is well worth a closer look.
