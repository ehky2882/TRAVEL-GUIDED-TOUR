Castel Sant'Elmo

From up here the whole of Naples is laid out like a map, and that was rather the point of putting a castle on this hilltop.

The ramp in front of you crosses the old moat to the castle's main gate, cut into walls of pale volcanic stone that rise sheer out of the ditch, with the imperial coat of arms carved above the entrance. Behind you lies the Vomero, the neighbourhood that grew up around the fortress much later.

The shape is best appreciated from the air, because Castel Sant'Elmo is a six pointed star, drawn up between 1537 and 1547 by the military engineer Pedro Luis Escrivá for the Spanish viceroy, Pedro de Toledo. It replaced a smaller Angevin fortress called Belforte, and its name comes, by way of a few slurred centuries, from a tenth century church of Sant'Erasmo that once stood on the hill.

Inside, the open parade ground known as the Piazza d'Armi now houses the Museo del Novecento, opened in 2010, with more than a hundred and seventy works by Neapolitan artists of the twentieth century. It's a peaceful use for a fortress with a violent past, since in 1587 lightning struck the gunpowder store and blew apart the church and the officers' quarters.

The interesting bit is who the castle was watching. For long stretches of its history its most important job was as a prison, holding the philosopher Tommaso Campanella in 1604 and patriots of the Neapolitan Republic of 1799, and it stayed a military prison until 1952. A star fort above a crowded city is a defence, certainly, but it's also a very good place from which to keep an eye on that city.

Climb up to the ramparts and walk the full circuit if you can, since the views run from the islands round to Vesuvius and out to the Phlegraean Fields. The Certosa di San Martino next door offers another view, with a monastery attached.
