San Carlo Theatre

This theatre had already been staging opera for more than forty years when La Scala opened in Milan.

Stand on the pavement of Via San Carlo, with the arcaded front of the Teatro di San Carlo on one side and the glass roofed Galleria Umberto the First behind you; to the left of the theatre as you face it, the Royal Palace carries on the same long frontage.

That connection is the key to the whole building. The San Carlo opened on the fourth of November 1737, commissioned by King Charles of Bourbon and designed by the royal architect Giovanni Antonio Medrano, and it was built against the palace itself, so the opera was quite literally an extension of the court. It's generally described as the oldest opera house in the world still in continuous use, and its horseshoe shaped auditorium became a model for theatres across Europe.

The fire came next, when on the thirteenth of February 1816 flames gutted the theatre, and the architect Antonio Niccolini rebuilt it in about ten months, reopening it in January 1817, which is the kind of speed that tells you how much an opera house mattered to a capital city. The neoclassical front you're looking at is Niccolini's work.

As for the music, Gioachino Rossini ran the house from 1815 to 1822, Gaetano Donizetti followed him, and in 1835 Donizetti's Lucia di Lammermoor had its first performance here.

Standing outside, it's striking how ordinary the theatre's position looks today, wedged between a palace and a shopping arcade. That's precisely the point, though, because Naples built its opera house in the middle of everything, and the city has never quite let it become a museum.
