Eepohjayoh day Kreestahleenee

In eighteen eighty nine a baron digging beneath his pahlatsoh, looking for water and building stone, broke into a Greek cemetery instead.

The doorway of number one hundred and thirty three Veeah day Kreestahleenee, right in front of you, belongs to that same family pahlatsoh, and the tombs lie underneath it, reached by a small staircase inside. Visits are guided and limited to groups of no more than eight, so the pavement out here is the right place to wait for yours.

Below ground there are four tombs side by side, cut into the soft yellow tuff that Naples is built on, and each has two levels: an upper room where the family carried out funeral rites, and a burial chamber beneath it. They date back about two thousand three hundred years, to a time when this hillside lay outside the walls of the Greek city of Neeapolis and served as its necropolis.

The detail people remember is the furniture, because the dead were laid on beds carved out of the rock itself, and those beds were painted to look upholstered, with mattresses and double pillows in yellow, purple, blue and red. Elsewhere the walls carry painted garlands, vases and altars, and in one tomb a head of Medyoosa keeps watch, with figures of Dyeonyesus and Arry adnee close by.

A restoration begun in twenty twenty with Italy's Central Institute for Restoration steadied the colours, and the site reopened in July twenty twenty two, with small groups to protect the paintings.

The people who made these rooms weren't picturing their dead as gone; they were picturing them at rest, in good company, on a well made bed. All that painted cushioning and false architecture is a family insisting on comfort, long before anyone could have guessed we'd come down the stairs to look.


Afterwards, walk down to the Basilica of Santa Mahreeah della Sahneetah, a few minutes away, where the catacombs of San Gowdyohzoh carry this hillside's long story of burial on into early Christian times.
