Skottoh Yonnoh

In eighteen eighty three, under the new glass roof of this arcade, a caffay opened where Neapolitans could hear the latest songs while they drank.

The Gallereeah Preencheepeh dee Nahpohlee is the arcade of iron, glass and pale stucco around you, across the road from the National Archaeological Museum, and Skottoh Yonnoh occupies rooms inside it, with a cocktail bar and caffay at ground level and more spaces above.

The man in the name, Vinchentsoh Skottoh Yonnoh, was an entrepreneur from the island of Prohcheedah who modelled his caffay shontonn on the caffay concerts of Paris, with singers performing between the tables. His first star, Ersilyah Sampyehree, was billed as the Sarah Bernhard of the caffay concert, and for a while this was one of the places where Neapolitan song set its trends.

A long silence followed, because after the First World War the rooms were turned into treasury offices for the Bankoh dee Nahpohlee, and they stayed that way until twenty twenty one, while the gallereeah itself, built between eighteen seventy three and eighteen eighty three, fell out of use for much of the twentieth century.

The revival came from the entrepreneur Lookah Yannootsee, who worked with the artist Ehoojaynyoh Teebahldee to bring the space back, and it reopened in twenty twenty three. Look closely at the walls, which were deliberately left weathered, with the traces of every earlier use still showing through. There's a library of around eighteen hundred books that anyone can sit and read, a stage for music and readings, and a dragonfly as the house emblem, chosen as a sign of rebirth.

Plenty of restorations scrub a building back to a single moment, and this one refuses to, letting the caffay, the bank and the years of neglect all stay in the room together.


When you step back into the arcade, look up at the glass roof, and then cross over to the Archaeological Museum, whose collections make even eighteen eighty three feel rather recent.
