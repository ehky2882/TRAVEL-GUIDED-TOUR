Ipogeo dei Cristallini

In 1889 a baron digging beneath his palazzo, looking for water and building stone, broke into a Greek cemetery instead.

The doorway of number 133 Via dei Cristallini, right in front of you, belongs to that same family palazzo, and the tombs lie underneath it, reached by a small staircase inside. Visits are guided and limited to groups of no more than eight, so the pavement out here is the right place to wait for yours.

Below ground there are four tombs side by side, cut into the soft yellow tuff that Naples is built on, and each has two levels: an upper room where the family carried out funeral rites, and a burial chamber beneath it. They date back about two thousand three hundred years, to a time when this hillside lay outside the walls of the Greek city of Neapolis and served as its necropolis.

The detail people remember is the furniture, because the dead were laid on beds carved out of the rock itself, and those beds were painted to look upholstered, with mattresses and double pillows in yellow, purple, blue and red. Elsewhere the walls carry painted garlands, vases and altars, and in one tomb a head of Medusa keeps watch, with figures of Dionysus and Ariadne close by.

A restoration begun in 2020 with Italy's Central Institute for Restoration steadied the colours, and the site reopened in July 2022, with small groups to protect the paintings.

The people who made these rooms weren't picturing their dead as gone; they were picturing them at rest, in good company, on a well made bed. All that painted cushioning and false architecture is a family insisting on comfort, long before anyone could have guessed we'd come down the stairs to look.

Afterwards, walk down to the Basilica of Santa Maria della Sanità, a few minutes away, where the catacombs of San Gaudioso carry this hillside's long story of burial on into early Christian times.
