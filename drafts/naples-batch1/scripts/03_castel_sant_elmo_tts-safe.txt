Kastel Sant Elmoh

From up here the whole of Naples is laid out like a map, and that was rather the point of putting a castle on this hilltop.

The ramp in front of you crosses the old moat to the castle's main gate, cut into walls of pale volcanic stone that rise sheer out of the ditch, with the imperial coat of arms carved above the entrance. Behind you lies the Vohmeroh, the neighbourhood that grew up around the fortress much later.

The shape is best appreciated from the air, because Kastel Sant Elmoh is a six pointed star, drawn up between fifteen thirty seven and fifteen forty seven by the military engineer Paydroh Looees Eskreevah for the Spanish viceroy, Paydroh deh Tohlaydoh. It replaced a smaller Anjevin fortress called Belforteh, and its name comes, by way of a few slurred centuries, from a tenth century church of Sant Erahzmoh that once stood on the hill.

Inside, the open parade ground known as the Pyatsah Darmee now houses the Moozayoh del Novehchentoh, opened in twenty ten, with more than a hundred and seventy works by Neapolitan artists of the twentieth century. It's a peaceful use for a fortress with a violent past, since in fifteen eighty seven lightning struck the gunpowder store and blew apart the church and the officers' quarters.

The interesting bit is who the castle was watching. For long stretches of its history its most important job was as a prison, holding the philosopher Tommahzoh Kampahnella in sixteen oh four and patriots of the Neapolitan Republic of seventeen ninety nine, and it stayed a military prison until nineteen fifty two. A star fort above a crowded city is a defence, certainly, but it's also a very good place from which to keep an eye on that city.


Climb up to the ramparts and walk the full circuit if you can, since the views run from the islands round to Vesuvius and out to the Flehgreean Fields. The Chertohzah dee San Marteenoh next door offers another view, with a monastery attached.
