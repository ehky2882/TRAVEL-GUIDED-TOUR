Neapolitan Sacred Art di Fabio Paolella

Most of the faces in this workshop have glass eyes, and every one of them has been painted by hand.

Up the hill from San Gregorio Armeno, the famous street of crib makers, the lanes around Via Anticaglia are a good deal quieter, and the small workshop in front of you is Neapolitan Sacred Art, the bottega of Fabio Paolella, where figures dressed in brocade and lace share the space with painted Madonnas in old fashioned frames.

Paolella makes terracotta pastori, the characters of the Neapolitan nativity scene, with glass eyes and fine painted detail, and his mother and sister make and fit their clothes. He also recreates devotional images in the styles of the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, such as the sorrowing Madonna Addolorata and the Immacolata, along with ex votos and small church ornaments.

How he learned matters just as much, since he started at fifteen as an apprentice in local workshops and never went to art school, so everything he knows came from watching older hands and copying the old methods until they became his own. A fully dressed figure of his costs several hundred euros, and he has pointed out, quite plainly, that the difference from a cheap one shows once you've taken it home.

Interestingly, he has also said that his most devoted customers tend to be collectors and scholars from abroad rather than Neapolitans themselves.

The busy street below keeps the crib making tradition visible to millions of visitors a year, and workshops like this one keep it slow. Both matter, though if you want to understand why Neapolitan nativity figures became collectors' pieces in the first place, you have to see one made the long way, one painted eye at a time.

When you leave, walk along Via Anticaglia and look up at the brick arches spanning the street, which are the remains of the Roman theatre where the emperor Nero is said to have performed.
