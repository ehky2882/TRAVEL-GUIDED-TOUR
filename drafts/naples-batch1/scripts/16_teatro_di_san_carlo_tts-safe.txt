San Karloh Theatre

This theatre had already been staging opera for more than forty years when La Skahlah opened in Milan.

Stand on the pavement of Veeah San Karloh, with the arcaded front of the Tayahtroh dee San Karloh on one side and the glass roofed Gallereeah Oombertoh the First behind you; to the left of the theatre as you face it, the Royal Palace carries on the same long frontage.

That connection is the key to the whole building. The San Karloh opened on the fourth of November seventeen thirty seven, commissioned by King Charles of Boorbon and designed by the royal architect Johvahnee Antohnyoh Medrahnoh, and it was built against the palace itself, so the opera was quite literally an extension of the court. It's generally described as the oldest opera house in the world still in continuous use, and its horseshoe shaped auditorium became a model for theatres across Europe.

The fire came next, when on the thirteenth of February eighteen sixteen flames gutted the theatre, and the architect Antohnyoh Neekohleenee rebuilt it in about ten months, reopening it in January eighteen seventeen, which is the kind of speed that tells you how much an opera house mattered to a capital city. The neoclassical front you're looking at is Neekohleenee's work.

As for the music, Jowkeenoh Rosseenee ran the house from eighteen fifteen to eighteen twenty two, Guyetahnoh Donnyzettee followed him, and in eighteen thirty five Donnyzettee's Loocheeah dee Lammermoor had its first performance here.

Standing outside, it's striking how ordinary the theatre's position looks today, wedged between a palace and a shopping arcade. That's precisely the point, though, because Naples built its opera house in the middle of everything, and the city has never quite let it become a museum.


If you have time, book one of the guided tours of the auditorium, or walk round the corner to Pyatsah del Plebisheetoh, where the Royal Palace shows off its full length.
