Riccardo Dalisi, Rua Catalana

For something like seven hundred years, this street has made its living out of metal.

Narrow and slightly crooked, Rua Catalana threads between the port and the old city, a couple of minutes' walk from the Maschio Angioino, and if you look up at the walls of the palazzi around you, you'll find figures in iron and tin fixed here and there to the stone.

In the fourteenth century the Angevin queen Joanna the First gave this stretch to Catalan merchants, who traded in metal goods such as pots, pans and tools, and the word rua is usually explained as a Neapolitan bending of the French rue, from the days when the city's rulers were French. Metalworkers have kept shops here ever since, though fewer than there once were.

The figures on the walls are the work of Riccardo Dalisi, the architect, designer and artist who was born in Potenza in 1931 and died in Naples in 2022. In 1979 the design firm Alessi asked him to rethink the traditional Neapolitan coffee pot, and he worked with the tinsmiths of streets like this one, making some two hundred tin models before the design was finished. That research won him the Compasso d'Oro, Italy's leading design prize, in 1981, and in 1997 he gave something back, fixing his sculptures to these walls in a project that brought architecture students to work alongside the craftsmen.

Most designers take a craft out of the workshop and into a factory, and Dalisi did very nearly the opposite, taking his ideas back to the workshop and leaving them on the street where they'd been made. Years later he even founded a prize of his own, the Compasso di Latta, or tin compass, to encourage young designers to work the same way.

From the end of the street, the Maschio Angioino is just ahead, and its white marble triumphal arch, squeezed between two dark stone towers, is well worth a closer look.
