Tempio di Segesta

This temple has stood here for nearly two and a half thousand years. It may never have been finished.

Come up the path from the ticket office and stop where the temple stands clear on its hill, with open country falling away around it. Thirty-six great Doric columns, six across each end and fourteen down each side, each about ten metres tall, holding up a roof line that's still complete.

It was built in the last decades of the fifth century BC, around four thirty to four twenty, for the city of Segesta. Its people were the Elymians, who weren't Greek at all, and nobody's quite sure where they came from, perhaps Italy, perhaps Anatolia. They were almost permanently at war with the Greek city of Selinunte. The architects and builders, though, were probably Greek.

Now look closely, because the clues are all here. The columns are smooth, with none of the vertical fluting a Greek temple normally has. Inside, there's no inner chamber, no cella, where the god's statue would stand. And down on the steps, look for the little knobs of stone still sticking out of the blocks. They were left on to protect the edges while the blocks were moved, and were meant to be chiselled off at the end. Nobody did.

[beat]

So why? The usual answer is war. The city was fighting for long stretches, and the money or the peace ran out. But some scholars think it was never meant to have a cella at all, and that the Elymians, worshipping in their own way, wanted an open sanctuary dressed up in Greek clothes. I rather like not knowing.

Goethe came here in the seventeen hundreds, on his Italian journey, and stood more or less where you are.

Before you leave, walk all the way round the outside, slowly, and run your eye along the steps. Every time you spot one of those knobs, you're looking at a job left unfinished two and a half thousand years ago.
