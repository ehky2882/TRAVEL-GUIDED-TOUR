Doh tonn boree Eemye Hon ten

This family has sold tea to theatregoers, trumpets to jazz musicians, and oodon to everybody, all on the same street.

Doh tonn boree, the south side of the canal. Look for a calm, low wooden shopfront in the middle of all the noise. Inside, it's quiet, polished and rather old fashioned, which on this street is the most surprising thing of all.

The story starts around eighteen fifteen, when the founder came to Doh tonn boree from Eemye choh in Nahrah. In eighteen thirty eight his daughter opened a teahouse for the theatre crowds, because this street was lined with theatres. Generations later, the family turned it into a shop selling Western musical instruments, Eemye Gahkkee, which became one of the best known in the country and grew up alongside Doh tonn boree's jazz scene.

Then the war. The air raids burned this district, and when the family came back in nineteen forty six they sold whatever they could: agar jelly, shaved ice, sweet bean soup. And, rather quietly, noodles. Customers loved the noodles. That was that.

Order the keetsoonay oodon. Keetsoonay means fox, and the dish is oodon topped with a sheet of sweet simmered fried tofu, which foxes, in Japanese folklore, are supposed to adore. Osaka claims the dish as its own invention. But the reason to come is the broth, the dahshee. It was perfected in nineteen forty nine by the fifth generation head and his wife, using kombo seaweed from Hokkye doh and dried mackerel and sardine flakes from Kyoo shoo. It's clear, pale and deep.

And here's the thing. On a street that reinvents itself every few years, this family has reinvented itself three times without ever leaving. Teahouse, music shop, noodle house. Same street, same name.

When you come out, walk along the canal and look for the building called Nahkah zah Kwee dah oh ray. Nahkah zah was the great theatre that stood there, the one that teahouse was built to serve. Its name survives on the building, even though the stage is long gone.
