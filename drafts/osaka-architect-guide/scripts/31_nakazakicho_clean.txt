Nakazakicho

Ten minutes from the skyscrapers of Umeda, and the city suddenly shrinks to the width of a bicycle.

Come up from Nakazakicho station onto Miyakojima-dori, with the towers of Umeda behind you to the west. Walk into the side streets to the south and north of the main road, and you'll find narrow alleys, potted plants on doorsteps, and rows of low wooden houses with tiled roofs.

These houses are nagaya, terraced row houses, built for working families in the early part of the last century. Some are more than a hundred years old. The reason they're still here is simple. In 1945, the air raids burned most of Osaka's old neighbourhoods. The bombs largely missed this one.

So for decades it was just a quiet backstreet district of old houses next to the city centre. Then, from around 2002, people began turning them into cafés. Now the alleys are full of tiny places: cafés, vintage clothes, handmade jewellery, little galleries and shops selling one sort of thing, often in rooms where a family once lived.

Have a look at the houses as you go. The wooden lattices on the windows, the narrow frontages, the little shrines tucked into corners. And notice how much of the neighbourhood is still simply lived in. Washing hangs out, bicycles lean on walls, and an old couple may well be sitting outside. Be quiet, and don't photograph people's homes.

And here's the thing. Osaka has spent a hundred years rebuilding itself, and most of it is new. Nakazakicho is what the whole city used to feel like, kept by accident. It's rather precious for that.

Get lost on purpose, there's no right route. And when you're done, keep walking east for about ten minutes and you'll hit Tenjinbashisuji, one of the longest shopping streets in Japan, covered all the way, running north and south for well over two kilometres.
