Osaka City Central Public Hall

A stockbroker paid for this building. He didn't live to see it open.

The building in front of you, on Nahkah noh sheemah between the rivers, is a red brick hall with bands of pale stone, big arched windows and a bronze dome. Look at the great arch in the middle of the front: it's the thing everyone photographs.

The man behind it was Ay noh skay Eewah mohtoh, a stockbroker from the share trading streets of Keetah hahmah, just across the river. He gave a million yen of his own money for a public hall for the people of Osaka, a vast sum at the time. There was a design competition, won by a young architect, Sheen eechee roh Ohkahdah, and his design was carried forward by Keengoh Tahtsoonoh and Yahsooshee Kahtah ohkah. Building began in nineteen thirteen.

Eewah mohtoh died in nineteen sixteen, two years before it was finished. The hall opened in nineteen eighteen.

Have a look along the roofline for two figures. They're Minerva, goddess of wisdom, and Mercury, god of merchants. For a building given by a stockbroker, in a city of traders, that's rather a fitting pair.

Inside, it's been used for operas, concerts and lectures since the beginning, and it still is. The great hall seats over eleven hundred people. Between nineteen ninety nine and two thousand and two the building was restored, strengthened against earthquakes and made accessible, and in December two thousand and two it was made a national Important Cultural Property.

Osaka has always been a city built by its merchants rather than by its rulers. Tokyo had the government; Osaka had the money, and the people who made it built the bridges, the schools and this hall. It's a monument to a man, but it's also a monument to that idea.

Entry to the building is free, and if you book a guided tour you'll be taken into the Special Room, which is otherwise closed to the public. Then walk west along the island to the domed library next door, and on to the museums at the far end.
