Nahkah zahkee choh

Ten minutes from the skyscrapers of Oomeh dah, and the city suddenly shrinks to the width of a bicycle.

Come up from Nahkah zahkee choh station onto Meeyah koh jeemah dohree, with the towers of Oomeh dah behind you to the west. Walk into the side streets to the south and north of the main road, and you'll find narrow alleys, potted plants on doorsteps, and rows of low wooden houses with tiled roofs.

These houses are nahgah yah, terraced row houses, built for working families in the early part of the last century. Some are more than a hundred years old. The reason they're still here is simple. In nineteen forty five, the air raids burned most of Osaka's old neighbourhoods. The bombs largely missed this one.

So for decades it was just a quiet backstreet district of old houses next to the city centre. Then, from around two thousand and two, people began turning them into cafés. Now the alleys are full of tiny places: cafés, vintage clothes, handmade jewellery, little galleries and shops selling one sort of thing, often in rooms where a family once lived.

Have a look at the houses as you go. The wooden lattices on the windows, the narrow frontages, the little shrines tucked into corners. And notice how much of the neighbourhood is still simply lived in. Washing hangs out, bicycles lean on walls, and an old couple may well be sitting outside. Be quiet, and don't photograph people's homes.

And here's the thing. Osaka has spent a hundred years rebuilding itself, and most of it is new. Nahkah zahkee choh is what the whole city used to feel like, kept by accident. It's rather precious for that.

Get lost on purpose, there's no right route. And when you're done, keep walking east for about ten minutes and you'll hit Ten jeen bahshee soojee, one of the longest shopping streets in Japan, covered all the way, running north and south for well over two kilometres.
