Bar Nah yootah

There's no menu. You tell the bartender what you like, and they make you something that didn't exist until a minute ago.

Near Triangle Park in Ahmayree kah moorah, find the foot of an ordinary building of bars and shops. Have a look at the signboards by the door for a small, intricate geometric symbol. That's your sign. The bar is on the fifth floor, and you go in through a door low enough to make most people duck.

Inside it's dim, wooden and cluttered. Bottles crowd the shelves and spill down onto the floor, and many of them are the bar's own: infusions, bitters and liqueurs made in house. It looks less like a cocktail bar than an alchemist's workshop, which I suspect is deliberate.

The name is worth a moment. Nah yootah is an old Buddhist word for an unimaginably large number, used in the traditional Japanese counting system for a figure with sixty or more noughts after it. A fair description of the possible drinks.

It opens at five in the evening and stays open until three in the morning, every day. In twenty twenty five it was one of two Osaka bars on the extended list of Asia's Fifty Best Bars.

What's worth noticing is how Osaka does this differently from Tokyo or Kyohtoh. In those cities, the great bars tend to be hushed, formal and precise. Here, the same skill comes with a bit of mischief: the low door, the hidden sign, the bottles on the floor. It's serious about the drink and not at all serious about itself.

When you come back down, Triangle Park is where Ahmayree kah moorah gathers at night, young people sitting on the steps with cans and skateboards. Walk east from it and you'll reach Sheen sye bahshee soojee, the long covered shopping street, in about five minutes.
