Namba Parks

The plan was a plain concrete passage between two blocks of shops. The architect proposed a canyon instead.

Find the lower walkway of Namba Parks, just south of Nankai Namba station, and look along a curving ravine of a street. The walls on either side are banded in coloured stone, like layers of rock, and they lean and curve as they rise. Overhead, glass bridges cross from side to side.

This was the site of Osaka Stadium, a baseball ground, which closed in the late nineteen nineties. The developer, Nankai Railway, brought in the Jerde Partnership from Los Angeles, the firm of Jon Jerde, who made his name designing shopping places that feel like towns.

The canyon is the first idea. The second is on top. Look up: the roof of the whole building steps up and up like a hillside, eight levels of gardens, with trees, rocks, lawns, streams, waterfalls and terraces, all the way up to the ninth floor. It's open to anyone, and on a summer's day, when the pavements of Namba bake, it's far cooler up there.

Jerde's office described it as bringing nature into urban density, and they say people use the park as their own back garden in the city. It drew twenty-one million visitors in its first year.

What's worth knowing is that this is a shopping centre, and nobody pretends otherwise. But it's a shopping centre that gave the city a hill. In a district with very few parks, that's no small thing.

Take the stairs and ramps up through the gardens, rather than the lifts, and go all the way to the top. Up there you can look back across Namba. And if you're heading on, Doguyasuji, the street of kitchen shops, is a few minutes' walk north-east.
