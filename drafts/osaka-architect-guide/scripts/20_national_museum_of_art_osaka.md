# 20 · The National Museum of Art, Osaka

**Category:** Visit · **Coordinates (WGS-84):** 34.691693, 135.492151 · **Length:** 318 words (about 127 s at 150 wpm)

## Script (clean)

Almost the whole of this museum is underground. What you see in front of you is little more than the door.

At the western end of Nakanoshima, look for a strange, tangled sculpture of curving steel tubes, sweeping up from a glass entrance. There's no gallery building to see, no wall, no roof. That's because the building is below your feet.

The architect was Cesar Pelli, the Argentine-born designer of skyscrapers all over the world, and here he did the opposite of a skyscraper. The steel above ground is meant to suggest bamboo, and the growth and vitality of contemporary art. Everything else, the galleries, the shop, the restaurant, goes down three floors.

The museum itself is older than this building. It opened in 1977 in the old art pavilion from Expo '70, out in the Expo park in Suita. That building was getting old, so in 2004 it moved here.

Go down and have a look. The entrance leads to an atrium that reaches all the way to the third basement, and daylight comes down into it. On the first basement level are three works that live here permanently: a big ceramic mural by Joan Miró called Innocent Laughter, from 1969, a sculpture by Alexander Calder called London, and a painting by Jiro Takamatsu called Shadow. In the galleries, the floors are end-grain Douglas fir, which softens the sound of footsteps.

On an island where every building wants to be seen, the national museum hid. It gave the open ground back to the city, and kept only the sculpture to tell you it's there. It's an odd idea for a museum, to be invisible, and I think it's rather a good one.

*[beat]*

When you come back up, look east. The black box of the Nakanoshima Museum of Art is just beside you, and close by it, graf studio, a design shop and kitchen run by an Osaka collective.
