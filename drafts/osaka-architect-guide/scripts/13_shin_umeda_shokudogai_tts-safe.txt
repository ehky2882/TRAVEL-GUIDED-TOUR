Sheen Oomeh dah Shoh koo doh gye

Overhead, every couple of minutes, a train. Underneath, about a hundred tiny bars and restaurants, and the smell of grilling.

You should be under the railway viaduct between Jay Arr Osaka station and Hahn kyoo Oomeh dah, at the entrance to a warren of narrow passages. Look in: low ceilings, steamed windows, signboards one after another, and people drinking highballs standing at counters barely wider than a shelf.

It opened in nineteen fifty, and the reason is rather touching. It was set up as a relief scheme for people retired from the national railway, a way of giving them a livelihood after the war, in the space under the tracks. Eighteen shops opened that first year. Now there are about a hundred.

The name means the new Oomeh dah food road. Some say it was called a road because the passages are so narrow, lined with so many places to eat, that they're less a building than a street. Have a look at the size of the shops as you walk in. Many of them seat eight or ten at most. The kitchens are about the size of a cupboard.

What's worth knowing, standing here, is that this is the real public space of Oomeh dah. The department stores and the glass towers all round you are new, polished and rebuilt every few years. This has barely changed, because it can't. It's wedged between railway arches. Office workers come here at six, a salaryman next to a student next to a pensioner, and for an hour or so they're all just people at a counter.

Most shops take cash, and many of them are standing only. If you'd rather walk than drink, head east under the tracks and keep going for about ten minutes. You'll reach Nahkah zahkee choh, a neighbourhood of old wooden row houses that came through the war, now full of tiny cafés.
