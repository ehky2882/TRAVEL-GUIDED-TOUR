The National Museum of Art, Osaka

Almost the whole of this museum is underground. What you see in front of you is little more than the door.

At the western end of Nahkah noh sheemah, look for a strange, tangled sculpture of curving steel tubes, sweeping up from a glass entrance. There's no gallery building to see, no wall, no roof. That's because the building is below your feet.

The architect was Sayzar Pelly, the Argentine born designer of skyscrapers all over the world, and here he did the opposite of a skyscraper. The steel above ground is meant to suggest bamboo, and the growth and vitality of contemporary art. Everything else, the galleries, the shop, the restaurant, goes down three floors.

The museum itself is older than this building. It opened in nineteen seventy seven in the old art pavilion from Expo seventy, out in the Expo park in Soo eetah. That building was getting old, so in two thousand and four it moved here.

Go down and have a look. The entrance leads to an atrium that reaches all the way to the third basement, and daylight comes down into it. On the first basement level are three works that live here permanently: a big ceramic mural by Zhoo ahn Meeroh called Innocent Laughter, from nineteen sixty nine, a sculpture by Alexander Calder called London, and a painting by Jeeroh Tahkah mahtsoo called Shadow. In the galleries, the floors are end grain Douglas fir, which softens the sound of footsteps.

On an island where every building wants to be seen, the national museum hid. It gave the open ground back to the city, and kept only the sculpture to tell you it's there. It's an odd idea for a museum, to be invisible, and I think it's rather a good one.

When you come back up, look east. The black box of the Nahkah noh sheemah Museum of Art is just beside you, and close by it, graff studio, a design shop and kitchen run by an Osaka collective.
