Sen neechee mah eh Doh goo yah soojee Shopping Street

Every plastic sushi in every restaurant window in Osaka came from somewhere. A good many of them came from here.

You should be under the arcade on Doh goo yah soojee, a covered street about a hundred and fifty metres long, a short walk south of Nahmbah's theatres. On both sides, shops crammed to the ceiling: knives, pots, piles of bowls, paper lanterns, shop curtains, signboards and plastic food so convincing it's faintly alarming.

It's a professional street. These are the shops where chefs and restaurant owners come to equip a kitchen, and the name says it: dohgoo means tools. The tools for running a restaurant.

It started in eighteen eighty two, as a pilgrims' road. People walked from the temples here at Sen neechee mah eh down to Shee ten noh jee and to Eemah meeyah Ebbeesoo shrine, and second hand dealers and sellers of household goods set up along the way to catch them. In the Tye shoh era, the early twentieth century, the shops turned into wholesalers and specialists. The arcade went up in nineteen seventy.

Have a look at the knife shops first. Osaka is the home of Sahkye knives, from the city just to the south, and a good shop here will sharpen and engrave what you buy. Then find a food sample shop. The plastic food on sale is the same as you see in restaurant windows.

What's worth knowing is that this is the backstage of Osaka's eating. Doh tonn boree is the stage, the lights and the queues. This is where the props are made, and the trays and the lanterns and the curtains with the shop's name dyed in. Walk through it and you understand how a city of tiny restaurants equips itself.

There are around fifty shops, and every October the street holds a festival with bargain stalls. If you've bought a knife, celebrate it with a meal: Kooroh mon Market is about ten minutes' walk east.
