This corner of Nahmbah lost one of the city's best loved buildings in two thousand and nine. What you're looking at is the way it was brought back.

Cross to the far side of Meedoh soojee, the big boulevard, and look back at the hotel. Down at the bottom, a long run of curving gables, one after another, like a row of rooftops on a temple. Above them, a tall tower wrapped in thin layers of aluminium fins.

Those gables are the memory. From nineteen fifty eight this was the Sheen Kahbookee zah, a theatre designed by Toh goh Moorahnoh, one of the great architects of post war Japan, and its front was a whole cascade of kahrah hahfoo, the swooping gable you see over temple gates and castle doorways. The theatre closed in two thousand and nine and the building came down.

When the new hotel went up, the architect was Kengoh Koomah, who admired Moorahnoh enormously. So he rebuilt that gabled front across the lower floors, and put his own tower of fins on top. The hotel opened on the first of December twenty nineteen, and its logo is literally the two ideas together, Moorahnoh's gables under Koomah's fins.

Inside, it calls itself a museum hotel. Around a hundred and eighty works by twenty nine artists, chosen by the curator Joonjee Eetoh, and at the heart of it the Gootye group, the radical Kahn sye artists of the fifties and sixties: Jeeroh Yoshee hahrah, Kahzoo oh Sheerahgah, Ahtsookoh Tahnahkah. There's Yah yoy Koosahmah too, if you're keeping count.

What's worth thinking about, standing here, is that this is a copy. Moorahnoh's building is gone. Some people find that a bit sad, an old building dressed up as itself. I rather think it's honest. The street got its gables back, and nobody's pretending the tower behind them is from nineteen fifty eight.

On the ground floor, facing the boulevard, there's a café called Tsoochee, which Koomah designed as well, and it's open to anyone. For the view, go up to the bar on the twentieth floor, Koomoh koomoh, and look east across the night skyline.
