Splash a little water on the statue. Everyone does, which is why you can barely see his face any more.

A narrow stone paved lane just south of Doh tonn boree, a couple of minutes from the neon. At its heart is a small temple yard, and in it a stone statue of the god Foodoh, completely covered in green moss, with a basin of water and ladles in front.

This is the Meezoo kahkay Foodoh, the water splashing Foodoh. People come to pray, scoop up a ladle of water and throw it over him. They've done it for so long, and so often, that he's grown a coat of thick moss, and his features have all but disappeared. In nineteen forty five the air raids burned the temple's main hall to the ground. The statue survived.

Now look along the lanes. There are two of them, stone paved, about eighty metres long and about three metres wide. Since the Mayjee era people have been coming to the temple, and the food stalls that served them grew into this alley of small restaurants and bars.

Have a look at the signboards at the ends. The one at the west entrance was written by the comic actor Kahn bee Foojee yahmah. The one at the east is by the storyteller Kahtsoorah Hahroo dahnjee the third. Both are names Osaka loves.

In two thousand and two and again in two thousand and three, fires tore through the lane. Under the building rules, a lane this narrow can't normally be rebuilt as it was. Tens of thousands of people signed in support, and it came back the same narrow width. That's the whole point of the place. Make it wider and you'd lose it.

Come back after dark, especially if it's been raining. The lanterns come on, the stones shine, and it's as atmospheric as anywhere in the city. Then step back out to Doh tonn boree and its lights. The contrast is half the pleasure.
