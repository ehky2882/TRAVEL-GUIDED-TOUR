# BOSTON · 15 — Old South Meeting House
T2 · single stop · B1b · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---

In the winter of 1773, Boston's arguments outgrew the hall built to hold them, and moved into this church.

You should be at the corner of Washington and Milk Streets, facing a brick meeting house with a tall white steeple and a clock partway up the tower. This is Old South, built in 1729 by a Puritan congregation. Puritans built rooms for preaching and talking, plain and full of seats, which made this the obvious place for a crowd.

If you let your eye run down the steeple to the doors, you're at the part of the story everyone knows. On the sixteenth of December, thousands of people packed in here, waiting to hear whether the governor would let three tea ships leave the harbor with their cargo aboard. Toward evening the answer came back: no. And Samuel Adams, by the accounts, said he could think of nothing more to be done.

The legend says those words were a prearranged signal, and the room emptied for the wharf.

The record tells it differently. The accounts written at the time put ten or fifteen minutes between Adams's remark and the shouting in the street. When it came, Adams and others called for people to stay in their seats, because the meeting wasn't finished, and the room sat through another speech before it closed. The signal story took shape almost a century later, when a historian squeezed the timeline together and Adams's first biographer turned it into a plan.

*[beat]*

The legend hands the night a conductor. The record hands it a crowd: thousands of people arguing for hours in a plain room, and the thing they're remembered for happening somewhere between the talking and the harbor.

One member of this congregation belongs in the picture. Phillis Wheatley, brought to Boston as an enslaved child, worshipped here, and her book of poems was published in London in 1773, the same year as the tea. Not everyone in these seats had the same stake in the word liberty.

During the occupation, British troops tore out the pews and exercised their horses on the floor. The room got its seats back, and it has been holding arguments ever since.
