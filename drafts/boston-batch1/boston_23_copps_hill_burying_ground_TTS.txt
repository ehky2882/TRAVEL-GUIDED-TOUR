# BOSTON · 23 — Copp's Hill Burying Ground
T2 · single stop · B3 · TTS-safe · 28 Sept 2026
---
The stone wall along the far edge of this burying ground is the side of a hill, sliced off and faced in granite.

You should be inside kops Hill Burying Ground, on the path near its northwest edge, where the ground stops at the fence above Snowhill Street. Slate headstones lean at every angle behind you. Ahead, past the rooftops, the water opens toward Charlestown.

Directly below you is the drop the city made. This was the North End's high ground, first known for the windmill the settlers put on top, and in sixteen fifty nine the town began burying its dead here. For a century and a half, that was the whole story. Then Boston wanted more land along its edges, and hills were the handiest source of fill. In the first years of the eighteen hundreds the top of kops Hill was dug down and hauled away, and the gravel went first to the shore where Commercial Street runs now, pushing the waterline out into the harbor.

The burying ground was in the way, and the city chose to cut around the dead. The streets were lowered on every side while the graves kept their ground. In eighteen thirty eight the Snowhill Street edge was dug back once more, and the graves along it were lifted and moved in behind a new retaining wall, the granite just under your feet. The drop from here to the sidewalk is about twenty feet. That's the height of hill the city left alone because people were buried in it.

Everything around you was lowered except the dead. The burying ground is a block of the old hill, trimmed square and propped up in stone, with the streets running past at the level the living settled for.

It holds the neighborhood that lived on and around it. Among the stones is the grave of Prince Hall, a free Black Bostonian who served in the Revolution and was a founder of Black Freemasonry in America. Along the Charter Street side lie many of the free Black families who lived at the foot of this hill, most of them without markers. Their graves stayed put for the same reason as everyone else's.

Boston spent the nineteenth century taking its hills apart and pouring them into the water. Here it stopped partway, and the reason it stopped is under the grass around you.
