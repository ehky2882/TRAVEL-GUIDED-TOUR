# BOSTON · 17 — Boston City Hall and Plaza
T2 · single stop · B1b · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---

No street sign around you says Scollay Square, but for about a hundred years this was a loud, crowded crossroads, and you're standing in the middle of where it was.

You should be on the Congress Street side of City Hall Plaza, with the building itself in front of you: a great block of concrete set on a brick base, its top floors stepping outward and hanging over the ones below. The brick plaza spreads around it. If construction fencing blocks the way in, the sidewalk gives you everything you need.

The old square sat where Court, Tremont and Cambridge Streets came together. From the middle of the nineteenth century it was the city's entertainment district: burlesque houses, bars, arcades, tattoo parlors, and sailors on leave. By the 1950s it had run down, and the city chose to clear it rather than repair it. In the early 1960s nearly all of it came down, little streets and all, to make room for a new Government Center.

The building in front of you was the centerpiece. It came out of an open design competition in 1962, won by two young architecture teachers against more than two hundred entries, and it opened in 1968. On the facade, a few big concrete volumes push out from the rest. They mark the council chamber and the mayor's office, so the building shows you where the government sits.

People have argued about it ever since. Some see a bold, open civic building; others see a fortress. Both readings have had half a century to harden, and neither side has run out of arguments.

*[beat]*

What's worth holding on to is the trade the city made here. Boston decided the way to start over was to clear the ground completely, and then it set its own government down in the clearing. That idea shaped a lot of the city around you, and Boston would spend decades working out what it cost.

Before you go, find one survivor. Across the plaza, on the curved brick row along Court Street, a giant copper kettle hangs over a doorway, steaming. It was a tea company's sign in Scollay Square from 1873, and when its building came down in 1967 it moved here. In 1875 the city's official measurer filled it with water to settle what it held: two hundred twenty-seven gallons, two quarts, one pint and three gills.
