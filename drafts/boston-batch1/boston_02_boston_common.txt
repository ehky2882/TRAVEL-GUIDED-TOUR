# BOSTON · 02 — Boston Common
B1a · T1 · plants S7 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---
A man lived here by himself, on the far slope toward the river, with an orchard and, by most accounts, a shelf of books.

You should be on the small rise in the middle of the Common, beside the tall column with a figure standing at its top: the Soldiers and Sailors Monument, on what's called Flagstaff Hill. Find the gold dome of the State House up the slope; that way is north. Tremont Street runs along the edge behind your right shoulder, and the open lawns fall away in front of you toward Charles Street.

The land, before the man. This whole peninsula had a name long before it had an English one. The Massachusett, the people of this coast, called it Shawmut, and people camped, fished and gathered here for thousands of years; digs on this ground have turned up traces of them far older than any town. The City of Boston now formally acknowledges the Massachusett Tribe at Ponkapoag as the people of this land.

Then the man. His name was William Blaxton, an English clergyman who had settled on the peninsula by himself, somewhere off toward the corner where Beacon and Charles Streets meet today. When the Puritans landed across the river, he invited them over, and before long he found he had more neighbors than he'd bargained for. In 1634 Blaxton sold his claim to the town and moved on, and the town set the land aside for everyone.

The grass is the last layer. The Common is often called the oldest public park in the country, and for most of its life it wasn't a park at all. It was pasture. Families grazed cows here for about two centuries; by 1646 there were already rules about how many, because the grass was running out. The cows were finally banned in 1830, and only then did the pasture start becoming what you see.

*[beat]*

Locals will tell you it's the Common, singular, never the Commons, and the old word explains itself: one piece of land, held in common. Keep the layers in mind as you stand here. First Shawmut, for thousands of years; then one Englishman alone; then a town's cows; and only then, a park. Boston is the latest name this ground has answered to, and the ground has had a lot of them.
