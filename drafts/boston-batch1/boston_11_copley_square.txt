# BOSTON · 11 — Copley Square
T1 · single stop · B5 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---

This square made one thing, and it wasn't money. It made readers.

You should be near the fountain in the middle of the square, the stone church on your left, the long granite front of the library on your right. The church is Trinity, finished in 1877 to a design by Henry Hobson Richardson: rough brown stone, a fortress of a tower, a clay tile roof. Behind it a slab of mirrored glass goes up sixty stories, and from the right angle the church appears in the glass, turned slightly, as if the tower were considering it.

*[beat]*

The library came second, built between 1888 and 1895 by Charles McKim, and it explains the first sentence. Boston's was among the first big city libraries in the country paid for by taxes and open to anyone, and McKim gave it the form of an Italian palace: a long block of granite with a row of arched windows along the second floor, on a platform of broad steps. Cut into the keystone of the front arch are three words. Free to all.

Across the river, the college had spent two centuries turning out trained minds and calling that its product. This was the machine's other half. It shelved the same books for people who would never see Harvard Yard: the clerk, the seamstress, the boy from the North End. The readers came out of here. So did the writers, eventually, and the engineers, and much of what the city sold for a century.

*[beat]*

The tower is the John Hancock Tower, from 1976, by Henry Cobb, now called by its address, two hundred Clarendon. Its windows fell out. In a winter gale in 1973, while it was still going up, the big panels began to shatter and drop, and for most of two years patches of the tower wore black plywood. No one was hurt. In the end all ten thousand three hundred and forty-four panes were replaced. The church had already sued over the excavation, and won.

Trinity has its own trouble with the ground. It sits on thousands of wooden piles driven through wet earth, and they must be kept below the water table or they rot.

The square was rebuilt and reopened in 2026; the old lawn on the library side was mostly paved, which some people liked and many didn't. It is named for a painter. It runs on readers.
