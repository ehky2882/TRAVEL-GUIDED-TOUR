# BOSTON · 19 — Robert Gould Shaw and 54th Regiment Memorial
B1a · T2 · pays S2 · TTS-safe · 28 Sept 2026
---
The family said no. They didn't want their son alone on a horse.

You should be on the edge of the Common where Beacon Street meets Park Street, facing a large bronze relief set into a stone frame, with the State House behind you across Beacon Street. In the bronze, a young officer rides a horse, and around him and past him, on foot, march rows of soldiers with rifles on their shoulders. Above them floats a winged figure.

The officer is Robert Gould Shaw, colonel of the Fifty fourth Massachusetts, one of the earliest Black regiments raised in the North during the Civil War. In May eighteen sixty three the regiment marched through the city on its way south, right past where you're standing. Shaw died leading them that summer, in South Carolina. When Boston decided to build him a memorial, the commission went to Augustus saint gaw denz, and the plan was the usual one for an officer: Shaw on horseback, by himself. His family refused. They wanted him shown with the men he led.

Look at the faces. It took saint gaw denz about fourteen years to finish this, and he worked from more than forty Black men who came to his studio as models, so the soldiers aren't a repeated type. Each one is somebody. Some look ahead, some look tired, one looks like he has something on his mind. The memorial was dedicated on Memorial Day, eighteen ninety seven.

Now walk around to the back, if you have the time. There are names there. When the memorial was unveiled, the names carved on it were the white officers'. The names of the Black soldiers who died with them were added in nineteen eighty two, eighty five years later.

This is a monument that got finished twice. Once in eighteen ninety seven, when the family's refusal put the men into the bronze; and again in nineteen eighty two, when the city put their names on the stone. The second revolution Boston fought, against slavery, had soldiers, and here they are, marching past the State House where the laws were made.

Stand in front of it a little longer than feels necessary. The bronze was cleaned and rehung in twenty twenty one, and it looks the way it was meant to: a column of men who chose to go, walking down Beacon Street, with a young colonel riding beside them rather than ahead of them.
