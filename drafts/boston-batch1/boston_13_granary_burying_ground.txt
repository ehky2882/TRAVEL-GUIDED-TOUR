# BOSTON · 13 — Granary Burying Ground
B1a · T2 · pays S3 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---
Those neat rows of gravestones in front of you don't mark the graves.

You should be on Tremont Street, at the heavy stone gateway of the Granary Burying Ground, looking in. The white steeple of Park Street Church is to your left. Past the gate, slate headstones stand in long, orderly lines across the grass, with a tall stone obelisk rising near the middle.

The rows, since they're the story. There are about two thousand three hundred markers here, and historians estimate more than five thousand people are buried in this ground. The numbers don't match, and neither do the positions. In the nineteenth century, keepers pulled many of the stones up and reset them in straight lines, partly to suit the taste of the day for tidy cemeteries and partly to make room for the mower. The result looks like a map, and it isn't one. Many of the stones now stand a fair distance from the people they name.

This burying ground, then, is a display more than a record, and it helps to walk it that way. The obelisk in the middle is for Benjamin Franklin's parents, raised long after they died, by admirers of their famous son. Near the front, not far from the fence, a single stone marks the grave shared by the dead of the Boston Massacre. Crispus Attucks is among them, a sailor of African and Native descent, by most accounts, killed on King Street in 1770. The stone is worth finding.

And the legend. Somewhere in here, you may hear, lies Mother Goose, the Boston grandmother who supposedly made up the nursery rhymes. There's a stone that reads Mary Goose. Mary was the first wife of a man named Isaac Goose; the story attaches to his second wife, Elizabeth, whose grave, if she's here, has no stone. And the rhymes themselves were being sung in England long before there was a Boston to sing them in.

*[beat]*

None of this makes the place less worth your time. A burying ground is a town's memory of itself, and this one has been edited, like most memories are: stones moved for the lawn, an obelisk for a famous son, a nursery rhyme looking for a grave. The people are here all the same, underneath the rows, keeping their own order.
