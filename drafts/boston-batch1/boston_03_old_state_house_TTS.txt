# BOSTON · 03 — Old State House and the Massacre Site
T1 · single stop · B1b · TTS-safe · 28 Sept 2026
---

State Street runs downhill to the harbor, and it has carried many processions past the building in front of you. It keeps two closer than the rest.

You should be on State Street just below Devonshire, looking uphill at the east end of a small brick building with a tiered white tower. This is the Old State House, built in seventeen thirteen for the colony's government, and it sits on top of a subway station. A balcony hangs from the middle of this face. In the paving just downhill from you, near the traffic island, a ring of cobblestones is set into the street. If construction fencing is up on the far side, stay out here; everything you need is on this face and underfoot.

If you look to the two corners of the roofline, you'll find a lion and a unicorn, the crown's own animals, perched on a building that turned against the crown. These are nineteenth century replacements; the originals were burned in seventeen seventy six, soon after the Declaration of Independence was read aloud from that balcony, on the eighteenth of July.

The ring is older news. In seventeen seventy, British regulars fired into a crowd on this spot, and among the dead was Crispus at tux, of African and Native descent, by most accounts. The ring marks the place and doesn't try to show you more.

Then comes eighteen fifty four. Anthony Burns had escaped slavery in Virginia and was working in Boston when he was arrested under the Fugitive Slave Act and ordered returned. On the second of June he was taken under armed guard down State Street, past this building, to a waiting ship. Windows along the route were hung with black, and opposite the balcony someone hung a coffin marked for the funeral of liberty.

Put the two days side by side. The street that heard independence read out was cleared, seventy eight years later, so a man could be sent back into slavery down the middle of it. The question of who that reading covered had been open since the day it was read, and in eighteen fifty four it came down the hill in public.

It didn't end on the dock. Within a year, Boston money bought Burns his freedom, and he came back north. The lion and the unicorn are still up there, looking down a street that took a long time to make up its mind.
