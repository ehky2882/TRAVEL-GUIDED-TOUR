# BOSTON · 14 — Park Street Church
B1a · T2 · plants S2 · TTS-safe · 28 Sept 2026
---
The Fourth of July, eighteen twenty nine. The Fourth of July, eighteen thirty one. Same church, two years apart.

You should be at the corner where Park Street meets Tremont Street, looking up at Park Street Church: red brick below, a tall white steeple rising in tiers above, and the Common across the street at your back. The church was finished in eighteen oh nine, designed by an English born architect named Peter Banner, and it's still a working congregation.

The first date. On the Fourth of July in eighteen twenty nine, a twenty three year old newspaperman named William Lloyd Garrison stood up in this church and gave his first major public speech against slavery. He was on a holiday that celebrated liberty, in a city proud of its revolution, arguing that the country's liberty had left enslaved people out. He'd go on to found an antislavery paper, the Liberator, and to spend the next three decades making Boston uncomfortable.

The second date. On the Fourth of July in eighteen thirty one, the children of the church's Sunday school sang a new hymn on these steps, called America. A young seminary student named Samuel Francis Smith had written the words. The tune, it's worth knowing, was borrowed. It's the melody of God Save the King.

Two answers to the same holiday, then, on one corner. One is a hymn that says the country's liberty is already won; the other is a young man saying it has barely begun. Boston would end up fighting a second revolution, this one against slavery, and it started in rooms like this one, on the same calendar as the first.

One more thing about the corner itself. People have called it Brimstone Corner for a long time, and there are two explanations. One says gunpowder was stored in the church's basement during the War of eighteen twelve. The other says the name came from the preaching. Both stories have their defenders, and the church has never needed to choose. Before you move on, let the steeple do what it was built to do, which is catch your eye from across the Common, on any day of the year, including the Fourth.
