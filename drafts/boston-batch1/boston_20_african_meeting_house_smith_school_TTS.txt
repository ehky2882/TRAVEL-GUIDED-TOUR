# BOSTON · 20 — African Meeting House and Abiel Smith School
T2 · single stop · B2 · TTS-safe · 28 Sept 2026
---

Two brick buildings share this little court, and they have opposite endings. One is known for everything that happened inside it. The other is known for being shut, by the people it was built for.

You should be in Smith Court, the short lane running off Joy Street on the north slope of Beacon Hill. The plain brick building set back along the lane, with its tall arched front, is the African Meeting House. The brick building on the corner of Joy Street is the ay bee el Smith School. Both belong to the Museum of African American History.

The meeting house went up in eighteen oh six, raised almost entirely by Black workmen, for a Black congregation that wanted a church of its own. It's among the oldest Black church buildings in the country. But it was never only a church. Through the nineteenth century this was where Boston's Black community gathered to organize, to argue and to hear speakers.

It was also a schoolhouse. At first, Black families wanted a school of their own, because their children were unwelcome in the town's schools, and from eighteen oh eight classes met right here. In eighteen thirty five the school moved next door, into the building on the corner, named for a white merchant who had left the town money to educate Black children.

And then the same families turned against it. A separate school had once looked like protection, but by the eighteen forties it looked like a wall, because a school set aside by race kept Black children out of every other school in the city. William Cooper Nell, who had grown up in this neighborhood, led a campaign for equal schools that ran for years. In eighteen forty nine many families pulled their children out of the Smith School altogether. When a lawsuit failed in the courts, they went to the legislature.

In eighteen fifty five Massachusetts ended segregated public schools, and the Smith School closed. An empty classroom was exactly what the people who fought over it had asked for.

The meeting house kept on meeting, and the school became a museum. Between them, this small court holds one community's whole arc: it built what it had been denied, then fought to be done with needing it.
