# BOSTON · 22 — Hanover Street
T2 · single stop · B3 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---
Three languages have been the everyday sound of this corner, one after another, and the corner never had to change to switch.

You should be at Hanover and Prince Streets, beside the brick front of St. Leonard's Church, looking down Hanover toward the open ground where the street runs out. Hanover is the North End's main street, narrow, busy, and lined on both sides with brick that mostly went up in the eighteen hundreds. Behind you it climbs toward the older end of the neighborhood.

The church at your shoulder is the easiest place to start. Its parish was founded in 1873, and it describes itself as the first Catholic church in New England built by Italian immigrants. The building came within twenty years of that. Beside it is a small walled garden, open to the street.

But the Italian North End is the third one. In the seventeen hundreds this was a merchants' quarter, and when the merchants moved to newer streets the brick they left behind filled with Irish families, arriving through the eighteen forties in enough numbers that by the middle of the century they were close to half the neighborhood. Irish voices on this corner, then, for a generation. From the eighteen seventies the Irish began to move out and two new arrivals moved in almost together: Jewish families from Eastern Europe, concentrated a street over on Salem, and Italians, concentrated here. For a few decades Yiddish and Italian shared the same blocks. Then the Jewish families left for Roxbury and Dorchester, and by the nineteen twenties Hanover Street spoke Italian nearly end to end.

*[beat]*

Beacon Hill runs its two versions of Boston side by side, a couple of blocks from each other. The North End ran three of them end to end, through the same stairwells, in the same rooms, without knocking anything down. That's the version of the pattern this street holds. The buildings weren't replaced when the people were. A tenement that held an Irish family in the eighteen fifties held a Jewish one in the eighteen nineties and an Italian one by the First World War, and the brick took no notice.

Look at the upper floors on either side of you, the ones nobody redecorates for visitors. That's the part of the street that has heard all three. The brick stayed. The languages moved through it.
