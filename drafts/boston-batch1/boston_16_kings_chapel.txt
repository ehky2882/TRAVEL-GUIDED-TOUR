# BOSTON · 16 — King's Chapel and its burying ground
B1a · T2 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---
This church was built around another church, and when it was done, the old one was taken apart and carried out from inside.

You should be at the corner of Tremont and School Streets, in front of a dark granite church with a row of columns across its front and a square, stubby tower with nothing on top. Beside it, behind an iron fence along Tremont Street, is a small, crowded burying ground.

The ground, because it came first. This is the town's original burying ground, older than the church by more than half a century. John Winthrop, the Puritan colony's founding governor, lies in it. The Puritans who ran Boston had come here partly to get away from the Church of England, so when an Anglican congregation was founded in town in 1686, nobody would sell it land for a building. The royal governor, Edmund Andros, took a corner of the public graveyard instead. The king's church went up among the Puritans' dead.

The first chapel was wooden. By the 1740s the congregation had outgrown it, and it hired Peter Harrison to design something grander in granite. The builders raised the new stone walls around the old wooden church, which went on standing inside them. When the granite was finished, the wooden chapel was dismantled and carried out through the new building, and its timber was shipped to Lunenburg, in Nova Scotia, where it became another church.

Look up at the tower. The design called for a tall steeple on that tower. The money ran out, and it was never built, which is why the tower looks as though it's waiting for something. And the columns along the front, which read as solid stone from where you're standing, are wood, finished with sand in the paint to pass for granite. They were added after the Revolution.

*[beat]*

After the Revolution the congregation changed too: it became Unitarian, while keeping much of its old prayer book. The whole building runs on reversals: a king's church in a town that wouldn't sell it a lot, stone built around wood, wood dressed as stone. It's still a working church. Stay a moment by the fence, where Winthrop's town and the king's chapel have been neighbors for more than three centuries, and seem to have made their peace.
