# BOSTON · 30 — Harvard Yard
T2 · single stop · B5 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---

The gate in front of you decides whether you get in. Most days it says yes. Some days, when the university has a reason, it says no, and the reason is rarely posted.

You should be on the sidewalk of Massachusetts Avenue in Cambridge, facing the biggest of the iron gates in the long brick wall. This is Johnston Gate, the main way into Harvard Yard, put up in 1889 and 1890 to a design by Charles McKim, who had studied here without finishing. Through the bars you can see the two oldest buildings on the campus facing each other across the path: Massachusetts Hall on the left, from 1720, and Harvard Hall on the right, from 1766.

For its first years the Yard was a cow pasture. That is the ordinary part. The strange part is the date. The colony's General Court voted the college into existence in 1636. Boston, across the river, was six years old and still mostly a village. It voted itself a college anyway.

*[beat]*

Two years later a young minister in Charlestown named John Harvard died and left the new school half his estate and his library, about four hundred books. The college took his name in 1639. Its first real asset was a shelf. Not a building, not land. Books, and the promise that people would come to use them.

That is a habit this region never lost. Other colonies grew tobacco or sugar. Massachusetts, from its first decade, grew graduates, and sent them out the way a mill sends out cloth: ministers, then lawyers, then engineers and everyone else. The trained mind became the local product, and the city downriver built an economy on selling it. You will see the other end of that machine in Copley Square, where Boston built a palace so that people who never passed through this gate could read the same books.

*[beat]*

Inside the Yard, if you get in, there is a bronze of a seated man in front of University Hall, the gray building at the far end. His left shoe is polished bright by visitors' hands. He has a story, and this isn't the place for it.

The gate is the place. It was built to look as if it had always been here, in the style of the buildings it guards, and it is a century and a half younger than either of them.
