# BOSTON · 07 — Old North Church
T1 · single stop · B3 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---
The two lanterns that made this church famous weren't hung for Paul Revere. And the steeple you're looking at isn't the one they hung in.

You should be at the Unity Street end of the Paul Revere Mall, the brick promenade under the lindens, facing the back of Old North Church. Its white steeple rises at the far end, over the roof. This is Christ Church, built in 1723 and still a working Episcopal parish.

That steeple is the third to stand there. The first went up in 1740 and was blown down in a storm in 1804. Its replacement fell to a hurricane in 1954, and what you see went up the following year, copied from the original. One thing came through both falls: the weathervane at the very top, set back up each time. It's the only piece up there that was up there in 1775.

The signal was Revere's idea. That April, Revere's circle knew the troops would soon march for Concord, one of two ways: over the narrow neck of land, or across the water. He arranged lanterns in this tower so the Sons of Liberty in Charlestown would know which, even if he never got out of town. One lantern for the road, two for the water. On the night of the eighteenth, most likely the sexton, Robert Newman, climbed up and showed two. Revere was still in Boston when they shone. Then he had himself rowed across the river, past a British warship, and rode.

*[beat]*

The version most Americans carry puts Revere on the far shore, saddled up and waiting, watching this tower. That comes from Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, who climbed the tower in April 1860 and began a poem the next day. It's a fine poem. It also turns the signal around. The lights Revere sent become lights sent to him, and a plan built by a network becomes one man's cue.

You'll meet that pattern all over Boston. Much of what the city remembers about 1775 was written down long afterward, by poets and grandchildren, and the record underneath is usually smaller and more tangled than the legend. The lanterns weren't a message for Revere. They were his message, in case he couldn't carry it himself.

When you look up, picture the signal pointed the other way: two small lights facing across the water, set by a man unsure he'd make it out.
