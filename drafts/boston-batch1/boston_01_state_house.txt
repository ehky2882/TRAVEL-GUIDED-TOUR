# BOSTON · 01 — Massachusetts State House
B1a · T1 · plants S1 · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---
The hill behind that gold dome is missing its top.

You should be on the Common, on the path that runs along its upper edge beside Beacon Street, facing north across the traffic. In front of you is the Massachusetts State House: red brick, a long porch of white columns, and above it all the dome, gilded, with a small gilt pine cone perched on the very top.

The dome, first. Charles Bulfinch designed the building, and it was finished in 1798 on land that had been John Hancock's cow pasture. The dome wasn't always gold. It started out as wooden shingles; in 1802 Paul Revere's company sheathed it in copper; later it was painted over, and it wasn't gilded until 1874. It also has a quieter job than looking expensive. When distances across the Commonwealth are officially measured from Boston, they're measured to this dome.

Now the hill. The street is Beacon Street and the neighborhood is Beacon Hill, both named for a warning signal the early town kept on the summit: a tall mast with a pot of tar at the top, ready to be lit if trouble came in from the sea. The summit stood just behind the State House, and you can't see it, because it isn't there. In the early eighteen hundreds, builders cut the top of the hill down by about sixty feet and carted the earth north, downhill, to fill a tidal pond on the far side of the neighborhood. By 1828 the Mill Pond was solid ground, and there were streets on it.

So the hill kept its name and lost its top. Boston did this more than once. The town was a knobby peninsula surrounded by marsh and mud, short on flat land and long on hills, and it solved the problem the direct way: it took its highest ground and put it into its lowest.

*[beat]*

Look again at the dome with that in mind. The gold is what everyone photographs, and it deserves the attention, but the building is also sitting on the edge of a hole the city dug in itself, a hill carried off by the cartload to make room for a town that wanted to grow. The beacon is long gone. The name stayed, and it points at something you're no longer able to see, which is a very Boston way to keep a memory.
