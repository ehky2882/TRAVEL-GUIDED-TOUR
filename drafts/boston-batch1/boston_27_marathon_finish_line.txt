# BOSTON · 27 — The Boston Marathon finish line
T2 · single stop · B5b · clean · 28 Sept 2026
---

The race that ends on this street starts in another town.

You should be on the sidewalk of Boylston Street, on the library side, at the wide painted stripe that crosses the road. The word on it is the one you'd expect. It is there all year, repainted each spring, and most days people who have never run a step come to put a foot on it and take the picture. The library is behind you, the tower's glass to your left, and Boylston runs off to your right, west, which is the direction the runners come from.

*[beat]*

The course begins twenty-six miles and change to the west, in Hopkinton, a small town with a green, and it has run on the state holiday called Patriots' Day, now the third Monday in April, since 1897. That is why it is often called the oldest marathon run every year anywhere. It comes in through the suburbs, over a rise in Newton with its own name, past a women's college whose students scream at the runners on principle, then turns right, then left onto this street, so that the last stretch is dead straight, with the line in sight the whole way.

For its first seven decades the race was for men. In 1966 a woman named Bobbi Gibb, who had been told in writing that women couldn't run the distance, hid in the bushes near the start, joined the field as it passed, and finished ahead of most of it. She wasn't official. Nobody could say she hadn't done it either. Women were admitted properly in 1972, and a wheelchair division followed in 1975. The town at the other end of the course put up a bronze of Gibb in 2026, a few steps from the start, mid-stride, running toward this street.

*[beat]*

Along this sidewalk, a little back from the line, rough granite pillars are set into the pavement at two points, with slender bronze lights beside them and cherry trees that flower in April. They mark where bombs went off during the race in 2013, and they are a memorial to the people killed there, kept deliberately small, at the height of a person.

The line is the point. Every other landmark in this city marks something that happened. This one marks something that is about to, once a year, for one morning, and then the paint just waits.
